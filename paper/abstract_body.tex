A Mixture-of-Experts model too large for one consumer GPU keeps most of its experts in host memory, and decoding one
token at a time then waits on host reads. We ask how fast such decoding could be on a given machine, where the
remaining time goes, and what knowing the future routing is worth. Our bound is the host reads of Belady's MIN with
bypass, the fewest that any cache of a given size per layer can make, at the machine's probed read rate. Our llama.cpp
expert cache, faster than two other systems at most configurations, reaches \dcShareMinLowMin--\dcShareMinLowMax\% of
the bound's speed on machines with consumer processors at the smallest budget we test. We call its time beyond the
bound the gap. Oracles built into the engine close about half of the gap; we attribute the rest without measuring it
part by part. At that budget, on machines whose link reads at least half as fast as the CPU, the experts MIN's greedy
schedule keeps pay in the engine as deployed only when each admitted expert is read once, not twice: then they close
\dmNewTogetherLow\% of the gap, in a test registered before its machines were rented. A second registered test, on two
machines, shows why: the engine's in-step fetches push MIN's set out of the cache. With them off, MIN's
fewest-admission set read twice runs \hsPlanZeroLowRng{} times as fast as a deployed cache without them, and reading
it once gains \hsOneReadMarginLowRng{} more of the deployed cache's speed. Policies without foresight, built from the
engine's own mechanisms, recover at most \olCapLowMax\% of what an oracle that reads ahead gains. In host reads, half
of foresight's value needs the routing of about \dcMed{} times as many distinct experts per layer as the cache holds.
