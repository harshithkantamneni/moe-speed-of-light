A Mixture-of-Experts model too large for one consumer GPU keeps most of its experts in host memory, and decoding one
token at a time then waits on host reads. We bound how fast such decoding can be on a given machine, measure where the
remaining time goes, and price knowledge of the future routing. The bound is the fewest host reads any expert cache of
a given size can make (Belady's MIN with bypass) at the machine's probed read rate. Our llama.cpp expert cache,
faster than two other systems at most settings, reaches \dcShareMinLowMin--\dcShareMinLowMax\% of the bound's speed on
consumer machines at the smallest cache we test. Oracles in the engine then change what is cached (the experts MIN
keeps instead of ours), how each admitted expert is read (once, copied straight into its GPU slot, instead of twice, by
the CPU and then by a background copy), and when. A per-machine table that also copies misses into slots the cache did
not choose hides the first change: with it, MIN's set pays only when read once; without it, on \dpNWord{} machines,
MIN's set alone closes \dmCpuSetaloneZeroLow\% of the gap and both changes together \dmCpuTogetherZeroLow\%. Even
reading ahead leaves about half of the gap. On five fast-link machines, four policies without foresight, built from the engine's admission rule and
layer-ahead prediction, recover at most \olCapLowMax\% of an oracle's gain. In trace replay, half of foresight's value needs the routing of as many distinct
experts as \dcMed{} of a full cache. Registered predictions were committed before their machines were rented; the
consumer range and the replay result are post hoc.
