\section{Scientific benchmarking checklist}\label{app:rules}
How this paper follows the twelve rules of Hoefler and Belli~\cite{hoefler2015benchmarking}.

\begin{longtable}{p{0.32\textwidth}p{0.61\textwidth}}\toprule
Rule & Where and how \\\midrule\endhead
1. Report the base case of a speed-up and its absolute performance. & Every ratio in \cref{tab:headline} is next to both absolute speeds; llama.cpp is configured with per-layer expert offload at equal expert memory, the setting its users run. \\
2. Explain subsets of benchmarks or resources. & Two models chosen for size and precision (MXFP4, BF16), a third that is FreeToken's own headline model; budgets span what fits a 32\,GB card; the 43.75\% budget is omitted where it does not fit. All 30 AIME-25 problems are used. \\
3. Arithmetic mean for costs, harmonic mean for rates. & Every problem decodes the same number of tokens, so the harmonic mean of rates is total tokens over total time; ratios of total time agree with the reported ratios of mean rates within 0.003. \\
4. Avoid summarising ratios. & Ratios are computed from mean speeds, never averaged across budgets or models; ranges list the per-budget values. \\
5. Report whether measurements are deterministic; give confidence intervals. & Decode is greedy and deterministic per engine; timing is not. Every comparison has a 95\% interval. \\
6. Do not assume normality. & Intervals are bootstrap percentile intervals (10{,}000 resamples, paired by problem); no distributional assumption. \\
7. Compare nondeterministic data soundly. & Paired bootstrap of the ratio of means; the variant a system is scored with is picked on a separate launch (except the Qwen3 25\% row of \cref{tab:headline}). \\
8. Check that central tendency is the right summary. & Mean speed over 256 tokens is FreeToken's own metric; per-token p50 and p99 latencies are recorded for every problem in the released rows. \\
9. Document all factors and the setup. & Machines, probes, power limits, memory clocks (\cref{sec:setting}), software commits (llama.cpp 4da6337, FreeToken 0d652e7), flags and job scripts are released; \cref{app:artifact}. \\
10. Report measurement, synchronisation and summarisation. & One client times every system from streamed token arrivals; no server-side timers enter a comparison (\cref{sec:setting}). \\
11. Show upper performance bounds. & The speed limit (\cref{sec:limit}) and all-in-VRAM speed are columns of \cref{tab:headline}; \cref{tab:limit} tightens the limit four ways; \cref{sec:limit} applies it to the published literature. \\
12. Plot enough to interpret; connect points only for trends. & \Cref{fig:shapley,fig:factorial,fig:ablation} are bar charts; \cref{fig:ratio,fig:audit,fig:grid} are scatter plots; \cref{fig:foresight} connects measured windows for the trend only, and its $W_{50}$ is a stated log interpolation between two measured windows. \\

\section{The law's form and its failures}\label{app:law}
\paragraph{Is the max the right form?} In 19 of the \lawCfg{} configurations nothing is copied, so they test one
roof. We therefore refit the law and its alternatives on the same \lawRows{} measurements under leave-one-host-out
validation (each of the \lawHostsLoho{} hosts predicted with constants fitted on the other \lawHostsLohoOther): the token-level max of
\cref{eq:law} with $G$ refit has a median error of \lohoMaxMed\% (90th percentile \lohoMaxPninety\%); the additive form
$G+X_c/B_c+X_p/B_p$, \lohoAddMed\% (\lohoAddPninety\%); a per-layer max summed over layers from the engine's per-layer
miss histograms, \lohoLayerMed\% (\lohoLayerPninety\%). The additive form ties in sample but misses the three
rows that copy misses on demand behind a 53\,GB/s link, where both paths carry most of the bytes, by
\lohoAddFastFetchMin\%, while the max is within $\pm$0.2\% there; on the slow-link copy rows the additive form is the
closer of the two. The per-layer form neither beats the max nor explains its failures: the Jensen gap between a sum
of per-layer maxima and the maximum of sums is a fifth of the Core Ultra's residual, none of the EPYC 7352's, and has
the wrong sign on the EPYC 9655 and on the prefetch rows. We keep \cref{eq:law}, with its evidence stated: the max beats the sum on three fast-link rows and ties
elsewhere, so the form is settled only where both paths carry most of the bytes. FreeToken's own runs are not
predicted, because its counters are not exposed; the law covers our engine and llama.cpp.

\paragraph{Where it fails, and why.} Each failure now has a measured cause. (i) Copying misses on demand (FETCH) at
25\% is over-predicted by \lohoFrozenFetchUltra\% on the Core Ultra 7 and \lohoFrozenFetchEpyc\% on the EPYC 7352 (links
of 36 and 26\,GB/s); \lohoMaxFetchUltra\% and \lohoMaxFetchEpyc\% with $G$ refit. The engine's per-layer timers show
why: a CPU miss served beside a copy takes \fetchBesideMin--\fetchBesideMax\% longer than one served alone, so the two
paths share DRAM less evenly than the probe's combined rate says; a variant of the law that uses the probe's
per-path rates under contention and a per-layer hand-off constant predicts both rows within \lohoAsymFetchUltra\% and
\lohoAsymFetchEpyc\%, at a worse median (\lohoAsymMed\%). (ii) llama.cpp on a Core Ultra 7 with 8 performance and 16 efficiency cores runs at 46\% of the
law at its default thread count, because it splits every matrix evenly across its threads; our helpers claim work
dynamically and stay on the law on the same machine. We did not test llama.cpp restricted to the performance cores.
(iii) On a 12-channel EPYC 9655 (559\,GB/s) host memory stops being the limit (\lohoMaxEpycMin{} to
\lohoMaxEpycMax\%): the per-layer timers show a floor of \epycFloorMin--\epycFloorMax\,$\mu$s per layer with CPU misses
that no desktop host has (their intercepts are at or below zero), the latency of the hand-off at a bandwidth where a
13\,MB expert takes 24\,$\mu$s. (iv) Prefetch configurations are under-predicted by \lohoMaxPfMin{} to
\lohoMaxPfMax\%: their copies overlap attention, which the law charges in full.

\section{Foresight in the engine}\label{app:foresight}
Three jobs ran oracle policies in the engine, each on a rented RTX 5090 host with weaker host memory than host B's.
\Cref{tab:single_read} lists every cell of the single-read factorial of \cref{sec:gap} on host O3 (a Ryzen 9 9950X
whose probe reads \fsfHostCpu\,GB/s by the CPU at the helper count, \fsfHostLink{} over the link, \fsfHostBoth{} with
both and \fsfHostMax{} at best; the law's FETCH tables \texttt{0,0,1,2,2} for gpt-oss and \texttt{0,0,1,1,2,3,4,4,5}
for Qwen3), and \cref{tab:accounting_measured} the milliseconds per token of every engine state the three jobs
realised beside each host's limit and the model's values. \Cref{tab:foresight} lists the first job (093), the
hit-optimal oracle with a window sweep on host O1 (a Ryzen 9 9950X: \fsmHostCpu, \fsmHostLink, \fsmHostBoth{} and
\fsmHostMax\,GB/s; tables \texttt{0,0,1,2,3} and \texttt{0,0,1,2,3,4,5,6,7}), and \cref{tab:foresight_bytes} the
second (094), MIN with bypass with background admissions beside the hit-optimal oracle on host O2 (a Ryzen 9 7950X:
\fsbHostCpu, \fsbHostLink, \fsbHostBoth{} and \fsbHostMax\,GB/s; the same tables). On O1 and O2 every cell but
gpt-oss 40\% and Qwen3 43.75\% is host-bound at the limit; on O3, whose memory is stronger, the same four.

\paragraph{The single-read oracles.} In the fetch oracle the step's plan is computed before the layer runs from the
trace's record of the current step: the misses MIN with bypass would admit are fetched by the engine's FETCH
operation into their victims' slots and run from there, and every other miss runs on the CPU; the oracle refuses an
admission when no resident's next use is further than the candidate's. The single-read prefetch admits, each step,
the non-resident experts whose first use is at least $d$ steps away ($d{=}2$ unpaced, 3 paced), provided the victim's
next use is further than the candidate's use after that first one; an expert used sooner than $d$ steps away misses
on the CPU, or is fetched when the two are combined. The same policies simulated on the trace with the engine's
semantics (the lookahead within the sequence, residents carried over) predict the engine's reads and hit rates: the
fetch oracle's hit rate within \fsfSimHitDiffMax{} points and its reads within \fsfSimReadsDiffMin{} to
\fsfSimReadsDiffMax\% (the engine reads fewer at the GPU-bound cells, where its cache is warm from the previous
configuration's run and the simulation starts cold), the combined oracle's hits within two points
(\cref{tab:single_read}). The paced copy path copies one 16\,MB piece at a time on a single copier thread and
publishes a landed copy at the start of the next step; the unpaced path copies each admission as one transfer. The
hit-optimal oracle's background admissions drive host memory at \fshHostUtilMin--\fshHostUtilMax\% of the probe's best
rate at the host-bound cells, the prefetching single-read oracles at \fsbpHostUtilMin--\fsbpHostUtilMax\%, the fetch
oracle at \fsfHostUtilMin--\fsfHostUtilMax\%. The predictions and their outcomes are in
\texttt{prereg/foresight\_outcome\_095.md}: of 56 clauses, 44 held and 12 failed, all of size (\cref{app:prereg}).

\paragraph{The double-reading oracles.} Both read the routing of the next $W$ decode steps; their copies ride the
paced admission path, and an expert still in flight at its step runs on the CPU like any other miss. The
\emph{hit-optimal} oracle admits the experts those steps will route to, soonest first, evicting the resident whose
next use is furthest (Belady within the window), for one future use as readily as for ten. The \emph{bytes-optimal}
oracle is MIN with bypass with its admissions made through the background path: a miss is served where it is, by the
CPU or a demand copy, and admitted only if its next use comes before the furthest next use among the residents. With
the whole sequence in view the hit-optimal oracle runs \fsmRatioGMid$\times$ [\fsmRatioLoGMid, \fsmRatioHiGMid] the
online policy at gpt-oss 25\% and \fsmRatioQMid$\times$ [\fsmRatioLoQMid, \fsmRatioHiQMid] at Qwen3 25\% on host O1,
\fspRatioGMid$\times$ and \fspRatioQMid$\times$ on O2, and \fsHitGpuMin--\fsHitGpuMax$\times$ at the two GPU-bound
cells; half of its gain at gpt-oss 25\% arrives between $W{=}4$ and 16 (\fsmWfiftyGMid{} tokens by log
interpolation; Qwen3 25\%: \fsmWfiftyQMid{} against the trace's 3.9). Where the host binds hardest it loses,
\fsHitLossMin--\fsHitLossMax\% at gpt-oss 11\% and Qwen3 12.5\% on both hosts, and at every window on O1
(\fsmNegWinMin--\fsmNegWinMax$\times$): it reads more host bytes than the online policy (\fsmReadsOracleGLow{} and
\fsmReadsOracleQLow{} experts per token against \fsmReadsBaseGLow{} and \fsmReadsBaseQLow),
\fsmTotalReadsOptMin--\fsmTotalReadsOptMax$\times$ the optimum's $R^\star$, because Belady admits an expert used once
where the optimum bypasses it and because each admission is read twice. An unpaced copy path, run at the four
host-bound cells of O1, reaches 98--99\% hits at three of them and still loses at gpt-oss 11\%, so copies that arrive
in time do not remove the loss there: the extra bytes cause it. The bytes-optimal oracle gains
\fsbGainHighMin--\fsbGainHighMax\% at the four cells of 25\% and above and \fsbGainLowMin--\fsbGainLowMax\% at the two
below (\fsbGainLowMaxAny\% with a 16-token window); its misses fall (\fsbMissesBaseGMid{} to \fsbMissesGMid{} and
\fsbMissesBaseQMid{} to \fsbMissesQMid{} per token at 25\%) but its total reads, misses plus the admissions that read
each admitted expert again, are \fsbTotalReadsOptMin--\fsbTotalReadsOptMax$\times$ $R^\star$ and exceed the online
policy's by \fsbLowReadsVsBaseMin--\fsbLowReadsVsBaseMax\% at the two lowest budgets; the law on its own counters
predicts its time within \fsbLawErrMax\% at every cell, so its gain is the bytes it saves, at most the \fsbLawGainHighMax{}
points of \fsbGainHighMax{} that the law accounts for at a cell. The hit-optimal oracle reads
\fspVsBypassBytesMin--\fspVsBypassBytesMax$\times$ the bytes-optimal one's host bytes on O2 and gains
\fspGainHighMin--\fspGainHighMax\% at the same four cells, and the law, with its GPU term calibrated on the online run,
over-predicts those runs by \fspLawErrHighMin--\fspLawErrHighMax\% (on O1, \fsmLawErrPosMin--\fsmLawErrPosMax\%): its gain
is the overlap of copies issued ahead of their use with the step, which the law charges in sequence. Pacing the
online policy's own copies changes it by under 1\% (\fsmPacedMin--\fsmPacedMax$\times$). With no FETCH table (every
miss on the CPU) the online policy runs \fsmAllcpuMin--\fsmAllcpuMax\% slower at the four host-bound cells; with the
engine's within-layer overlap of a layer's CPU misses with its GPU hits turned off it changes by under 1\% at every
cell (0.5\% faster to 0.6\% slower), so the overlap the accounting charges is across the step and in rebalancing, not
within a layer. The bytes-optimal oracle with a 16-token window keeps \fsbSixteenShareMin--\fsbSixteenShareMax\% of
the whole-sequence gain at the five cells where that gain's interval excludes zero (more than all of it at gpt-oss
11\%, where the shorter window admits less).

\paragraph{Why the bytes-optimal oracle's misses stay above the optimum's reads.} Besides the second read, its misses alone are
\fsbReadsRatioHostMin--\fsbReadsRatioHostMax$\times$ the optimum's reads at the four host-bound cells (at the two
GPU-bound ones the ideal simulation below is itself 1.3--1.4$\times$ the optimum, which sees across sequences, and the
engine's misses are at or below it). The engine's paced copy path moves one 16\,MB piece at a time from pageable host
memory on a single copier thread, behind the demand copies, publishes a landed copy at the start of the next step, and
admits nothing while 64 copies are queued; at 16--30 admissions per token (210--285\,MB) it lags the admissions by
steps, and an expert still in flight is a miss the CPU reads again. The last four columns of \cref{tab:foresight_bytes}
simulate MIN with bypass on the measured routing trace with the engine's own semantics (the lookahead within the
sequence, residents carried over, no queue cap) and a copy that lands $d$ steps after it is issued, during which the
expert misses and is not re-admitted: at $d{=}1$, the ideal, the misses come within
\fslIdealOverMin--\fslIdealOverMax\% of the limit's optimum at the host-bound cells; the engine's misses meet the
simulation at $d{=}$\fslFitHostMin--\fslFitHostMax{} there, about three steps, and at or below \fslFitGpuMax{} at
the GPU-bound cells. At the fitted latency the simulation admits \fsbSimAdmitMin--\fsbSimAdmitMax$\times$ what
the engine admitted, so the queue cap, not only the latency, keeps the engine's misses above the ideal. The predictions and their outcomes are in
\texttt{prereg/foresight\_outcome\_093.md} and \texttt{094.md}: of 36 clauses for the first job, 9 held, 2 held on
the point estimate, 21 failed and 4 were untested; of 38 for the second, 12 held, 1 on the point estimate, 24 failed
and 1 was untested (\cref{app:prereg}). The second job's failures are of size, not direction: the bypass oracle's
misses were predicted within 15\% of the optimum's reads and came 29--47\% above (the copy latency and the queue
cap); its hit rate was predicted within 4 points of the optimum's and fell 5--11 points short at three cells; its gain
was predicted at 10--45\% at the host-bound cells and came below that at the two lowest budgets; the law was predicted
to over-predict it by 10--40\% at the host-bound cells and over-predicted by 5--9\%; and the two oracles were
predicted within 15\% of each other at 25\% and above, where the hit-optimal one was 1.24--1.38$\times$ faster.

Placing each layer's hot experts is the first gain: a static cache of the 32 experts per layer most requested on
other text (the models' own MATH-500 outputs) runs \ablStaticGpt$\times$ and \ablStaticQwen$\times$ stock, which pins
whole layers instead. Its hit rate (\ablStaticHitGpt\% and \ablStaticHitQwen\%) depends on how well the profile
matches the workload: profiled in hindsight on the measured problems it hits \ablHindHitGpt\% and \ablHindHitQwen\%
and runs faster (hatched). Replacing experts online closes that gap without a profile, if admissions are rationed:
LRU admits every miss, and on Qwen3 its copies raise host reads from \rdStaticQwen{} to \rdLruQwen{} experts per
token, so it runs \ablLruQwen$\times$ the static cache (\ablLruGpt$\times$ on gpt-oss); the decayed-frequency policy
reads \rdDfaGpt{} and \rdDfaQwen{} per token, runs \ablDfaStaticGpt$\times$ and \ablDfaStaticQwen$\times$ the
profiled static cache, and the hindsight cache runs \ablHindGpt{} and \ablHindQwen{} times as fast as it. On traces
LRU misses only \polLruMoreMin--\polLruMoreMax\% more than decayed frequency at these budgets (\cref{sec:traces}); the
rest of its cost here is the copies it admits. On host S, with everything else as in the full system, LRU runs
\scLruSlowMin--\scLruSlowMax\% slower than decayed frequency across the six cells of \cref{tab:headline} and trails
FreeToken at \scLruTrailCells{} of them: the admission policy is most of the lead over FreeToken
(\scPolicyShareGptMin--\scPolicyShareGptMax\% of it at gpt-oss 25 and 40\%, more than the whole of it at gpt-oss 11\%
and Qwen3 12.5 and 25\%). The GPU-signalled CPU helpers, GPU slot maps, GPU sampling and FETCH add 2--9\% each, and the
law's FETCH table 5--8\% over the fixed one. The full system runs \ablGptX$\times$ (gpt-oss) and \ablQwenX$\times$
(Qwen3) stock llama.cpp.

\paragraph{Choosing the split per machine.} On host B, the law's table beats the fixed table we used
before at every budget of both models, by \lawGainMin--\lawGainMax\% (launch 1). \Cref{tab:split} shows why at one
budget: a single missed expert should run on the CPU (about 155\,$\mu$s, hidden behind the GPU's other experts) rather
than be copied (about 225\,$\mu$s, on the critical path); that entry alone is worth \splitFOne\%. Fetching everything,
FreeToken's offload strategy expressed in our engine, is the worst table, and running everything on the CPU the second
worst. The law's table was within 1\% of the best of five alternatives on launch 1 and tied with it on launch 2. Given
each run's measured misses, \cref{eq:split} ranks the tables correctly except law against lite, which are within 1\%,
and overstates their differences about 1.8$\times$.

\paragraph{Machines with half the link.} On RTX 5090s whose links we measured at 27--30\,GB/s instead of 53 (jobs
085, 085b and 088: gpt-oss next to a Core i9-14900K, Qwen3 next to a Ryzen 9 7900 in a PCIe~4.0 slot), the law's tables fetch less
(gpt-oss \texttt{\hpTableGpt}, Qwen3 \texttt{\hpTableQwen}) and beat the fixed table by \hpGptLawMin--\hpGptLawMax\% on
gpt-oss and \hpQwenLawMin--\hpQwenLawMax\% on Qwen3, about twice the gain on host B for Qwen3 and four to six times
for gpt-oss: the per-machine split matters most where the link is slow. On the Ryzen, ours leads FreeToken by
\hpQwenFtMin--\hpQwenFtMax$\times$; with the fixed table it would trail at 12.5\%. On the Core i9 the first comparison
(job 085) was confounded: FreeToken's hybrid backend ran gpt-oss at \iNineHybridMin--\iNineHybridMax\,tok/s, below its
own offload backend (\iNineOffloadMin--\iNineOffloadMax), because its CPU executor spread 23 threads across the 8
performance and 16 efficiency cores and its calibration then sent two thirds of the misses over the slow link, the
same failure \cref{sec:law} reports for llama.cpp; the 1.4--2.0$\times$ lead that gave us measured a thread
configuration. We reran it (job 088, a second rental of the same CPU model) with FreeToken's executor on 8 threads
pinned to the performance cores: its hybrid backend then runs \slHybGainMin--\slHybGainMax$\times$ faster than before
(\slHybLow, \slHybMid{} and \slHybHigh\,tok/s; its calibration now copies \slFetchFrac\% of misses), beating its own
offload backend at 11 and 25\% and tying it at 40\%, and ours leads its better configuration by \slFtLow, \slFtMid{}
and \slFtHigh$\times$ (lowest interval bound \slFtLoMin). Restricted to the CPU alone, FreeToken's 8-thread executor
decodes \slFzeroMid\,tok/s at every budget. llama.cpp on the same rental runs \slLlamaTeight\,tok/s with one thread
per performance core (\slLlamaErrTeight\% from the law's prediction) and \slLlamaTall{} with all 24 threads
(\slLlamaErrTall\%): the hybrid-core failure is a thread-count choice, and the law holds once the choice is right. The
law predicted llama.cpp on the Ryzen within \hpLlamaQwen\%. \Cref{fig:ratio} puts every same-machine comparison on one
axis.

\paragraph{Long outputs.} Our lead does not come from the first 256 tokens. On gpt-oss, on ten held-out hard
problems (AIME 2022, problems 11--15 of both exams) that neither system has finished reasoning about after 2{,}048
tokens (at most \hardAnsMax{} of 10 reach an answer), ours runs \hardAllA, \hardAllB{} and \hardAllC{} times FreeToken
at 11, 25 and 40\% over all 2{,}048 tokens, and \hardLateA, \hardLateB{} and \hardLateC{} over tokens 257--2{,}048
(job 083, host A); both engines stream one event per token. On Qwen3, over tokens 257--2{,}048 of ten held-out
MATH-500 problems, ours runs \longQwenA{}, \longQwenB{} and \longQwenC{} times FreeToken at 12.5, 25 and 43.75\%.
Both leads are larger than over the same runs' first 256 tokens. Turning off FreeToken's prefill overlap, which lends
it cache slots during each prompt, changes its overall speed by 1\% or less.

\paragraph{FreeToken's own headline model.} On FreeToken's own headline model,
Qwen3.6-35B-A3B in BF16 (256 experts of 6.3\,MB per layer, hybrid linear attention), the two systems are close: ours runs
\qsixFtA{} times FreeToken at 12.5\% and ties at 25 and 37.5\% (\qsixFtB, \qsixFtC), at \qsixLlA--\qsixLlC$\times$ llama.cpp (job 086,
host A). FreeToken as shipped, with its automatic backend and cache size, runs this model at \qsixAuto\,tok/s on that
machine, above the 77--83\,tok/s its paper reports on an RTX 5090, so our setup does not handicap it; FreeToken's
fetch cap and thread count were left at their defaults, while our FETCH table was swept. Our lead is therefore
model-dependent: largest with few large experts (gpt-oss, top-4 of 128), smallest with many small ones.

\paragraph{Other systems and outputs.} Two newer llama.cpp-based systems, Pipelined Sharding~\cite{pipelinedsharding2026} and
leloch's expert cache~\cite{leloch2026}, run gpt-oss-120b at 1.28--1.44$\times$ stock llama.cpp; ours ran 1.42--2.34$\times$
the better of them (job 077). Teacher-forced on fixed text (\klStepsGpt{} steps per model) against stock llama.cpp with every weight in the
VRAM of an RTX PRO 6000 (job 090), our cache at 25\% has a mean KL divergence over the full vocabulary of
\klMeanGpt{} nats on gpt-oss and \klMeanQwen{} on Qwen3 (99.9th percentile \klNinetyNineGpt{} and \klNinetyNineQwen,
maximum \klMaxGpt{} and \klMaxQwen), picks the same next token on \klTopGpt\% and \klTopQwen\% of steps, and changes
the loss by \klNllGpt\% and \klNllQwen\%. Stock llama.cpp's own \texttt{--n-cpu-moe} placement diverges from the same
reference by as much (mean KL \klStockMeanGpt{} and \klStockMeanQwen, agreement \klStockTopGpt\% and \klStockTopQwen\%):
the differences are those of running experts on the CPU (the helpers quantise activations to Q8\_0 where the GPU path
uses Q8\_1), not of the cache. Task accuracy was not measured.

\paragraph{Prompts and batches.} The cache serves single-token steps; prompts and batches above one run llama.cpp's
stock graph with every expert in host memory, and pay for the cache's resident experts and its one-thread CPU side.
In \texttt{llama-batched-bench} at 25\% on host S (job 089, both models) our prefill at 512 and 2{,}048
tokens runs \bbPrefillMin--\bbPrefillMax\% of stock llama.cpp's and our decode at 2 and 4 parallel sequences
\bbBatchMin--\bbBatchMax\% of it (the tool feeds both engines the same random tokens).

\section{Artifact description}\label{app:artifact}
\begin{itemize}\setlength\itemsep{1pt}
\item \textbf{System.} A patch to llama.cpp commit \texttt{4da6337} (the expert cache, FETCH, the mailbox and the
\texttt{llama-ec-bench} instrumentation: teacher-forced loss, logit dumps, routing lookahead records); built with CUDA 13.0
for compute capability 12.0.
\item \textbf{Jobs.} One shell script per job; each installs FreeToken at commit \texttt{0d652e7}, downloads the
models from Hugging Face, runs the bandwidth probes, writes any on-machine prediction, runs the client, and stores raw
rows (one JSON line per problem), server logs and engine counters. A small runner rents a Vast instance, streams a
heartbeat, fetches the results and only then destroys the instance.
\item \textbf{Analysis.} \texttt{scripts/*\_stats.py} compute every interval from the raw rows;
\texttt{scripts/perlayer\_model.py}, \texttt{speed\_limit\_v2.py}, \texttt{shapley\_gap.py}, \texttt{policy\_study.py},
\texttt{foresight\_exact.py}, \texttt{batchk\_trace.py}, \texttt{fig\_audit\_sol.py} and \texttt{scorecard.py} produce the
model comparison, the limit, the accounting, the trace studies, the audit figure and the scorecard;
\texttt{scripts/wsg\_tables.py} and \texttt{wsg\_numbers2.py} generate every table, figure and number in this paper from
their outputs. The audit's \audAllN{} published rows and the physical speed-of-light model they are scored against are
\texttt{data/published\_measurements.json}, \texttt{data/audit/} and \texttt{scripts/audit.py}.
\item \textbf{Cost.} The \costRentals{} rentals (\costMachines{} distinct machines) of jobs 058--\costJobHi{} cost \costUsd{} US dollars at their hourly GPU prices, by the ledger in the repository; storage and download charges, which the ledger omits, add to this.
\end{itemize}
