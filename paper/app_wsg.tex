\section{The calibrated time model}\label{app:law}
Our cache's one per-machine decision, how many of a layer's misses to fetch rather than run on the CPU, comes from a
calibrated model of time per token. With $X_c$ the bytes the CPU reads per token and $X_p$ the bytes the link reads,
\begin{equation}
T \;=\; G_{\mathrm{fit}} \;+\; \max\!\Big(\frac{X_c}{B_c},\ \frac{X_p}{B_p},\ \frac{X_c+X_p}{B_{cp}}\Big),
\label{eq:law}
\end{equation}
with one constant $G_{\mathrm{fit}}$ per engine and model, fitted once on one host and frozen (it is neither the profiled
$G$ of \cref{eq:sum} nor $T_{\text{GPU}}$ of \cref{eq:demand}). It is a calibrated model, not a law: $G_{\mathrm{fit}}$ is
fitted, and it exceeds the whole all-in-VRAM token (\lawGRefit{} against \vramMs\,ms) because it absorbs
per-token host work and the CPU helpers' shortfall against the probe (below). As a predictor of time it ties a naive
one-path model with the same $G_{\mathrm{fit}}$ (time $= G_{\mathrm{fit}} +$ all host bytes over the best probed rate):

It over-predicts Qwen3's speed on the later hosts by \lawFrozenQwenMin{} to \lawFrozenQwenMax\%, a budget-dependent
bias whose $G_{\mathrm{fit}}$ was borrowed from llama.cpp. Its value is the per-machine fetch table it picks (\cref{eq:split} below),

\paragraph{What the model says, and the split it picks.} \Cref{eq:law} is a calibrated model of execution that reads host
memory in sequence with its GPU work, as our cache and llama.cpp do. For such an engine every mechanism acts only
through the bytes read from host memory per token and the number of read paths in use: a second path adds bandwidth on
every host of the blind test but the 12-channel EPYC ($B_{cp}$ against $\max(B_c,B_p)$: \secondMin--\secondMax\%
on its Ryzen hosts, \secondUltra\% on the Core Ultra, \secondEpyc\% on the Zen~2 EPYC; the RTX 3090 host's Core
i9-11900KF, where the two paths together read less than the CPU alone, is a second exception), and that is the whole
gain available to fetching or splitting misses between the CPU and PCIe. Prefetching never reduces the bytes; what
it can buy is overlap of the reads with the step, which the model does not price: charging every read in sequence, it
over-predicts the time per token of MIN prefetched by \fxPacedLawTimeMin{} to \fxPacedLawTimeMax\% (\cref{sec:foresight})
and of a Belady prefetch by up to \fspLawErrHighMax\% (\cref{app:foresight}). The model's job in the system is the split.
Its
per-layer form,
\begin{equation}
T(n,f)=\max\!\big(\tfrac{fS}{B_p}+g,\ [n{>}f](L_c+\tfrac{(n-f)S}{B_c}),\ [0{<}f{<}n]\tfrac{nS}{B_{cp}}\big),
\label{eq:split}
\end{equation}
with $g$ the GPU's expert work per layer (48\,$\mu$s for Qwen3, 37\,$\mu$s for gpt-oss, from profiles) and
$L_c{=}20\,\mu$s the hand-off, picks for each count $n$ of missed experts the number $f$ to copy with the smallest
$T$. On host B the model's table beats the fixed table we used before at every budget of both models, by
\lawGainMin--\lawGainMax\%; on hosts whose link we measured at 27--30\,GB/s instead of 53 it fetches less and gains
\hpGptLawMin--\hpGptLawMax\% on gpt-oss and \hpQwenLawMin--\hpQwenLawMax\% on Qwen3, the per-machine split mattering
most where the link is slow (\cref{app:system}). In the running example on host B the table is
\texttt{\scTableGptB} ($f(0..4)$): one missed expert runs on the CPU, hidden behind the GPU's other experts; from two
misses the model copies one. The lead over FreeToken at that cell, \ftRatioGptMid$\times$, is mostly the admission
policy (\scPolicyShareGptMin\% of it by the LRU ablation of \cref{app:system}), the rest the CPU path that FreeToken's
bandwidth model declines to use there.

\paragraph{Is the max the right form?} In 19 of the \lawCfg{} configurations nothing is copied, so they test one
roof. We therefore refit the model and its alternatives on the same \lawRows{} measurements under leave-one-host-out
validation (each of the \lawHostsLoho{} hosts predicted with constants fitted on the other \lawHostsLohoOther): the token-level max of
\cref{eq:law} with $G_{\mathrm{fit}}$ refit has a median error of \lohoMaxMed\% (90th percentile \lohoMaxPninety\%); the additive form
$G_{\mathrm{fit}}+X_c/B_c+X_p/B_p$, \lohoAddMed\% (\lohoAddPninety\%); a per-layer max summed over layers from the engine's per-layer
miss histograms, \lohoLayerMed\% (\lohoLayerPninety\%). The additive form ties in sample but misses the three
rows that copy misses on demand behind a 53\,GB/s link, where both paths carry most of the bytes, by
\lohoAddFastFetchMin\%, while the max is within $\pm$0.2\% there; on the slow-link copy rows the additive form is the
closer of the two. The per-layer form neither beats the max nor explains its failures: the Jensen gap between a sum
of per-layer maxima and the maximum of sums is a fifth of the Core Ultra's residual, none of the EPYC 7352's, and has
the wrong sign on the EPYC 9655 and on the prefetch rows. We keep \cref{eq:law}, with its evidence stated: the max beats the sum on the three fast-link copy rows and on the
EPYC 9655 rows, where both fail, the sum is closer on the two slow-link copy rows, and elsewhere the two are within a
few points, so the form is settled only where both paths carry most of the bytes. FreeToken's own runs are not
predicted, because its counters are not exposed; the model covers our engine and llama.cpp.

\paragraph{Where it fails, and why.} Each failure now has a measured cause. (i) Copying misses on demand (FETCH) at
25\% is over-predicted by \lohoFrozenFetchUltra\% on the Core Ultra 7 and \lohoFrozenFetchEpyc\% on the EPYC 7352 (links
of 36 and 26\,GB/s); \lohoMaxFetchUltra\% and \lohoMaxFetchEpyc\% with $G_{\mathrm{fit}}$ refit. The engine's per-layer timers show
why: a CPU miss served beside a copy takes \fetchBesideMin--\fetchBesideMax\% longer than one served alone, so the two
paths share DRAM less evenly than the probe's combined rate says; a variant of the model that uses the probe's
per-path rates under contention and a per-layer hand-off constant predicts both rows within \lohoAsymFetchUltra\% and
\lohoAsymFetchEpyc\%, at a worse median (\lohoAsymMed\%). (ii) llama.cpp on a Core Ultra 7 with 8 performance and 16 efficiency cores runs at 46\% of the
model's prediction at its default thread count, because it splits every matrix evenly across its threads; our helpers claim work
dynamically and stay on the model on the same machine. We did not test llama.cpp restricted to the performance cores.
(iii) On a 12-channel EPYC 9655 (559\,GB/s) host memory stops being the limit (\lohoMaxEpycMin{} to
\lohoMaxEpycMax\% with $G_{\mathrm{fit}}$ refit under leave-one-host-out validation; \cref{tab:law} gives the blind errors): the per-layer timers show a floor of \epycFloorMin--\epycFloorMax\,$\mu$s per layer with CPU misses
that no desktop host has (their intercepts are at or below zero), the latency of the hand-off at a bandwidth where a
13\,MB expert takes 24\,$\mu$s. (iv) Prefetch configurations are under-predicted by \lohoMaxPfMin{} to
\lohoMaxPfMax\%: their copies overlap attention, which the model charges in full.

\section{The accounting as a model}\label{app:accounting}
\begin{figure}[t]\centering
\includegraphics[width=\linewidth]{figs/shapley.pdf}
\caption{The accounting as a model: Shapley attribution of the gap between our cache's measured time per token and
the bound, for every cell of \cref{tab:headline} on host B, averaged over all \shOrders{} orders in which the
five fixes can be applied. Fixes: overlapping host reads with GPU work, which in \cref{eq:limit}'s form also moves
experts onto the CPU until the two paths balance; the optimum's reads instead of the best online policy's (foresight);
the best online policy's reads at the probe's highest rate instead of the engine's reads through \cref{eq:law}
(policy and read paths); the engine's per-token host work; and GPU kernels at the datasheet rate, the term that
closes the model on the measured time. Whiskers: the range of foresight's marginal cost over the orders.}
\label{fig:shapley}
\end{figure}

 Between the measured time of a cell and its limit lie five modelled fixes:
overlap of host reads with GPU work (in \cref{eq:limit}'s form, which also rebalances experts onto the CPU), the
optimum's reads instead of the online policy's (\emph{foresight}), the best online policy's reads at the probe's
highest rate instead of the engine's through \cref{eq:law} (policy and read paths), the engine's per-token host work,
and GPU kernels at the datasheet rate. Adding them in a fixed order makes the first one added look largest, so we
report each fix's Shapley value, its average marginal cost over every order (\cref{fig:shapley}), a method with a
precedent in performance debugging of microservices \citep{shapleyiq2024}. Two of the five terms are definitions
rather than measurements: the GPU term is the residual, its factor calibrated per cell so that the model with no fix
applied reproduces the measured time, and ``overlap'' is \cref{eq:limit}'s form. On host B the model has two regimes.
In the four host-bound cells missing foresight is the largest cost, \shFsHostMin--\shFsHostMax\% of the gap
(\shFsHostTokMin--\shFsHostTokMax\% of the token; in the running example \shFsGMid\%, where it edges overlap by
\shTieMs\,ms and is the largest marginal cost in \shOrdersGMid{} of the \shOrders{} orders); in the two GPU-bound cells
host reads serialised with GPU work is largest, \shOvlGpuMin--\shOvlGpuMax\% of the gap, and foresight second at
\shFsGpuMin\%. Policy and read paths cost \shPolMin--\shPolMax\%, per-token host work \shHostMin--\shHostMax\%, and
GPU kernels below datasheet, net of the overlap the engine achieves, \shGpuMin--\shGpuMax\%. The regimes are a
property of the host: recomputed for the three oracle hosts, whose host memory is weaker, foresight edges overlap at
three of their six GPU-bound cells. The model prices foresight and overlap as nearly independent players, and the
interior states it values are not realised by any online policy. The rest of this section measures them.

\paragraph{Where the gain comes from.} \Cref{fig:ablation} builds the system up from stock llama.cpp on one machine.
Placing each layer's hot experts is the first gain: a static cache of the 32 experts per layer most requested on
other text (the models' own MATH-500 outputs) runs \ablStaticGpt$\times$ and \ablStaticQwen$\times$ stock, which pins
whole layers instead. Its hit rate (\ablStaticHitGpt\% and \ablStaticHitQwen\%) depends on how well the profile
matches the workload: profiled in hindsight on the measured problems it hits \ablHindHitGpt\% and \ablHindHitQwen\%
and runs faster (hatched). Replacing experts online closes that gap without a profile, if admissions are rationed:
LRU admits every miss, and on Qwen3 its copies raise host reads from \rdStaticQwen{} to \rdLruQwen{} experts per
token, so it runs \ablLruQwen$\times$ the static cache (\ablLruGpt$\times$ on gpt-oss); the decayed-frequency policy
reads \rdDfaGpt{} and \rdDfaQwen{} per token, runs \ablDfaStaticGpt$\times$ and \ablDfaStaticQwen$\times$ the
profiled static cache, and the hindsight cache runs \ablHindGpt{} and \ablHindQwen{} times as fast as it. On traces
LRU misses only \polLruMoreMin--\polLruMoreMax\% more than decayed frequency at these budgets (\cref{app:traces}); the
rest of its cost here is the copies it admits. On host S, with everything else as in the full system, LRU runs
\scLruSlowMin--\scLruSlowMax\% slower than decayed frequency across the six cells of \cref{tab:headline} and trails
FreeToken at \scLruTrailCells{} of them: the admission policy is most of the lead over FreeToken
(\scPolicyShareGptMin--\scPolicyShareGptMax\% of it at gpt-oss 25 and 40\%, more than the whole of it at gpt-oss 11\%
and Qwen3 12.5 and 25\%). The GPU-signalled CPU helpers, GPU slot maps, GPU sampling and FETCH add 2--9\% each, and the
model's FETCH table 5--8\% over the fixed one. The full system runs \ablGptX$\times$ (gpt-oss) and \ablQwenX$\times$
(Qwen3) stock llama.cpp.

\paragraph{How tight is the bound?} \Cref{tab:limit} changes one assumption at a time on host B. Pooled slots need
\globalReadsMin--\globalReadsMax\% fewer reads and raise the host-bound limits by 5--10\%; starting MIN warm changes it by
at most 1.4\%; the median probe sample instead of the highest lowers the host-bound limits by 8\%. The GPU term moves
most: at batch size 1 the all-in-VRAM runs reach only \gpuEffPctGpt\% (gpt-oss) and \gpuEffPctQwen\% (Qwen3) of the
datasheet rate, and with the measured rate the limits at 25\% and above fall to \limMeasGpuGpt{} and
\limMeasGpuQwen\,tok/s, 8--9\% above the all-in-VRAM speed. In the running example (gpt-oss at 25\%) the limit on host B
is \limExMidG\,tok/s (\limMeasGpuGpt{} with the measured GPU rate) and the cache runs 109\,tok/s. With every expert in
its slots the cache matches stock llama.cpp (\cOneTwoEight$\times$ and \cOneTwoEightQ$\times$). The bound assumes an
expert run on the CPU is read from DRAM (MIN's shortest re-read distance is \lthreeReuseMin\,GB of host traffic) and
that computation runs at memory speed. The routing traces are each model's own greedy text on the 30 prompts; replayed
through a simulator of the deployed policy they reproduce the engine's hit rates within \simHitQwenMax{} points.

\paragraph{Reaching the read time.} The microbenchmark of \cref{sec:limit} (\texttt{jobs/ec2/readsched.cu}) takes
MIN's misses per layer and step from \texttt{scripts/readsched.py} and runs no model. Usable cores minus two CPU threads
read their share of host memory from an ordinary buffer, as the engine's helpers do, and the copy engine copies the
rest, 13.25\,MB at a time, from pinned memory into GPU slots; both buffers rotate through 4\,GiB so that no cache holds
them. \emph{Per layer} splits each layer's experts, whole, between the two paths at the probe's rates and waits for
both before the next layer; \emph{per token} sums the same split over the token and waits once; \emph{link only} sends
every read over PCIe, as admitting every miss does (MIN with one read sends only its admissions); \emph{CPU only} reads everything on the CPU. \Cref{tab:readsched}
gives each host. Waiting per layer instead of per token costs at most \jdWaitCostAmdMax{} point of the host term on the
two AMD hosts and \jdWaitCostMax{} on the Intel host, whose link is the slowest; there, reading everything on the CPU
beats the per-layer split.
The split ignores which reads are admissions; \cref{sec:limit} gives the link-aware bound, which sends every
admission over the link (\texttt{scripts/linkaware.py}, \texttt{prereg/linkaware.json}).

\paragraph{Choosing the split per machine.} On host B, the model's table beats the fixed table we used
before at every budget of both models, by \lawGainMin--\lawGainMax\% (launch 1). \Cref{tab:split} shows why at one
budget: a single missed expert should run on the CPU (about 155\,$\mu$s, hidden behind the GPU's other experts) rather
than be copied (about 225\,$\mu$s, on the critical path); that entry alone is worth \splitFOne\%. Fetching everything,
FreeToken's offload strategy expressed in our engine, is the worst table, and running everything on the CPU the second
worst. The model's table was within 1\% of the best of five alternatives on launch 1 and tied with it on launch 2. Given
each run's measured misses, \cref{eq:split} ranks the tables correctly except the model's table against the lite one, which are within 1\%,
and overstates their differences about 1.8$\times$.

\paragraph{Machines with half the link.} On RTX 5090s whose links we measured at 27--30\,GB/s instead of 53 (jobs
085, 085b and 088: gpt-oss next to a Core i9-14900K, Qwen3 next to a Ryzen 9 7900 in a PCIe~4.0 slot), the model's tables fetch less
(gpt-oss \texttt{\hpTableGpt}, Qwen3 \texttt{\hpTableQwen}) and beat the fixed table by \hpGptLawMin--\hpGptLawMax\% on
gpt-oss and \hpQwenLawMin--\hpQwenLawMax\% on Qwen3, about twice the gain on host B for Qwen3 and four to six times
for gpt-oss: the per-machine split matters most where the link is slow. On the Ryzen, ours leads FreeToken by
\hpQwenFtMin--\hpQwenFtMax$\times$; with the fixed table it would trail at 12.5\%. On the Core i9 the first comparison
(job 085) was confounded: FreeToken's hybrid backend ran gpt-oss at \iNineHybridMin--\iNineHybridMax\,tok/s, below its
own offload backend (\iNineOffloadMin--\iNineOffloadMax), because its CPU executor spread 23 threads across the 8
performance and 16 efficiency cores and its calibration then sent two thirds of the misses over the slow link, the
same failure \cref{app:law} reports for llama.cpp; the 1.4--2.0$\times$ lead that gave us measured a thread
configuration. We reran it (job 088, a second rental of the same CPU model) with FreeToken's executor on 8 threads
pinned to the performance cores: its hybrid backend then runs \slHybGainMin--\slHybGainMax$\times$ faster than before
(\slHybLow, \slHybMid{} and \slHybHigh\,tok/s; its calibration now copies \slFetchFrac\% of misses), beating its own
offload backend at 11 and 25\% and tying it at 40\%, and ours leads its better configuration by \slFtLow, \slFtMid{}
and \slFtHigh$\times$ (lowest interval bound \slFtLoMin). Restricted to the CPU alone, FreeToken's 8-thread executor
decodes \slFzeroMid\,tok/s at every budget. llama.cpp on the same rental runs \slLlamaTeight\,tok/s with one thread
per performance core (\slLlamaErrTeight\% from the model's prediction) and \slLlamaTall{} with all 24 threads
(\slLlamaErrTall\%): the hybrid-core failure is a thread-count choice, and the model holds once the choice is right. The
model predicted llama.cpp on the Ryzen within \hpLlamaQwen\%. \Cref{fig:ratio} puts every same-machine comparison on one
axis.

\paragraph{Long outputs.} Our lead does not come from the first 256 tokens. On gpt-oss, on ten held-out hard
problems (AIME 2022, problems 11--15 of both exams) that neither system has finished reasoning about after 2{,}048
tokens (at most \hardAnsMax{} of 10 reach an answer), ours runs \hardAllA, \hardAllB{} and \hardAllC{} times FreeToken
at 11, 25 and 40\% over all 2{,}048 tokens, and \hardLateA, \hardLateB{} and \hardLateC{} over tokens 257--2{,}048
(job 083, host A); both engines stream one event per token. On Qwen3, over tokens 257--2{,}048 of ten held-out
MATH-500 problems, ours runs \longQwenA{}, \longQwenB{} and \longQwenC{} times FreeToken at 12.5, 25 and 43.75\%.
Both leads are larger than over the same runs' first 256 tokens. Turning off FreeToken's prefill overlap, which lends
it cache slots during each prompt, changes its overall speed by 1\% or less.

\paragraph{FreeToken's own headline model.} On FreeToken's own headline model,
Qwen3.6-35B-A3B in BF16 (256 experts of 6.3\,MB per layer, hybrid linear attention), the two systems are close: ours runs
\qsixFtA{} times FreeToken at 12.5\% and ties at 25 and 37.5\% (\qsixFtB, \qsixFtC), at \qsixLlA--\qsixLlC$\times$ llama.cpp (job 086,
host A). FreeToken as shipped, with its automatic backend and cache size, runs this model at \qsixAuto\,tok/s on that
machine, above the 77--83\,tok/s its paper reports on an RTX 5090, so our setup does not handicap it; FreeToken's
fetch cap and thread count were left at their defaults, while our FETCH table was swept. Our lead is therefore
model-dependent: largest with few large experts (gpt-oss, top-4 of 128), smallest with many small ones.

\paragraph{FreeToken tuned.} On an RTX 5090 with a Ryzen 9 9950X (job 098, one launch, configurations in a shuffled
order per cell) FreeToken ran in five settings per cell: hybrid with its calibrated fetch split (its default), hybrid
with the fetch cap fixed at one and at two, hybrid with 8 CPU threads instead of one per physical core, and offload.
Its best setting was the calibrated hybrid with 8 threads at three cells and offload at the other three (gpt-oss 25
and 40\%, Qwen3 43.75\%), at most \fttBestOverDefaultMax$\times$ its \cref{tab:headline} setting; the fixed caps were
never better than its own split by more than \fttCapBestMax\%. Ours over FreeToken's best: \fttGLow, \fttGMid{} and \fttGHigh{} on gpt-oss, \fttQLow, \fttQMid{} and
\fttQHigh{} on Qwen3, within \fttHostSDiffMax{} of host S's ratios at every cell. With every expert on the CPU, stock llama.cpp
decodes gpt-oss \fttCpuGpt$\times$ and Qwen3 \fttCpuQwen$\times$ as fast as FreeToken's CPU backend.

\paragraph{Other systems and outputs.} Two newer llama.cpp-based systems, Pipelined Sharding \citep{pipelinedsharding2026} and
leloch's expert cache \citep{leloch2026}, run gpt-oss-120b at 1.28--1.45$\times$ stock llama.cpp; ours ran 1.42--2.34$\times$
the better of them (job 077). KTransformers \citep{ktransformers} (kt-kernel 0.7.0, exact BF16 CPU experts, the same
number of experts statically on the GPU) ran Qwen3 in BF16 at \ktOverLlamaMin--\ktOverLlamaMax$\times$ stock
llama.cpp on an RTX 5090 with a Ryzen 9 9950X, and ours at \ktOursOverMin--\ktOursOverMax$\times$ it (job 078); both
static placements read the same CPU expert bytes from the same DRAM. Its published speeds rely on AMX Xeons and
INT4/INT8 CPU weights, which that host lacks and which change the model, so this is not its best setting. Teacher-forced on fixed text (\klStepsGpt{} steps per model) against stock llama.cpp with every weight in the
VRAM of an RTX PRO 6000 (job 090), our cache at 25\% has a mean KL divergence over the full vocabulary of
\klMeanGpt{} nats on gpt-oss and \klMeanQwen{} on Qwen3 (99.9th percentile \klNinetyNineGpt{} and \klNinetyNineQwen,
maximum \klMaxGpt{} and \klMaxQwen), picks the same next token on \klTopGpt\% and \klTopQwen\% of steps, and changes
the loss by \klNllGpt\% and \klNllQwen\%. Stock llama.cpp's own \texttt{--n-cpu-moe} placement diverges from the same
reference by as much (mean KL \klStockMeanGpt{} and \klStockMeanQwen, agreement \klStockTopGpt\% and \klStockTopQwen\%):
the differences are those of running experts on the CPU (the helpers quantise activations to Q8\_0 where the GPU path
uses Q8\_1), not of the cache. Task accuracy was not measured.

\paragraph{Prompts and batches.} The cache serves single-token steps; prompts and batches above one run llama.cpp's
stock graph with every expert in host memory, and pay for the cache's resident experts and its one-thread CPU side.
In \texttt{llama-batched-bench} at 25\% on host S (job 089, both models) our prefill at 512 and 2{,}048
tokens runs \bbPrefillMin--\bbPrefillMax\% of stock llama.cpp's and our decode at 2 and 4 parallel sequences
\bbBatchMin--\bbBatchMax\% of it (the tool feeds both engines the same random tokens).

\section{Artifact description}\label{app:artifact}
\begin{itemize}\setlength\itemsep{1pt}
\item \textbf{System.} A patch to llama.cpp commit \texttt{4da6337} (the expert cache, FETCH, the mailbox and the
\texttt{llama-ec-bench} instrumentation: teacher-forced loss, logit dumps, routing lookahead records); built with CUDA 13.0
for compute capability 12.0.
\item \textbf{Jobs.} One shell script per job; each installs FreeToken at commit \texttt{0d652e7}, downloads the
models from Hugging Face, runs the bandwidth probes, writes any on-machine prediction, runs the client, and stores raw
rows (one JSON line per problem), server logs and engine counters. A small runner rents a Vast instance, streams a
heartbeat, fetches the results and only then destroys the instance.
\item \textbf{Analysis.} \texttt{scripts/*\_stats.py} compute every interval from the raw rows;
\texttt{scripts/perlayer\_model.py}, \texttt{speed\_limit\_v2.py}, \texttt{shapley\_gap.py}, \texttt{policy\_study.py},
\texttt{foresight\_exact.py}, \texttt{batchk\_trace.py}, \texttt{fig\_audit\_sol.py} and \texttt{scorecard.py} produce the
model comparison, the limit, the accounting, the trace studies, the audit figure and the scorecard;
\texttt{scripts/wsg\_tables.py} and \texttt{wsg\_numbers2.py} generate every table, figure and number in this paper from
their outputs. The audit's \audAllN{} published rows and the physical speed-of-light model they are scored against are
\texttt{data/published\_measurements.json}, \texttt{data/audit/} and \texttt{scripts/audit.py}.
\item \textbf{Cost.} The \costRentals{} rentals (\costMachines{} distinct offers) of jobs 058--\costJobHi{} cost \costUsd{} US dollars at their hourly GPU prices, by the ledger in the repository; storage and download charges, which the ledger omits, add to this.
\end{itemize}
