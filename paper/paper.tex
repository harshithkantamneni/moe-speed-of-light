\title{Where the Seconds Go:\\[2pt]\large A Speed Limit and a Measured Accounting for Mixture-of-Experts Decode\\ on One Consumer GPU}
\author{Harshith Kantamneni\\Independent Researcher}
\date{}

\begin{document}
\maketitle


\begin{abstract}
A Mixture-of-Experts model too large for one consumer GPU keeps most experts in host memory. How fast could it
decode, and what is the rest of the time made of? Belady's optimum with bypass on the model's own routing, at
probed and datasheet rates, gives a speed limit in seconds: our llama.cpp expert cache reaches
\bothLimitPctMin--\bothLimitPctMax\% of it on two RTX 5090 hosts, and \audInclassN{} published measurements in its
class a median \audInclassMed\%. A Shapley accounting names routing foresight the largest missing piece where host
memory binds; oracles in the engine measure it. Spent as the optimum does, admitting what it admits and reading each
admitted expert once, foresight is worth \fsfGainMin--\fsfGainMax\% serialised and \fsOvlBestGainMin--\fsOvlBestGainMax\%
overlapped; spent the usual ways, by Belady prefetch or serve-then-copy, it reads
\fsExcessReadsOptMin--\fsExcessReadsOptMax$\times$ the optimum's bytes and looked worthless where host memory is
saturated. Nine models' traces give the horizon perfect foresight needs, $W_{50}\approx\fsPre\,(C/k)^{\fsExp}$
tokens, which speculative batching does not supply once rejected drafts are paid for.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}
Open-weight Mixture-of-Experts models now carry 30--120\,B parameters and activate a few billion per token; a desktop
GPU has 24--32\,GB. Running such a model at batch 1 therefore means keeping most experts in host DRAM, and each
decoded token then reads its routed experts from one of three places: GPU memory, host memory by the CPU that runs the
expert, or host memory across PCIe into a GPU slot~\cite{ivanov2021datamovement,hoefler2021sparsity}. A line of
systems has built on this: GPU expert caches with demand fetch~\cite{eliseev2023offloading,xue2024moeinfinity,song2024promoe},
CPU execution of misses~\cite{kamahori2024fiddler,ktransformers,zhong2025hybrimoe,huang2025cpugpu,daop2025,dali2026},
per-miss choices between the two~\cite{freetoken}, and expert prediction~\cite{hwang2024pregated,zhu2025preattention,wang2026seqmoe}.
Each reports a speed-up over a baseline of its choosing. None states how fast its machine \emph{could} decode the model
within the same GPU memory, or how much of the remaining time any design could remove, and the trace-level evaluation
contracts proposed for this field~\cite{zhang2026reproducible} count cache misses, not seconds.

This paper asks those two questions of a consumer machine and answers them in seconds. The answer to the first is a
ceiling: the fewest host bytes any exact-routing policy can read, from Belady's optimum with bypass on the model's own
routing, over the probed host rate and the GPU's datasheet rate. The answer to the second is an accounting of the gap
to that ceiling, first as a model and then as a measurement: we built oracles of future routing into the engine and
measured what foresight is worth under each way of spending it. Spent as the optimum spends it, admitting only what
MIN with bypass admits and reading each admitted expert from host memory once, foresight pays at every budget, and
most of what it is worth where host memory binds is the bytes it saves. Spent the way caches usually spend it, by a
Belady prefetch that admits experts the optimum would bypass, or by the serve-then-copy admission of caches with
CPU-executed misses, which reads an admitted expert twice, it costs \fsExcessReadsOptMin--\fsExcessReadsOptMax$\times$
the optimum's bytes and looked worthless at the lowest budgets, in our own first attempts included. We work on rented
RTX 5090, 4090
and 3090 hosts with desktop CPUs, with gpt-oss-120b~\cite{openai2025gptoss} and Qwen3-30B-A3B~\cite{yang2025qwen3},
routing traces of nine models, and \audAllN{} published measurements from \audSources{} sources, by the method of
Hoefler and Belli~\cite{hoefler2015benchmarking}: a performance model with bounds, statistically sound comparisons,
and every prediction committed to a public repository before the machine it concerns was started; \scHeldStrictPct\%
of \scScored{} scored clauses held under the scorecard's rule, which demands the interval wherever a timing is
involved (\cref{app:prereg}). One example runs through the paper:
gpt-oss-120b with 32 of each layer's 128 experts resident, a quarter of its expert bytes, on an RTX 5090.

\paragraph{Contributions.}
\begin{itemize}\setlength\itemsep{1pt}
\item \textbf{A speed limit in seconds} (\cref{sec:limit}), for any exact-routing system with $C$ expert slots per
layer, from the exact Belady optimum with bypass, two host read paths and measured ceilings, varied four ways and
stated for pooled caches too; and the \emph{host-memory law} that places an engine in its coordinates
(\cref{sec:law}): time per token as a GPU term plus host bytes over the probed rate of the paths that read them,
predicted blind on \lawHosts{} machines, re-scored with frozen constants on three more, and used by our cache to
choose, per machine, which misses to copy and which to run on the CPU.
\item \textbf{An accounting of the gap, predicted and then measured} (\cref{sec:gap}): a Shapley attribution over
five modelled fixes, and oracle policies in the engine that spend foresight four ways (fetched in the step, prefetched
ahead of it, both on a paced copy path, and as a Belady prefetch), measured on one host at six cells beside the
model's values: at the four host-bound cells \amBytesHostMin--\amBytesHostMax\% of the gap is the bytes foresight
saves, \amOverlapHostMin--\amOverlapHostMax\% the overlap it allows, and the \amRestHostMin--\amRestHostMax\% that
remains is named.
\item \textbf{What would recover it} (\cref{sec:traces}): across nine models' routing traces, which online policy
earns its reads, how fast the hot set drifts, how many tokens of foresight close half of the gap between a
single-read online policy and the optimum ($W_{50}\approx\fsPre\,(C/k)^{\fsExp}$), and why batched speculative
verification cannot supply them once rejected drafts are paid for.
\end{itemize}
The instrument is our llama.cpp expert cache (\cref{sec:setting}): per-layer slots, rationed admission, CPU helpers
the GPU signals, and demand copies split by the law; it runs \bothLlamaXMin--\bothLlamaXMax$\times$ stock llama.cpp
and leads FreeToken~\cite{freetoken} at \bothLeadCells{} of \bothCells{} cells on two RTX 5090 hosts.
Running missed experts on the CPU~\cite{kamahori2024fiddler,ktransformers}, caching hot experts with CPU-executed
misses~\cite{zhong2025hybrimoe,huang2025cpugpu,llamacppdisc24528}, splitting a layer's misses between CPU and PCIe
from a bandwidth model~\cite{freetoken,dali2026}, a PCIe turnover ceiling~\cite{wisp2026}, bytes over bandwidth as a
bound~\cite{budgetingbytes2026}, Belady with bypass as a cache-level oracle~\cite{zhang2026reproducible} and a table of
systems against the all-in-VRAM speed~\cite{wang2026seqmoe} are all published; our system uses the first three, and
what is new is the limit in seconds, the accounting and its measurement, the trace results, and the measurements.

\section{Setting and Instrument}\label{sec:setting}
\paragraph{Workload.} Single-request decode. gpt-oss-120b (MXFP4 experts; 36 MoE layers of 128 experts, top-4,
13.25\,MB per expert) and Qwen3-30B-A3B in BF16 (48 layers of 128 experts, top-8, 9.44\,MB per expert). Prompts are
the 30 AIME-25 problems in FreeToken's own benchmark format; each system decodes 256 tokens per problem, greedily,
after one warm-up request on a fixed held-out text, in one session in which the cache carries over between problems.
One client measures every system with FreeToken's own metric, (tokens$-$1) over the time between the first and last
token's arrival. A \emph{cell} is a model at a budget: the fraction of each layer's experts resident on the GPU
(11, 25 and 40\% for gpt-oss; 12.5, 25 and 43.75\% for Qwen3).

\paragraph{Machines and probes.} RTX 5090 (32\,GB, 1.79\,TB/s datasheet) hosts with desktop CPUs, rented by the hour,
each named here by a letter. \Cref{tab:headline} is measured on \emph{host B}, a Ryzen 9 9950X3D rental whose card
reports a \memClkHeadline\,MHz memory clock, \memClkRatio$\times$ the common clock (\memClkCommon\,MHz on
\memClkCommonN{} of the \memClkOtherRentals{} other RTX 5090 jobs that recorded one) and whose probe read device memory
3\% above the datasheet rate the limit assumes and about 9\% above the common-clock cards', and on \emph{host S}, a
Ryzen 9 9950X rental at the common clock whose CPU reads host memory \scCpuBwDrop\% slower (its card also has a lower
power limit, \scPowerW{} against \scPowerWB\,W, and a lower maximum SM clock, 3105 against 3420\,MHz). The ablation and the
long-output runs use \emph{host A}, a second rental of the 9950X3D model at the common clock; the oracle measurements
of \cref{sec:gap} use three further rentals, \emph{O1} (Ryzen 9 9950X), \emph{O2} (Ryzen 9 7950X) and \emph{O3}
(Ryzen 9 9950X), whose host memory is weaker than host B's. Each job first runs a probe (\texttt{concur}) that reads
host memory from $t$ CPU threads, over PCIe with zero-copy kernels and with the copy engine, and from both at once;
FreeToken's own probe (\texttt{ft bench bw}) runs too. We call the CPU rate at the helper thread count $B_c$, the
zero-copy PCIe rate $B_p$ and the combined rate $B_{cp}$, and the highest rate the probe reached by any method
$B_{\mathrm{host}}$. GPU clocks cannot be locked on these rentals; the cards' power limits are in the released probe
files (350--600\,W).

\paragraph{The instrument.} Our cache is a 3{,}500-line patch to llama.cpp. Each MoE layer has $C$ GPU slots.
Routing is decided on the GPU; resident experts run there, and each miss either runs on CPU helper threads, which the
GPU signals through a mailbox in mapped memory, or is copied into a slot and run on the GPU (FETCH). Admission
follows a decayed-frequency score (half-life 16 tokens, an admission margin $\kappa{=}1$; a llama.cpp pull request
admits by a decayed heat with hysteresis in the same spirit~\cite{llamacpppr26563}) and copies in the background, so an
admitted miss is read from host memory twice, once by the CPU that serves it and once by its copy. Sampling runs on
the GPU. The one per-machine decision, how many of a layer's $n$ missed experts to copy and how many to run on the
CPU, is a table $f(n)$ computed from the probe by the law of \cref{sec:law} when the job starts. The cache serves
single-token steps; prompts and batches above one run llama.cpp's stock graph with every expert in host memory
(\bbPrefillMin--\bbPrefillMax\% of stock llama.cpp's prefill speed, \bbBatchMin--\bbBatchMax\% of its decode at 2--4
sequences; \cref{app:system}).

\paragraph{Equal memory, statistics and protocol.} llama.cpp keeps whole layers' experts on the GPU
(\texttt{--n-cpu-moe}~$n$), our cache keeps $C$ experts of every layer, and FreeToken takes a cache rate; we match the
number of experts on the GPU (within 2.5\%). Measured VRAM on host B: ours 9.5--25.9\,GB and llama.cpp 9.5--25.3\,GB
across the budgets, FreeToken 28.4--28.6\,GB at every budget, because it preallocates. Ratios are of mean speeds, paired by problem,
with 95\% percentile intervals from 10{,}000 bootstrap resamples; they cover problem sampling within one launch on one
machine, not rentals (two rentals of one CPU model differed by 19--22\% in decode speed and read 62 against 47\,GB/s
of host bandwidth).
Where a system has variants (our FETCH tables, FreeToken's offload and hybrid backends), a first launch picks each
system's faster variant per budget and a second launch, which the comparison uses, runs only those (one row of
\cref{tab:headline} has no second launch; on host S FreeToken's variants were carried over from host B's selection
rather than re-picked). Every job's predictions are in the header of its script, committed and pushed before the
machine starts; \cref{app:prereg} scores every clause under one rule.

\paragraph{The instrument against its peers.} \Cref{tab:headline} compares the three systems at six cells on two
RTX 5090 hosts. On host B ours leads FreeToken at every budget, by \ftLeadGptMin--\ftLeadGptMax\% on gpt-oss and
\ftLeadQwenMin--\ftLeadQwenMax\% on Qwen3; the lead is largest when many experts miss and the CPU path carries them
(gpt-oss at 11--25\%), smallest at Qwen3 12.5\%, where our cache already reads host memory at \qlowBWpct\% of the
machine's combined rate, and at Qwen3 43.75\%, where few experts miss and FreeToken's fused GPU kernels count. On host
S, whose CPU reads \scCpuBwDrop\% slower (\scCpuBw{} against \scCpuBwB\,GB/s) behind the same link, ours leads at
\scOursLeadCells{} cells (gpt-oss \scLeadGptMin--\scLeadGptMax\%; Qwen3 12.5 and 25\% \scFtQLow{} and
\scFtQMid$\times$) and FreeToken leads at Qwen3 43.75\% (\scFtQHigh, interval \scFtLoQHigh--\scFtHiQHigh); every
ratio is \scRatioDropMin--\scRatioDropMax{} below host B's; with the CPU slower the law's tables copy more of each
layer's misses and the CPU path, which is our lead, is worth less, though the card's lower power limit and clocks
move the three systems too. The lead is mostly admission rationing: with
LRU in place of decayed frequency, everything else equal, the cache runs \scLruSlowMin--\scLruSlowMax\% slower across
the six cells of host S and trails FreeToken at \scLruTrailCells{} of them (the ablation, \cref{app:system}). On
FreeToken's own headline model, Qwen3.6-35B-A3B, ours leads by 3\% at one budget and ties at two; on an RTX 4090 and an RTX
3090 ours runs \grThreekLlamaMin--\grFourkLlamaMax$\times$ llama.cpp at the cells that fit 24\,GB, and on Mixtral-8x7B,
with 2 of 8 experts resident, it is within 2\% of llama.cpp, because there is little locality to earn (\cref{app:grid}).
Teacher-forced against an all-VRAM reference, the cache's output differs by a mean KL of \klMeanGpt{} and \klMeanQwen{}
nats over the full vocabulary (top-1 agreement \klTopGpt\% and \klTopQwen\%), the same divergence as stock llama.cpp's
own CPU placement (\cref{app:system}).

\section{A Host-Memory Law}\label{sec:law}
With experts partly in host memory, a decode step does two kinds of work: GPU work that every token needs (attention,
dense projections, resident experts, the output head, launches), and reads of the experts that are not resident. The
second is limited by host memory. The CPU reads $X_c$ bytes per token (the experts it runs), PCIe reads $X_p$ bytes
(the experts copied to the GPU, whether on demand or ahead of time), and when both run at once they share the DRAM:
\begin{equation}
T \;=\; G \;+\; \max\!\Big(\frac{X_c}{B_c},\ \frac{X_p}{B_p},\ \frac{X_c+X_p}{B_{cp}}\Big).
\label{eq:law}
\end{equation}
$X_c$ and $X_p$ come from the engine's counters of misses, fetches and admissions, which are counts, not timings.
$B_c$, $B_p$ and $B_{cp}$ come from the machine's probe. $G$ is one constant per engine and model, fitted once on one
host (4.82\,ms for our cache on gpt-oss-120b, 5.06\,ms for llama.cpp) and then frozen in the repository. The form is
FreeToken's residual-bandwidth split of one layer~\cite{freetoken}, lifted to the token; the difference is that the
three rates are probed rather than assumed to add, and that the law is tested as a predictor.

\paragraph{Tested as a predictor.} On every machine of the blind test the job wrote the law's prediction from its own
probe before any model ran (\cref{tab:law}; all of its cache rows are gpt-oss-120b on RTX 5090 hosts). Over
\lawCfg{} configurations on \lawHosts{} machines (\lawMeas{} measurements; one machine ran twice) the median error is
\lawMedian\% per configuration; without the 12-channel EPYC 9655 it is \lawDeskMedian\% over \lawDeskN{}
configurations (90th percentile \lawDeskPninety\%). llama.cpp \texttt{--n-cpu-moe} is predicted within 4\% on the
Ryzen hosts, including two Qwen3 configurations on host B (+3.6\%, +4.0\%; for Qwen3, $G{=}4.3$\,ms, the mean
residual of three llama.cpp runs on another host), and misses by \lawLlamaOffMin--\lawLlamaOffMax\% on the
hybrid-core and server hosts. With its constants frozen, the law scores the cache on the \lawFrozenHosts{} RTX 5090
hosts rented later (\lawFrozenN{} measurements at the six cells of \cref{tab:headline}, not written before the runs)
at a median \lawFrozenMed\%, 90th percentile \lawFrozenPninety\%, worst \lawFrozenMax\% (\lawFrozenWorstCell).
Refit under leave-one-host-out validation, the token-level max has a median error of \lohoMaxMed\% (90th percentile
\lohoMaxPninety\%), the additive form $G+X_c/B_c+X_p/B_p$ \lohoAddMed\% (\lohoAddPninety\%) and a per-layer max summed
over layers \lohoLayerMed\% (\lohoLayerPninety\%); the max beats the sum on the three rows that copy misses on
demand behind a 53\,GB/s link, where both paths carry most of the bytes, and on the EPYC 9655 rows, where both fail,
the sum is closer on the two rows that copy behind a slow link, and elsewhere the two are within a few points
(\cref{app:law}). Each failure has a
measured cause (\cref{app:law}): on-demand copies behind a slow link share DRAM less evenly than the probe's combined
rate says; llama.cpp splits every matrix evenly across threads and runs at 46\% of the law on a hybrid-core host; and
on a 12-channel EPYC host memory stops being the limit and a per-layer latency floor takes over. $G$
(\lawGRefit\,ms refit) exceeds the whole all-in-VRAM token (\vramMs\,ms) because it absorbs a shortfall the probe does
not see: the helpers read at \helperRateMin--\helperRateMax$\times$ the probe's time per expert, and a fitted rate
factor is collinear with $G$ at this sample size.

\paragraph{What the law says, and what it is for.} \Cref{eq:law} is a calibrated model of execution that reads host
memory in sequence with its GPU work, as our cache and llama.cpp do. For such an engine every mechanism acts only
through the bytes read from host memory per token and the number of read paths in use: a second path adds bandwidth on
every host of the blind test but the 12-channel EPYC ($B_{cp}$ against $\max(B_c,B_p)$: \secondMin--\secondMax\%
on its Ryzen hosts, \secondUltra\% on the Core Ultra, \secondEpyc\% on the Zen~2 EPYC; the RTX 3090 host's Core
i9-11900KF, where the two paths together read less than the CPU alone, is a second exception), and that is the whole
gain available to fetching or splitting misses between the CPU and PCIe. Prefetching never reduces the bytes; what
it can buy is overlap of the reads with the step, which the law does not price and \cref{sec:gap} measures (there
the law over-predicts overlapped execution by up to \fspLawErrHighMax\%). The law's job in the system is the split.
Its
per-layer form,
\begin{equation}
T(n,f)=\max\!\big(\tfrac{fS}{B_p}+g,\ [n{>}f](L_c+\tfrac{(n-f)S}{B_c}),\ [0{<}f{<}n]\tfrac{nS}{B_{cp}}\big),
\label{eq:split}
\end{equation}
with $g$ the GPU's expert work per layer (48\,$\mu$s for Qwen3, 37\,$\mu$s for gpt-oss, from profiles) and
$L_c{=}20\,\mu$s the hand-off, picks for each count $n$ of missed experts the number $f$ to copy with the smallest
$T$. On host B the law's table beats the fixed table we used before at every budget of both models, by
\lawGainMin--\lawGainMax\%; on hosts whose link we measured at 27--30\,GB/s instead of 53 it fetches less and gains
\hpGptLawMin--\hpGptLawMax\% on gpt-oss and \hpQwenLawMin--\hpQwenLawMax\% on Qwen3, the per-machine split mattering
most where the link is slow (\cref{app:system}). In the running example on host B the table is
\texttt{\scTableGptB} ($f(0..4)$): one missed expert runs on the CPU, hidden behind the GPU's other experts; from two
misses the law copies one. The lead over FreeToken at that cell, \ftRatioGptMid$\times$, is mostly the admission
policy (\scPolicyShareGptMin\% of it by the LRU ablation of \cref{app:system}), the rest the CPU path that FreeToken's
bandwidth model declines to use there.

\section{The Speed Limit}\label{sec:limit}
\paragraph{Bound.} Consider any system that executes the model's exact routing with at most $C$ experts of each layer
resident on the GPU. Per token, each of the $Lk$ routed experts is either served by a resident copy (the GPU reads it
from VRAM) or not, in which case its $S$ bytes are read from host memory once more: by the CPU that runs it, or by the
copy that brings it into a slot. Let $R^\star$ be the reads per token of Belady's MIN with bypass~\cite{belady1966study,hasslinger2018,bypassopt}
applied per layer to the routing trace, the fewest reads of any policy, even one that knows the future (an expert may
be evicted after serving the current token; a policy that keeps every requested expert until the token ends reads
\exactReadsMoreMin--\exactReadsMoreMax\% more). If $c$ experts per token run on the CPU, host memory supplies at least
$\max(R^\star,c)\,S$ bytes and the GPU reads the dense bytes $D$ plus $(Lk-c)\,S$. With everything overlapped,
\begin{equation}
\bar T \;\ge\; \min_{0\le c\le Lk}\ \max\!\Big(\frac{D+(Lk-c)S}{B_{\mathrm{gpu}}},\ \frac{\max(R^\star\!,c)\,S}{B_{\mathrm{host}}}\Big),
\label{eq:limit}
\end{equation}
with $B_{\mathrm{gpu}}$ the GPU's datasheet rate and $B_{\mathrm{host}}$ the highest host read rate the probe measured
(\bHost\,GB/s on host B). A cell is \emph{host-bound} when the minimum is at $c^\star{=}0$, the host term binding
with every expert the GPU can take, and \emph{GPU-bound} otherwise; on host B gpt-oss at 11 and 25\% and Qwen3 at
12.5 and 25\% are host-bound, the two largest budgets GPU-bound, balanced by running 13 and 30 experts per token on the
CPU. The bound's class: exact routing, whole experts of unmodified bytes, at most $C$ of each layer resident, one
token per forward pass. A system free to pool its $LC$ slots across layers has a looser bound,
\poolOverLayerMin--\poolOverLayerMax\% higher (\cref{tab:limit}); FreeToken keeps one LRU over all layers and
llama.cpp pins whole layers, so \cref{tab:headline} scores them against the pooled bound, and speculative decoders,
which verify several tokens per pass, lie outside it. The bound assumes that an expert run on the CPU is read from
DRAM (the optimum's shortest re-read distance is \lthreeReuseMin\,GB of host traffic, far beyond any CPU cache), that
computation runs at memory speed, and that the two read paths share $B_{\mathrm{host}}$ freely; it drops all
latencies and starts from an empty cache. $B_{\mathrm{host}}$ is the highest rate our probe reached, not a hardware
peak, so a system that reads host memory faster than the probe could exceed the limit (a second rental of the same
CPU probed 90.3\,GB/s), and host B's card, whose memory clock is 1.21$\times$ the datasheet's, read 1,847\,GB/s in our
probe against the 1,792 the bound assumes, which raises its GPU-bound limits by 3\%. The limit exceeds the all-in-VRAM
speed at the GPU-bound cells because it uses the datasheet rate, of which batch-1 kernels reach 52--61\%; with the
measured GPU rate (\cref{tab:limit}) it is 8--9\% above all-in-VRAM, the CPU's share. The traces are the models' own
greedy text on the 30 AIME-25 prompts, templated and tokenised exactly as the server does, with routing recorded
teacher-forced by our llama.cpp instrumentation; replayed through a simulator of our deployed policy they give hit
rates within \simHitGptMax{} points of the engine's own counters on gpt-oss and within
\simHitQwenMin--\simHitQwenMax{} points on Qwen3.

\paragraph{Varying it, and applying it.} \Cref{tab:limit} changes one assumption at a time. A single pool of
$LC$ slots shared across layers, the true equal-memory constraint, needs \globalReadsMin--\globalReadsMax\% fewer
reads than per-layer slots and raises the four host-bound limits by 5--10\%; it leaves the two largest budgets
unchanged, where the limit is the machine's aggregate bandwidth and read counts no longer matter. Starting the
optimum warm, after \warmSteps{} tokens, changes it by at most 1.4\%. $B_{\mathrm{host}}$ is one probe sample; the
six concurrent samples on host B read \bHostMed\,GB/s at the median, and the median lowers the host-bound limits by
8\%. The GPU term is the one that moves most: at batch 1 the all-in-VRAM runs reach only \gpuEffPctGpt\% (gpt-oss) and
\gpuEffPctQwen\% (Qwen3) of the datasheet rate, and with the measured rate the limit at 25\% and above collapses to
one aggregate line, \limMeasGpuGpt{} and \limMeasGpuQwen\,tok/s. A per-layer sum, $\sum_l\max(\text{GPU}_l,\text{host}_l)$
with the optimum's reads per layer, which no system without cross-layer prefetch can beat, sits
\twoFormsMin--\twoFormsMax\% of a measured token below the token-level maximum. Against the limit as published our
cache stands at \bothLimitPctMin--\bothLimitPctMax\% on the two hosts of \cref{tab:headline}, FreeToken at
\ftPoolPctMin--\ftPoolPctMax\% and llama.cpp at \llPoolPctMin--\llPoolPctMax\% of the pooled bound their designs
allow; with every tightening at once on host B, ours at \limAllPctMin--\limAllPctMax\%. In the running example the
limit on host B is \limExMidG\,tok/s (\limMeasGpuGpt{} with the measured GPU rate) and the cache runs 109\,tok/s, a
quarter of it. Even with every weight in VRAM, our cache with every expert in its slots matches or beats stock
llama.cpp (\cOneTwoEight$\times$ and \cOneTwoEightQ$\times$): the cache costs nothing when the model fits.

\paragraph{Published systems against their own limit.} Rule 11 of scientific benchmarking asks for an upper bound
beside every measurement~\cite{hoefler2015benchmarking}. We applied a bound with \cref{eq:limit}'s ingredients
(Belady's optimum with bypass per layer, the CPU and PCIe paths, no latencies) in a per-layer form that charges dense
work in sequence with the expert reads, which is tighter than \cref{eq:limit} (\texttt{scripts/audit.py}), with each
paper's stated hardware at datasheet rates and a routing trace of its model where we have one, to \audAllN{}
published batch-1 measurements from \audSources{} sources (\cref{app:audit} lists every row with its source and
plots them). Inside the bound's class, exact routing at one token per pass, the \audInclassN{} trace-scored
measurements reach a median \audInclassMed\% of their own speed of light (quartiles
\audInclassQone--\audInclassQthree\%; \audInclassBelowFifth{} of them below a fifth); the \audSpecN{} speculative
and \audLossyN{} lossy rows, scored against a bound that is not theirs, sit lower (all \audTraceN{} trace-scored rows,
\audTraceMed\%), the \audIidN{} rows that needed an i.i.d. routing assumption higher (\audIidMed\%, an overestimate),
and all \audAllN{} rows at \audAllMed\%. Three caveats bound what this says. The ceiling takes the host rate from the
datasheet where ours takes it from the probe; for \audImputedN{} rows the per-layer capacity is an upper bound
imputed from the card's memory, which loosens their bound; and the \audAllfitN{} rows with every expert resident sit
at a median \audAllfitMed\% with nothing offloaded, so for small models at batch 1 the dropped latencies are not
small. Even so, the in-class measurements sit at a median seventh, and three quarters of them below a quarter, of a
latency-free datasheet ceiling, and the next section says what the missing part is made of.

\paragraph{The accounting, as a model.} Between the measured time of a cell and its limit lie five modelled fixes:
overlap of host reads with GPU work (in \cref{eq:limit}'s form, which also rebalances experts onto the CPU), the
optimum's reads instead of the online policy's (\emph{foresight}), the best online policy's reads at the probe's
highest rate instead of the engine's through \cref{eq:law} (policy and read paths), the engine's per-token host work,
and GPU kernels at the datasheet rate. Adding them in a fixed order makes the first one added look largest, so we
report each fix's Shapley value, its average marginal cost over every order (\cref{fig:shapley}), a method with a
precedent in performance debugging of microservices~\cite{shapleyiq2024}. Two of the five terms are definitions
rather than measurements: the GPU term is the residual, its factor calibrated per cell so that the model with no fix
applied reproduces the measured time, and ``overlap'' is \cref{eq:limit}'s form. On host B the model has two regimes.
In the four host-bound cells missing foresight is the largest cost, \shFsHostMin--\shFsHostMax\% of the gap
(\shFsHostTokMin--\shFsHostTokMax\% of the token; in the running example \shFsGMid\%, where it edges overlap by
\shTieMs\,ms and is the largest marginal cost in \shOrdersGMid{} of the \shOrders{} orders); in the two GPU-bound cells
host reads serialised with GPU work is largest, \shOvlGpuMin--\shOvlGpuMax\% of the gap, and foresight second at
\shFsGpuMin\%. Policy and read paths cost \shPolMin--\shPolMax\%, per-token host work \shHostMin--\shHostMax\%, and
GPU kernels below datasheet, net of the overlap the engine achieves, \shGpuMin--\shGpuMax\%. The regimes are a
property of the host: recomputed for the three oracle hosts, whose host memory is weaker, foresight edges overlap at
three of their six GPU-bound cells. The model prices foresight and overlap as nearly independent players, and the
interior states it values are not realised by any online policy. The rest of this section measures them.

\paragraph{Foresight, measured.} An oracle policy reads the routing of the rest of the sequence from a trace recorded
on the same machine over the same teacher-forced text, and the engine runs it at every cell beside the online policy
(\cref{fig:factorial}; \cref{tab:single_read}, \cref{app:foresight}). The policies differ in how they use the
foresight. \emph{fetch} is the limit's own policy, MIN with bypass, with its admissions served the way the bound
counts them: a miss whose next use comes before the furthest next use among the residents is fetched into the victim's
slot in the step that needs it and read from host memory once; every other miss runs on the CPU. It overlaps nothing
across steps, so it is the model's foresight-only state $v(F)$ realised. \emph{fetch + prefetch} adds the overlap
that foresight makes possible: an expert whose first use is at least two steps away is copied ahead of time into the
slot of the resident whose next use is further off, so that the copy lands before the use and the expert is still read
once; \emph{paced} runs the same prefetch on the engine's paced copy path (16\,MB pieces) with a three-step lead.
\emph{hit-optimal} is the policy a prefetching cache would seem to want and the one our first oracle job ran: Belady
within the window, admitting every expert the coming steps will route to, soonest first, through the background
admission path (here unpaced), for one future use as readily as for ten, so it admits what the optimum bypasses and
re-copies what it evicts; a miss that is served on the CPU before its copy lands is read twice. The host, O3, reads \fsfHostCpu\,GB/s by the CPU, \fsfHostLink{} over the link and \fsfHostBoth{} with both;
its limits are \fsLimitGLow--\fsLimitGHigh\,tok/s on gpt-oss and \fsLimitQLow--\fsLimitQHigh{} on Qwen3, and the
online policy stands at \fsFracBaseGHigh--\fsFracBaseQLow\% of them.

With each admitted expert read once, foresight pays at every cell. The fetch oracle reads
\fsfReadsOptMin--\fsfReadsOptMax$\times$ the optimum's $R^\star$ (in the running example \fsfReadsGMid{} experts per
token against the online policy's \fsReadsBaseGMid{} and $R^\star{=}$\fsReadsOptGMid), hits within \fsfSimHitDiffMax{} points of what
the trace predicts for it, and runs \fsfRatioGLow, \fsfRatioGMid{} and \fsfRatioGHigh$\times$ the online policy on
gpt-oss and \fsfRatioQLow, \fsfRatioQMid{} and \fsfRatioQHigh$\times$ on Qwen3 (every interval within $\pm$0.02),
\fsfFracMin--\fsfFracMax\% of the limit and \fsfHostRecovMin--\fsfHostRecovMax\% of the gain the model's $v(F)$
predicts at the host-bound cells. The law on its own counters, with $G$ calibrated on the online run, under-predicts
its time by \fsfLawAbsMax\% at most (\fsfLawErrMin{} to \fsfLawErrMax\%): a fetched expert must land before its
layer's experts run, a per-layer latency the law, which charges bytes at the probed rate, does not price; that
latency is the rest of $v(F)$. Given the same foresight two to three steps ahead, the prefetch adds the overlap: fetch + prefetch
runs \fsbtRatioGptMin--\fsbtRatioGptMax$\times$ the online policy on gpt-oss and \fsbtRatioQwenMin--\fsbtRatioQwenMax$\times$
on Qwen3, and paced \fsbpRatioGptMin--\fsbpRatioGptMax$\times$ and \fsbpRatioQwenMin--\fsbpRatioQwenMax$\times$,
\fsbpFracMin--\fsbpFracMax\% of the limit, \fsbpHostClosedMin--\fsbpHostClosedMax\% of the gap closed at the
host-bound cells (in the running example \fsbpRatioGMid$\times$, \fsbpFracGMid\% of the limit, \fsbpClosedGMid\% of
the gap). The hit-optimal oracle on the same host is slower than every prefetching single-read variant at every cell
and than the fetch oracle at the four host-bound cells (it ties fetch within a point at the two GPU-bound ones), runs
\fshRatioGMid$\times$ in the running example, and \emph{loses} at the two lowest budgets (\fshRatioGLow$\times$ at
gpt-oss 11\%, \fshRatioQLow$\times$ at Qwen3 12.5\%): its admissions alone are
\fshAdmitsOptMin--\fshAdmitsOptMax$\times$ $R^\star$, its reads \fshReadsOptMin--\fshReadsOptMax$\times$, and it
drives host memory at \fshHostUtilMin--\fshHostUtilMax\% of the probe's best rate at the host-bound cells, where the
online policy already runs at \fsBaseHostUtilMin--\fsBaseHostUtilMax\%. Our first two oracle jobs, on hosts O1 and
O2, had spent foresight only in the usual ways, as this Belady prefetch (paced there) and as MIN with bypass whose
admissions are copied after the CPU has served them (two reads per admitted expert), and found the Belady prefetch
worth \fsHitGainMin--\fsHitGainMax\% and the serve-then-copy policy \fsbGainHighMin--\fsbGainHighMax\% at the four
cells of 25\% and above, and at most \fsbGainLowMaxAny\% or a loss at the two lowest (\cref{app:foresight}). Both read
\fsExcessReadsOptMin--\fsExcessReadsOptMax$\times$ $R^\star$: the Belady prefetch's host traffic sat at
\fsmUtilWinMin--\fsmUtilWinMax\% of the probe's best rate at every window on O1, so its speed at the host-bound cells
was $R^\star$ over its reads times that utilisation, \fsmHostFracMin--\fsmHostFracMax\% of the limit with the whole
sequence in view; and the bytes-optimal one read \fsbLowReadsVsBaseMin--\fsbLowReadsVsBaseMax\% \emph{more} than
the online policy at the two lowest budgets, where it gained \fsbGainLowMin--\fsbGainLowMax\%. The excess reads, not
the foresight, were what made foresight look worthless where host memory is saturated: admitting what the optimum
bypasses, and reading an admitted expert twice, which is the admission path of every cache that serves misses on the
CPU and copies them afterwards, ours included.

\paragraph{The accounting, measured.} The four states online, fetch, fetch + prefetch and limit decompose the gap on
host O3 at the host-bound cells into the bytes foresight saves (online to fetch): \amBytesHostMin--\amBytesHostMax\%
of the gap; the overlap foresight allows (fetch to the faster of the two prefetch states):
\amOverlapHostMin--\amOverlapHostMax\%; and the rest, \amRestHostMin--\amRestHostMax\% (\cref{tab:accounting_measured};
in the running example \amBytesGMid, \amOverlapGMid{} and \amRestGMid\%). The model's shares for the same host are
\amModelFHostMin--\amModelFHostMax, \amModelOHostMin--\amModelOHostMax{} and \amModelRestHostMin--\amModelRestHostMax\%:
it over-prices the bytes \amBytesMultMin--\amBytesMultMax$\times$ and the overlap \amOverlapMultMin--\amOverlapMultMax$\times$,
and under-prices what remains \amRestMultMin--\amRestMultMax$\times$, which is three things the oracles leave in
place. The order matters less than it did for the model: taken overlap first, with the Belady prefetch standing for
overlap at about the online policy's bytes, the overlap is worth \amOvlFirstGMid{} and \amOvlFirstQMid\% of the gap
at the two 25\% cells and the bytes given it \amBytesGivenOvlGMid{} and \amBytesGivenOvlQMid\% (at the two lowest
budgets that prefetch loses, so the order cannot be taken), though the paced Belady prefetch, which gained more on
O1 and O2, was not run on O3. The prefetch is imprecise: its reads are \fsbpReadsOptMin--\fsbpReadsOptMax$\times$ $R^\star$, the cost of
choosing victims two to three steps early and of the misses that fall inside the lead. The CPU misses that remain are
served in sequence with the step: the law over-predicts the prefetching states by \fsbpLawErrMin{} to
\fsbpLawErrMax\%, least at the lowest budgets, where \fsbpCpuMissesGLow{} and \fsbpCpuMissesQLow{} misses per token stay on the CPU. And the GPU work runs
at \gpuEffPctGpt--\gpuEffPctQwen\% of the datasheet rate the limit counts, which is most of the remaining gap at the
GPU-bound cells: on Qwen3 at 43.75\% on host A, with our earlier fixed FETCH table, Nsight Systems splits a token of
our cache (FreeToken) into 4.93
(5.20)\,ms of expert copies over PCIe, 2.29 (2.21) of resident-expert products, 1.65 (1.34) of attention
projections, 0.49 (0.37) of attention, 0.38 of output head and 0.97 (0.40) of norms, small kernels and cache
control, with our host-side time at 0.27\,ms; neither system overlaps its PCIe copies with GPU compute there, which
would save 5--10\%. Two things the model prices as overlap are worth nothing in this engine: turning off its
within-layer overlap of a layer's CPU misses with its GPU hits changes speed by under 1\% at every cell, and
predicting the next layer's experts, the usual form of prediction~\cite{hwang2024pregated,zhu2025preattention}, never
lowers host bytes, which is what we measured when we built it. Foresight across tokens is the lever, and the first
oracle job measured its horizon: half of the Belady prefetch's speed gain at gpt-oss 25\% arrived between windows of
4 and 16 tokens (\fsmWfiftyGMid{} by log interpolation), a horizon of the same order as the \fsGptQuarter{} tokens at
which, in the next section, single-read Belady on its trace closes half of the read gap.

\section{What the Traces Say}\label{sec:traces}
The accounting names foresight across tokens as the largest cost where the host binds, and the engine confirms it.
What would recover it, and what would not? We replay the models' own sampled text for \fsModels{} MoE
models~\cite{muennighoff2024olmoe,yang2025qwen3,openai2025gptoss} (\polModels{} traces of 33{,}000--152{,}000 tokens,
the cache carried across conversations) through an event-atomic simulator: each token requests its $k$ experts per
layer as one set, and no expert of the current token is evicted to serve it, the accounting that cache-level studies
of this problem call for~\cite{zhang2026reproducible}.

\paragraph{Policies.} \Cref{tab:policy} scores LRU, LFU, decayed frequency, ARC, S3-FIFO and a profiled static cache
against the exact optimum and against Belady within a window of $W$ future tokens, at three budgets per model. Three
things hold across models. First, the online policies are close to one another and far from the optimum: the best of
them reads \polBestOnlineMin--\polBestOnlineMax\% more than it, while decayed frequency is the best online policy
in \polDfBest{} of \polCells{} cells and S3-FIFO in \polSsfBest{} (within 1\% of the best in \polDfNear{} and
\polSsfNear{}). Where the cache turns over every token ($C/k\le1.5$, \polLruLowCells{} cells) the six online policies
are within \polLowSpreadMax\% of one another; from $C/k{=}2$ LRU reads \polLruMoreMin--\polLruMoreMax\% more than
decayed frequency on eight of nine models, Qwen2-57B being the exception, and classic LFU fails at large budgets
(\polLfuMax$\times$ the optimum). The literature's disagreement on recency against
frequency~\cite{si2026expertcache,flashmoe2026,mira2026,specmd} is consistent with this regime dependence. Second, a
static cache profiled on other text reads \polStaticMin--\polStaticMax$\times$ the optimum, because the hot set
moves: the $C$ most requested experts of a layer change by \driftHundredMin--\driftHundredMax\% between consecutive
windows of 100 tokens and \driftThousandMin--\driftThousandMax\% between windows of 1{,}000 (median
\driftThousandMed\%), so popularity follows the conversation; the skew is mild, the top quarter of experts taking
\skewQuarterMin--\skewQuarterMax\% of requests. Third, four tokens of foresight beat every online policy in every cell
(median \polWfourEquarter$\times$ the optimum at $E/4$), so foresight is worth several times the choice of policy.
Our deployed hysteresis costs \polKappaMin--\polKappaMax\% reads over plain decayed frequency on eight models and
up to \polKappaQwenMax\% on Qwen3; it was tuned for time, where it rations copies, not for reads.

\paragraph{How far ahead.} The optimum reads \fsSaveMin--\fsSaveMax\% fewer experts from host memory than decayed
frequency with each admitted miss fetched once, the single-read form of our policy (median \fsSaveMed\%; against
the deployed serve-then-copy form with hysteresis, \cref{tab:policy}, the saving is larger). The lookahead needed scales with the cache's turnover time $C/k$, slots per layer over
experts per token: half of the gap closes at $W_{50}\approx\fsPre\,(C/k)^{\fsExp}$ tokens ($r{=}\fsR$ in log--log over
\fsFitN{} points; the exponent's 95\% interval over a bootstrap of models is \fsExpLo--\fsExpHi, its leave-one-model-out
range \fsExpLomoMin--\fsExpLomoMax, and without the \fsInterp{} points whose $W_{50}$ lies below one token it is
\fsExpNoInterp), and 90\% needs a median of \fsNinety$\,C/k$. Small caches ($C/k\le2$) recover half of the gap with
one or two tokens of foresight; gpt-oss-120b at 25\% ($C/k{=}8$), the running example, needs about \fsGptQuarter{}
tokens, and at 50\% about \fsGptHalf{} (both measured on its trace; the fit gives 9 and 23). A prefetching system
needs that horizon plus the lead of its copy path, two to three steps in ours. The law adds the condition: fewer
reads are worth time only where host memory is the binding term, and a system that streams every expert through a
saturated bus each token, as on the devices of~\cite{wisp2026,budgetingbytes2026}, has no slots to fill and nothing
to foresee.

\paragraph{Speculation is not foresight.} Speculative decoding verifies $K$ draft tokens in one forward pass, so a
layer's experts for $K$ positions are requested together and a missed expert is read once for all of them, which
several systems propose as the source of foresight for a cache~\cite{spmoe,moespac2026,cascade2025}. On the same
traces, with $K$ positions per step and drafts accepted at rate $\alpha$ until the first rejection, batched
verification saves an online cache a median \bkFreeMin--\bkFreeMax\% of its reads across models at $K{=}8$ when
rejected drafts cost nothing (\bkFreeModelMax\% on the model that gains most), the equivalent of a window of two to
four tokens; once the rejected drafts' experts are counted, the saving
is \bkSameNineMin--\bkSameNineMax\% at $\alpha{=}0.9$ and becomes a loss of \bkSameEightLossMin--\bkSameEightLossMax\% at
$\alpha{=}0.8$, because the union of $K$ positions' expert sets is \bkUnionMin--\bkUnionMax\% of $Kk$ across models, budgets and
variants, several times $C$ (\cref{app:prereg} lists the variants). The optimum gains little from batching (\bkBeladyMin{} to
\bkBeladyMax\%). Foresight must come from knowing future routing, as a router decoupled from the
backbone~\cite{readme2024} or a forecaster~\cite{wang2026seqmoe} would provide, and $W_{50}$ says how far ahead.

\section{Related Work}\label{sec:related}
\paragraph{Offloading systems.} Demand-paging systems cache experts per layer and copy misses over
PCIe~\cite{eliseev2023offloading,xue2024moeinfinity,song2024promoe,tang2024hobbit,pagingexperts2026}. CPU-execution systems run
non-resident experts on the host~\cite{kamahori2024fiddler,ktransformers,zhong2025hybrimoe,daop2025,wang2026cloudgrade};
a set-associative GPU cache with CPU-executed misses~\cite{huang2025cpugpu}, a llama.cpp fork~\cite{leloch2026,llamacppdisc24528}
and two llama.cpp pull requests~\cite{llamacpppr26563,llamacpppr27861} are the closest designs to ours, and
FreeToken~\cite{freetoken} and DALI~\cite{dali2026} split each layer's misses between CPU and PCIe, FreeToken from a
closed-form bandwidth model. Pipelined Sharding~\cite{pipelinedsharding2026} plans sub-layer placement under a VRAM
budget. Predictors guess upcoming experts from the previous layer's state~\cite{hwang2024pregated,zhu2025preattention},
several steps ahead with a learned forecaster~\cite{wang2026seqmoe,mira2026}, or a batch's routes across
layers~\cite{expertflow}; Read-ME~\cite{readme2024} makes routing known in advance by decoupling the router and then
caches in Belady's manner. Speculative decoding has been combined with expert offloading as a source of
batching~\cite{spmoe,moespac2026,cascade2025}. Batched-throughput systems~\cite{flexgen2023,cao2024moelightning,yuan2025moelens}
and SSD tiers~\cite{ssdllama,edgezero} are outside our single-request, host-DRAM scope.

\paragraph{Models, bounds and audits.} FreeToken's split and our \cref{eq:split} are bandwidth models of one layer;
\cref{eq:law} is a whole-token model tested blind across machines and against alternatives. Max-of-paths cost models
go back to FlexGen~\cite{flexgen2023} and MoE-Lightning~\cite{cao2024moelightning}; WiSP~\cite{wisp2026} bounds decode by
PCIe turnover, Budgeting Bytes~\cite{budgetingbytes2026} by bytes over bandwidth, and MoE-CAP~\cite{moecap} measures
bandwidth utilisation against a single peak. Qiu et al.~\cite{qiu2026gguf} predict llama.cpp throughput from GGUF
metadata for models held entirely in GPU or unified memory; Suram~\cite{cacheable} matches one offloaded configuration
to bytes per token over bandwidth; Si et al.~\cite{si2026expertcache} derive when a copy pays off and find recency
competitive with frequency at large budgets. The roofline~\cite{williams2009roofline} is the single-memory ancestor of
our bound. Zhang~\cite{zhang2026reproducible} proposes a trace-level evaluation contract for expert caching, decomposes
the online-to-optimal gap in cache blocks in one fixed order, and audits the reporting of ten papers; SeqMoE~\cite{wang2026seqmoe}
reports four systems against the all-in-VRAM speed. Trace studies of policies cover two or three models
each~\cite{zhang2026reproducible,si2026expertcache,flashmoe2026,specmd}. Shapley attribution of a performance gap has a
precedent in ShapleyIQ~\cite{shapleyiq2024}. The choice between prefetching what the optimum would bypass and
admitting conservatively is the integrated prefetching-and-caching trade-off of Cao et al.~\cite{cao1995prefetching},
which our oracles re-derive in this setting. Limited lookahead in paging goes back to Albers~\cite{albers1997lookahead};
Hasslinger et al.~\cite{hasslinger2018} combine Belady within a window with LRU or LFU beyond it for web caches, the
policy family we use to measure the horizon.

\section{Limitations}\label{sec:limits}
\begin{itemize}\setlength\itemsep{1pt}
\item \textbf{Hardware.} Consumer cards (RTX 5090 for the headline comparisons, a 4090 and a 3090 at the cells that
fit 24\,GB; an RTX PRO 6000 for all-in-VRAM runs) and desktop Ryzen and Intel hosts, rented; GPU clocks could not be
locked, power limits differed (350--600\,W), and host B's card reads device memory 3\% above the datasheet rate,
where the common-clock cards read below it; \cref{tab:headline} therefore reports host S beside it, where our lead is \scRatioDropMin--\scRatioDropMax{}
smaller and FreeToken leads at one cell. A 12-channel server CPU breaks the law's bandwidth assumption
(\cref{sec:law}). Intervals cover problem sampling on one machine, not machine-to-machine variance, which
\cref{fig:ratio} shows across \ratioHostsN{} hosts; the oracle factorial ran on one host.
\item \textbf{Workload.} Single-request decode; AIME prompts, which were also used while developing the system;
greedy decoding; prompts of 52--879 tokens (median 130 for Qwen3, 186 for gpt-oss). Long outputs are measured on held-out problems. Prompts and batches above
one bypass the cache and run \bbPrefillMin--\bbPrefillMax\% and \bbBatchMin--\bbBatchMax\% of stock llama.cpp's
speed; speculative verification bypasses it and is not measured.
\item \textbf{Competitor settings.} FreeToken ran its faster backend per budget with its own bandwidth calibration,
default thread count and fetch cap, while our FETCH table was swept; its CPU backend failed on a hybrid-core host at
its default thread count and was rerun on the performance cores. Each comparison uses one confirmation launch,
except Qwen3 at 25\%, which is from the selection launch for both systems.
\item \textbf{The bound, the accounting and the audit.} Exact routing only: expert skipping, substitution or
deferral change the model and fall outside it; a per-layer budget, which a pooled cache can beat by 5--10\%. Two of
the accounting's five terms are definitions (\cref{sec:gap}). The oracles read a recorded trace, not a predictor; they
measure what this engine does with foresight, and their prefetch still reads \fsbpReadsOptMin--\fsbpReadsOptMax$\times$
the optimum's bytes. The published rows use datasheet ceilings and, for eleven of them, i.i.d. routing.
\item \textbf{Constants and outputs.} $G$, $g$ and $L_c$ are fitted once and frozen; the per-layer model overstates
differences between FETCH tables about 1.8$\times$. Output parity is measured by teacher-forced KL divergence,
agreement and loss against an all-VRAM reference, not by task accuracy.
\end{itemize}

\section{Conclusion}
On a consumer machine, the optimum's host bytes over the probed host rate and the datasheet GPU rate give a speed
limit in seconds; ours reaches a quarter to two fifths of it, and published systems in its class a median seventh
of a datasheet version. A host-memory law,
predicted blind and tested against its alternatives, places an engine in the limit's coordinates and tells it which
path to use for a miss on a given machine. Where the host binds, the largest piece of the gap is the policy's
ignorance of future routing, and in the engine that piece is recovered only when foresight is spent as the optimum
spends it, admitting what MIN with bypass admits and reading each admitted expert once: so spent, it runs our cache
\fsfGainMin--\fsfGainMax\% faster on the bytes alone and \fsOvlBestGainMin--\fsOvlBestGainMax\% faster with the
overlap it allows, at every budget; spent as a Belady prefetch or through the serve-then-copy admission of its first
oracles, it read \fsExcessReadsOptMin--\fsExcessReadsOptMax$\times$ the optimum's bytes and gained at most
\fsbGainLowMaxAny\%, or lost, at the lowest budgets. What remains is the prefetch's own excess reads, CPU misses
served in sequence, and GPU kernels at half the datasheet rate. Nine models'
traces say that neither an online policy nor speculative batching buys that foresight; it must come from knowing the
routing a few to tens of tokens ahead, and $W_{50}$ says how many.
