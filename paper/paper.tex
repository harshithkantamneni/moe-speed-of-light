\begin{abstract}
A Mixture-of-Experts model too large for one consumer GPU keeps most of its experts in host memory, and decoding one
token at a time then waits on host reads. We bound the time per token by the fewest host reads any policy can make
with $C$ GPU slots per layer (Belady's MIN with bypass) over the machine's measured read rate; MIN's own reads,
replayed layer by layer without the model, reach at least \jdLayerMin\% of this read time, yet our llama.cpp expert
cache, faster than FreeToken at \bothLeadCells{} of \bothCells{} configurations, stands at
\bothLimitPctMin--\bothLimitPctMax\% of the bound. Building oracles of the future routing into the engine, with each
rented RTX 5090 host as a sample, we find that foresight pays when it is spent as MIN spends it, caching MIN's set and
reading each expert once, and that which of MIN's many hit-optimal sets is used matters: the greedy one loses on a
machine with a slow link, where the set with the fewest admissions gains. An order-free accounting shows the two
choices interact: together they close up to \fsBalBothMax\% of the gap to the bound, either alone at most
\fsBalAloneMax\%. Degrading the oracle prices foresight: across nine models, half of MIN's saving needs the routing of
about \dcMed$\,C$ distinct experts per layer ahead, a horizon no online policy, learned admission order, speculative
batch or forecaster we tested approaches.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}
Open-weight Mixture-of-Experts (MoE) models now carry 30--120\,B parameters and activate a few billion per token; a
desktop GPU has 24--32\,GB. Running such a model for one user therefore means keeping most experts in host DRAM. Each
decoded token reads its routed experts from GPU memory if they are resident, and otherwise from host memory: either the
CPU runs the expert, or the expert is copied over PCIe into a GPU slot \citep{ivanov2021datamovement,hoefler2021sparsity}.
Many systems build on this: GPU expert caches with demand fetch \citep{eliseev2023offloading,xue2024moeinfinity,song2024promoe},
CPU execution of misses \citep{kamahori2024fiddler,ktransformers,zhong2025hybrimoe,huang2025cpugpu,daop2025,wang2026cloudgrade},
a per-miss choice between the two \citep{freetoken,dali2026}, and expert prediction \citep{hwang2024pregated,zhu2025preattention,wang2026seqmoe}.
Each reports a speed-up over a baseline of its choosing. None states how fast the same machine \emph{could} decode the
model within the same GPU memory, or what the rest of the time is made of.

We answer both questions for batch-1 decode of gpt-oss-120b \citep{openai2025gptoss} and Qwen3-30B-A3B \citep{yang2025qwen3}
on rented RTX 5090 hosts, with three findings.

\textbf{(1) Where host memory binds, time is host bytes, and the bound is nearly reachable} (\cref{sec:limit}). The
fewest host reads any exact-routing policy can make, over the machine's measured read rate, bounds the time per token.
Replayed layer by layer without the model, MIN's reads reach \jdLayerMin--\jdLayerMax\% of that read time on three
hosts, and requiring every admission to cross the link raises the bound by at most \laOverMidMax\%. Our cache reaches
\bothLimitPctMin--\bothLimitPctMax\% of the bound; by one procedure, published systems in the same class reach a median
of \audInclassMed\%.

\textbf{(2) Foresight pays when spent as MIN spends it, and which MIN matters} (\cref{sec:gap}). With the future
routing known, caching what MIN caches and copying each expert once, in the step, is
\fxFetchAllGainMin--\fxFetchAllGainMax\% faster than our deployed cache on three hosts at six budgets. MIN has many
hit-optimal schedules: the greedy one admits so eagerly that on a machine whose link reads at \jfPfRatio{} of its CPU
rate it loses (\jfPfFetch$\times$, on three launches), while the one with the fewest admissions gains there
(\jfPfPlan$\times$); on the three machines it ran on, it adds \jfGainLowMin--\jfGainLowMax{} to the speed ratio at
gpt-oss 11\%. An order-free (Shapley)
accounting shows that what to cache and how to load interact: either alone closes at most \fsBalAloneMax\% of the gap,
both together up to \fsBalBothMax\%. A model of the two read paths predicts the deployed cache and the in-step states
within a median \hmFrozenStepMed\% on \hmHostsN{} hosts.

\textbf{(3) Foresight has a measurable price} (\cref{sec:horizon}). Degraded to a window of the next $W$ tokens, the
oracle gains little over the deployed cache: a 16-token window on the deployed path at most \jaBSixteenMidGainMax\%,
and shorter or half-right windows \jaBEightHalfLowMin--\jaBFourMidMax$\times$. On nine models' traces, the window that closes half of the read gap holds about
\dcMed$\,C$ distinct experts per layer, a one-constant rule that predicts a held-out model as well as a two-constant
power law. No online policy, learned admission order, speculative batch or forecaster we tested comes near it.

\paragraph{Contributions.}
\begin{itemize}\setlength\itemsep{1pt}
\item A \textbf{bound on time per token} for exact-routing systems with $C$ slots per layer, from MIN's host reads and
the machine's probed rates, with a microbenchmark and a link-aware variant that show its read time is nearly reachable,
beside our cache, FreeToken, llama.cpp and \audAllN{} published measurements (\cref{sec:limit}).
\item An \textbf{in-engine oracle factorial} that crosses what to cache with how each expert is loaded, on
\fsAccHosts{} hosts, with an order-free accounting, a test of launch-to-launch variation, a model of the two read paths
tested on hosts it was not fitted on, and a comparison of MIN's greedy and fewest-admission schedules in the engine
(\cref{sec:gap}).
\item A \textbf{price on foresight}: its value by horizon and accuracy, in the engine and on nine models' traces, and a
one-constant rule for the horizon in distinct experts (\cref{sec:horizon}).
\item An artifact in which every prediction was committed before the machine it concerns started, and every number is
generated by a script (\cref{app:prereg,app:artifact}).
\end{itemize}

\section{Setting and Method}\label{sec:setting}
\paragraph{Models and workload.} gpt-oss-120b (36 MoE layers of 128 experts, top-4, MXFP4, 13.25\,MB per expert) and
Qwen3-30B-A3B in BF16 (48 layers of 128 experts, top-8, 9.44\,MB per expert), on the 30 AIME-25 problems; the cache
carries over between problems, as in one user's session. The system comparison decodes 256 tokens per problem
greedily through each system's server; the oracle jobs replay each model's own greedy output teacher-forced, so that
every configuration sees the same routing. A \emph{budget} is the share $C/E$ of each layer's experts that fit in GPU
slots: 11\%, 25\% and 40\% for gpt-oss, 12.5\%, 25\% and 43.75\% for Qwen3.

\paragraph{The engine.} Our cache is a 3{,}500-line patch to llama.cpp \citep{llamacpp}. Each MoE layer has $C$ GPU
slots. Routing is decided on the GPU; resident experts run there. A \emph{miss} is read from host memory in one of two
ways: CPU helper threads run it, or it is \emph{fetched}, copied into a slot and run on the GPU in the same step. The
deployed policy admits a miss when its decayed request count (half-life 16 tokens) beats the lowest resident's by a
margin $\kappa{=}1$; the CPU serves the admitted miss and a background copy then fills its slot, so it is read from
host memory twice. How many of a layer's misses to fetch rather than run on the CPU is a per-machine table computed from
the machine's bandwidth probe (\cref{app:law}).

\paragraph{Configurations and terms.} \Cref{tab:configs} names the policies. The oracles read the routing of the rest of
the problem from a trace recorded on the same machine; they decide, they do not predict. \emph{MIN} is Belady's optimum
with bypass \citep{belady1966study,bypassopt}: it \emph{admits} a miss into a slot only if some resident is needed later
than the miss, and otherwise \emph{bypasses} it, serving it without admission; with bypass, this maximises hits under
$C$ slots \citep{carlisle1995kcoloring,jain2016hawkeye}. An admitted expert is \emph{loaded} in one of two ways:
\emph{in the step}, copied over PCIe before the GPU runs it (one host read, on the critical path), or \emph{by the CPU},
served there and copied in the background (two reads). The two \emph{read paths} are the CPU's own reads of host
memory and the PCIe link. A budget is \emph{host-bound} when host reads, not the GPU, bound the time. The \emph{gap} is
the deployed cache's time per token minus the bound's.

\input{tab_configs}

\paragraph{Hosts and statistics.} RTX 5090 cards (32\,GB, 1.79\,TB/s datasheet) in desktop hosts, rented by the hour.
Every job first probes how fast the host reads its memory: from CPU threads ($B_c$), over PCIe ($B_p$), both at once
($B_{cp}$), and the highest rate by any method ($B_{\mathrm{host}}$); $B_p/B_c$ is the host's \emph{link-to-CPU
ratio}. Time per token is averaged over problems, ratios are ratios of means paired by problem, and within-host
intervals are 95\% bootstrap percentiles over problems. Machines differ far more than problems or launches: two panel
hosts with the same CPU model differ by \pnSameCpuDiffMin--\pnSameCpuDiffMax\% in the deployed cache's time, while
\jcRelaunchN{} machines relaunched later came within \jcRelaunchBaseMax\% of their first launch and every ratio within
\jcRelaunchRatioMax, more than a typical within-host interval, so we do not read smaller differences on one host. A host panel (\pnHosts{} hosts, the first \pnProblems{} problems, configurations in a seeded random
order) therefore treats the host as the unit: pooled intervals come from a crossed bootstrap over hosts and
problems \citep{kalibera2013rigorous}. Each job's predictions were committed before its machine started;
\cref{app:prereg} scores all of them.

\section{A Bound on Time per Token}\label{sec:limit}
Consider any system that executes the model's exact routing with at most $C$ experts of each layer resident on the
GPU. Per token each of the $Lk$ routed experts is either served by a resident copy, which the GPU reads from its own
memory, or read from host memory: by the CPU that runs it, or by the copy that brings it into a slot. Let $R^\star$ be
MIN's host reads per token, summed over layers, on the routing trace; no policy, even one that knows the future, reads
fewer. If
$c$ experts per token run on the CPU, host memory supplies at least $\max(R^\star,c)\,S$ bytes of experts of $S$
bytes each, and the GPU reads the dense weights $D$ plus $(Lk-c)\,S$. With everything overlapped,
\begin{equation}
\bar T \;\ge\; \min_{0\le c\le Lk}\ \max\!\Big(\frac{D+(Lk-c)S}{B_{\mathrm{gpu}}},\ \frac{\max(R^\star\!,c)\,S}{B_{\mathrm{host}}}\Big).
\label{eq:limit}
\end{equation}
A budget is \emph{host-bound} when the minimum is at $c{=}0$: the GPU could take every expert, and host reads bind.
That holds at the two smallest budgets of each model. The bound covers exact routing, whole experts of unmodified
bytes, at most $C$ residents per layer and one token per forward pass; it drops all latencies and lets the two read
paths share $B_{\mathrm{host}}$ freely.

\Cref{tab:headline} places three systems against it on two hosts. Our cache leads FreeToken \citep{freetoken} at
\bothLeadCells{} of \bothCells{} configurations and runs \bothLlamaXMin--\bothLlamaXMax$\times$ stock llama.cpp at equal
GPU expert memory. Tuned over five settings on a third host, FreeToken gains at most \fttBestOverDefaultMax$\times$ and
still trails at \fttLeadCells{} of six cells (\cref{app:system}). Against the bound, our cache stands at
\bothLimitPctMin--\bothLimitPctMax\%; with the GPU term at the rate all-in-VRAM decode reaches at batch size 1, at
\bothLimitMeasPctMin--\bothLimitMeasPctMax\%.

\paragraph{How tight is it?} The GPU term is loose: at batch size 1 all-in-VRAM decode reaches only
\gpuEffPctGpt\% (gpt-oss) and \gpuEffPctQwen\% (Qwen3) of the datasheet rate, which moves the bound at the larger budgets
but not where the host term binds. With every assumption of \cref{tab:limit} relaxed at once, our cache stands at
\limAllPctMin--\limAllPctMax\% of the bound on host B. The host term is nearly reachable. A microbenchmark (job 102)
replays MIN's per-layer reads of gpt-oss at 11\% and 25\% on three hosts without the model, CPU threads reading part of
each layer's experts while the copy engine copies the rest into slots: waiting for every layer, it reaches
\jdLayerMin--\jdLayerMax\% of the host term (\cref{tab:readsched}). It runs no compute, and lets every read take either
path; but an expert that is admitted and read once must cross the link. MIN has many hit-optimal schedules, and the
fewest admissions any of them needs is \laAdmMinLow{} per token at gpt-oss 11\%, against \laAdmGreedyLow{} for the greedy
rule (a linear program over reuse intervals, \texttt{scripts/linkaware.py}). Sending every admission over the link at
the probe's highest link rate raises the bound by at most \laOverLowMax\% at 11\% and \laOverMidMax\% at 25\% on \laHosts{}
probed hosts. Pooling the $LC$ slots across layers would lower the reads by \globalReadsMin--\globalReadsMax\%; we score
FreeToken's single cache against that pooled bound too (\ftPoolPctMin--\ftPoolPctMax\%).

\paragraph{Published systems.} Scored by one procedure (the same bound at each paper's datasheet ceilings, MIN's reads
from our traces of its model; \cref{app:audit}), the \audInclassN{} published batch-1 measurements inside the bound's
class reach a median \audInclassMed\% of it (quartiles \audInclassQone--\audInclassQthree\%), and our cache
\audOursMin--\audOursMax\%. Systems in this class run several times slower than the routing allows on the same machine;
the next section asks what the missing time is made of.

\section{Where the Seconds Go}\label{sec:gap}
The deployed cache does not know which experts the coming tokens will need. We measure what knowing them is worth by
running oracles inside the engine: on three hosts (O3--O5) at all six budgets, on the \pnHosts-host panel at the two
host-bound gpt-oss budgets, and on follow-up launches. \Cref{fig:factorial} shows each state against the deployed
cache on the same host. A running example: on O4 (a Ryzen 9 9950X) at gpt-oss 11\%, the bound is \fsExBoundMs\,ms per
token and the deployed cache takes \fsExBaseMs; MIN copying each admission in the step takes \fsExFetchMs, and with the
copies made a few steps ahead, \fsExPacedMs.

\paragraph{Spent as MIN spends it, foresight pays.} MIN with each admission copied once, in the step, is faster than the
deployed cache at every budget on O3--O5, by \fxFetchAllGainMin--\fxFetchAllGainMax\%, and by
\fxPacedAllGainMin--\fxPacedAllGainMax\% with the copies made ahead. The usual ways of spending foresight waste reads:
Belady prefetch admits experts MIN would bypass, and serving a miss on the CPU before copying it reads every admission
twice. They read \fxUsualReadsOverOptMin--\fxUsualReadsOverOptMax$\times$ MIN's bytes and, at the smallest budget of
each model on O4 and O5, gain at most \fxUsualLowGainMax\% or lose, where MIN with one read gains
\fxFetchLowMin--\fxFetchLowMax$\times$; the trade-off is classical for disks \citep{cao1995prefetching} and CPU
caches \citep{jain2018prefetch}. Even the best oracle reaches only \fxBestFracMin--\fxBestFracMax\% of the bound on O4
and O5 at the host-bound budgets.

\paragraph{Which of MIN's schedules matters.} MIN has many hit-optimal schedules. The greedy rule our oracles follow
admits a miss whenever some resident is needed later, and may evict it again before its next use; the schedule with the
fewest admissions among those with the most hits (\cref{sec:limit}) admits far fewer. Job 104 gave the engine that
schedule, computed on each host from its own routing trace, on three machines whose link reads at
\jfRatioMin--\jfRatioMax{} of their CPU rate (\cref{tab:job104}). It copies \jfCopyRatioMin--\jfCopyRatioMax{}
times as many experts in the step as the greedy schedule, for \jfMissDevMin--\jfMissDevMax\% more misses, and at gpt-oss
11\% it is \jfGainLowMin--\jfGainLowMax{} faster relative to the deployed cache. On the machine whose link reads at
\jfPfRatio{} of its CPU rate, where the greedy schedule loses on three launches (\jfPfFetch$\times$ here), it gains
(\jfPfPlan$\times$, interval [\jfPfPlanLo, \jfPfPlanHi]). Loaded by the CPU instead, the same set also gains there
(\cref{tab:job104}). MIN's set spent once pays on every machine we ran; how much depends on the link.

\input{tab_job104}

\paragraph{The machine decides how much.} A copy in the step puts the read on the PCIe link and on the critical path.
\Cref{fig:hostdep} plots each state's gain against the host's link-to-CPU ratio. MIN's greedy schedule with one read
gains less as the ratio falls (correlation \hdFetchCorrLow{} with its log at 11\%); on \jeNewN{} further machines with
ratios \jeNewRatioMin--\jeNewRatioMax{} it still gains \jeNewFetchLowMin--\jeNewFetchLowMax$\times$ at 11\%, and it
loses only on the machine at \hdRatioMin. Made ahead of use, the same copies lose nowhere. Admitting every miss, which
sends every read over the link, is at best at parity with the deployed cache (\hdAaMin--\hdAaMax$\times$).

\paragraph{A model of the two read paths.} Let $X_c$ and $X_p$ be the bytes per token that the CPU and the link read
from host memory in a state (from its counters), and $B_c$, $B_p$, $B_{cp}$ the probed rates of each path alone and of
both. The calibrated model of \cref{app:law} predicts $T = G + \max(X_c/B_c,\ X_p/B_p,\ (X_c{+}X_p)/B_{cp})$, with $G$,
the GPU's work and fixed costs, one constant per budget taken from the other hosts' fits. Given the byte counts, it
predicts the deployed cache and every state that copies in the step within a median \hmFrozenStepMed\% (90th
percentile \hmFrozenStepPninety\%) on \hmHostsN{} hosts; from the probe alone, written before any timed run, within a
median \jaProbeMed\% on later launches. It is optimistic for copies in the step on slow links (up to
\jaProbeFetchMinAbs\%, \cref{app:law}), and for copies in the background or ahead (median \hmFrozenBgMed\%): those
copies are not free. It gets the sign of the greedy copy's gain right on every host, but that test is weak, since the
copy loses on one machine only.

\paragraph{An order-free accounting.} Two choices separate the deployed cache from MIN with one read: \emph{what to
cache} (the deployed policy's admissions or MIN's) and \emph{how to load} (by the CPU, two reads, or in the step, one).
The engine ran all four combinations on O4, O5, the panel and three new machines. In the running example, changing only
what to cache closes \fsExSetTwo\% of the \fsExGap\,ms gap, changing only how to load \fsExReadsOnline\%, and both
\fsExBoth\% (rounded): the choices interact, because MIN caches \fxExAdmitsOverOnline{} times as many experts per token
as the deployed policy and, loaded by the CPU, each is read twice. We credit each choice with its effect averaged over
the two orders, its Shapley value \citep{fawcett2016ablation,jain1991art} (\fsExSet\% and \fsExReads\% here);
\cref{tab:shapley} also lists each alone and the interaction. Copying ahead is nested under MIN's set
(\fsExPacing\%); what no oracle touches is the rest (\fsExRest\%). On the \fsBalHosts{} hosts whose link reads at least
half as fast as the CPU, either change alone closes at most \fsBalAloneMax\% of the gap and both together
\fsBalBothMin--\fsBalBothMax\%; on the \fsSlowHosts{} below that line, loading in the step costs time at
\fsSlowReadsLoseN{} of \fsSlowCells{} host-budgets. These use the greedy set. With the fewest-admission set (job 104),
both together close \jfBothPlanLowMin--\jfBothPlanLowMax\% at 11\% on its three machines, against
\jfBothGreedyLowMin--\jfBothGreedyLowMax\% with the greedy set, and the interaction remains
(\jfInterPlanLowMin--\jfInterPlanLowMax\%).

\paragraph{The rest.} What no oracle touches has three visible parts. The prefetch is imprecise (it reads
\fsbpReadsOptMin--\fsbpReadsOptMax$\times$ MIN's bytes); the overlap is partial (charging every read in sequence with the
GPU's work over-predicts the prefetching state by \fxPacedLawTimeMin{} to \fxPacedLawTimeMax\%); and the GPU runs at
about half its datasheet rate at batch size 1. On slow-link hosts every state either loads the link or leaves reads to
the CPU helpers, which take \helperRateMin--\helperRateMax$\times$ the probe's time per expert, and the oracles' greedy
admissions add copies the fewest-admission set avoids. Little of the rest is slack in the bound: splitting MIN's reads
between the two paths at their probed rates tightens it by \fsSlackBalMin--\fsSlackBalMax\% of the gap on fast-link
hosts, and sending every admission over the link raises it by at most \laOverMidMax\%.

\section{How Much Foresight Is Needed}\label{sec:horizon}
The oracles know the whole future; a forecaster would know part of it, imperfectly. We give the cache a \emph{window},
the routing of the next $W$ tokens, exact or with errors, and ask what it is worth in the engine and, on the traces of
nine models, how long it must be.

\paragraph{Window policies.} \emph{Window $W$} extends admitting every miss: at each step, each miss in request order
takes a free slot or else the victim, the lowest-scored resident not requested in the window (or, if all are, the one
requested furthest ahead), unless the window shows the victim needed before the miss; admissions are copied in the step.
With no window this is admitting every miss, the online policy that reads least at these budgets; with the whole
problem in view it is MIN with one read. \emph{Deployed + window} gives the same window to the deployed policy, so that
foresight changes only what is cached. A degraded window keeps each future expert with probability $r$ and replaces it
otherwise, so precision and recall are both $r$. The engine plans each step exactly as a replay script does (within
\pnReplayMax\% on the panel's GPUs).

\input{tab_window100}

\paragraph{Against the deployed cache, a short window pays little.} On the deployed path (\cref{tab:window100}, \jaHosts{}
hosts), a 16-token window runs \jaBSixteenLowMin--\jaBSixteenLowMax$\times$ the deployed cache at gpt-oss 11\% and
\jaBSixteenMidMin--\jaBSixteenMidMax$\times$ at 25\%, about what MIN with two reads gains; shorter or half-right windows
lose up to \jaWorstBLossPct\%. Copied in the step, the same window beats the deployed path where the link reads at least
\jaPathHighRatioMin{} of the CPU rate and loses to it at \jaPathLowRatioMax{} or below; on the \pnHostsLow-host panel a
4-token window in the step is slower than the deployed cache on \pnSlowerFourLow{} hosts at 11\%, a 16-token one on
\pnSlowerSixteenLow. On the deployed path the engine misses \jaReplayDevMin--\jaReplayDevMax\% more than an instant
replay (the deployed state matches its replay within \jaBaseReplayDevMax\%); background copies that land late are the
likely cause.

\paragraph{Against admitting every miss, it recovers a share fast.} \Cref{fig:value} gives the share of MIN's gain over
admitting every miss that each window recovers. At gpt-oss 11\%, exact windows of 1, 4 and 16 tokens recover
\pnShareOneLowMin--\pnShareOneLowMax, \pnShareFourLowMin--\pnShareFourLowMax{} and
\pnShareSixteenLowMin--\pnShareSixteenLowMax{} of it in time; at 25\%, where the cache turns over more slowly,
\pnShareFourMidMin--\pnShareFourMidMax{} at 4 tokens. The calibrated model predicts these shares within a median
\hmWinShareErrMed{} (\hmWinShareN{} host-budget-windows). Time lags reads where the link is slow, because a window still
copies \pnLinkPctFour\% of its misses in the step (MIN: \pnLinkPctMin\%): its share of the time gain falls below its
share of the read saving by \pnGapFourLowMin{} where the two paths are equally fast and \pnGapFourLowMax{} at a ratio of
\pnGapRatioAtMax. Accuracy matters as much as horizon: an 8-token window right half the time recovers
\pnShareEightHalfLowMin--\pnShareEightHalfLowMax, about what 2 exact tokens buy, and loses to the deployed cache on
\pnSlowerEightHalfLow{} of \pnHostsLow{} hosts and, on the deployed path, on all \jaHosts{}
(\jaBEightHalfLowMin--\jaBEightHalfLowMax$\times$; \cref{app:value}).

\paragraph{The horizon across nine models.} An event-level simulator replays each model's own sampled text
(33{,}000--152{,}000 tokens) \citep{zhang2026reproducible}. Against admitting every miss, which reads least among LRU,
LFU, two decayed-frequency variants, ARC and S3-FIFO in \rbPolAaWins{} of \rbPolCells{} points (\cref{app:traces}), the
window $W_{50}$ that closes half of the read gap to MIN ranges from \dcWMin{} to \dcWMax{} tokens. Paging theory measures
lookahead in distinct pages \citep{albers1997lookahead,breslauer1995lookahead}, and so does the rule that fits: the
experts a layer requests within $W_{50}$ number about \dcMed$\,C$ (quartiles \dcLo--\dcHi). Fitted on eight models and
tested on the ninth, this one-constant rule predicts the held-out horizon as well as a two-constant power law in
tokens (\cref{tab:lomo}; the two are not distinguished) and better than $W_{50}\propto C/k$. Its value is the unit: a
forecaster's target is the next $\approx$\dcMed$\,C$ distinct experts of each layer.

\paragraph{Nothing realisable reaches it.} \emph{Online policies} cannot (above). \emph{A learned admission order}
reads \lreFewerMin--\lreFewerMax\% fewer experts in the engine than the deployed policy with single reads but runs
\lreOverBaseMin--\lreOverBaseMax$\times$ the deployed cache. \emph{Speculative batches} save an online cache
\bkSameNineMin--\bkSameNineMax\% of its reads at 90\% draft acceptance and cost \bkSameEightLossMin--\bkSameEightLossMax\% at

\section{Related Work}\label{sec:related}
\paragraph{Offloading systems.} Demand-paging systems cache experts per layer and copy misses over
PCIe \citep{eliseev2023offloading,xue2024moeinfinity,song2024promoe,tang2024hobbit,pagingexperts2026}; an upstream
llama.cpp pull request adds such a cache, admitting every miss into an LRU \citep{llamacpppr29887}. CPU-execution
systems run non-resident experts on the host \citep{kamahori2024fiddler,ktransformers,zhong2025hybrimoe,daop2025,wang2026cloudgrade,atsinfer2026};
a set-associative GPU cache with CPU-executed misses \citep{huang2025cpugpu}, a llama.cpp fork \citep{leloch2026,llamacppdisc24528}
and two llama.cpp pull requests \citep{llamacpppr26563,llamacpppr27861} are the closest designs to ours.
FreeToken \citep{freetoken} and DALI \citep{dali2026} split each layer's misses between CPU and PCIe. Predictors guess
upcoming experts from the previous layer's state \citep{hwang2024pregated,zhu2025preattention}, several tokens ahead
with a learned forecaster \citep{wang2026seqmoe,mira2026}, or from draft tokens \citep{spmoe,moespac2026,cascade2025};
Read-ME decouples the router so routing is known in advance \citep{readme2024}. Batched-throughput
systems \citep{flexgen2023,cao2024moelightning,yuan2025moelens} and SSD tiers \citep{ssdllama,edgezero} are outside our
scope.

\paragraph{Bounds, oracles and caching theory.} WiSP \citep{wisp2026} bounds decode by PCIe turnover, Budgeting
Bytes \citep{budgetingbytes2026} by bytes over bandwidth for a given fetch set, and MoE-CAP \citep{moecap} measures
bandwidth utilisation against a single peak; the roofline \citep{williams2009roofline} is the single-memory ancestor of
our bound. \citet{zhang2026reproducible} proposes a trace-level evaluation contract and reports a 44--46\% gap
between causal policies and MIN with bypass; \citet{liang2026lrc} measure, across 20 models, the hit rate
of an oracle cache that sees $m$ tokens ahead against the cache ratio $C/k$. We add the time domain: an oracle inside a
real engine, the cost of how foresight is spent, and a horizon rule that predicts held-out models. SAEM \citep{saem2026}
gives its own stage-level planner perfect knowledge of the coming stage in its engine and finds that worth at most
about 14\% on an A100; SpecMD \citep{specmd} benchmarks expert-cache and speculative prefetch policies across hardware; \citet{angelopoulos2025cache} give competitive bounds for layered expert paging. With bypass, MIN maximises hits
exactly \citep{carlisle1995kcoloring,jain2016hawkeye}; when bypass and admission cost differ, the time-optimal placement
is a different problem \citep{chopt2020}, which is why we use MIN's reads as an input to a bound rather than as a
time-optimal policy. Lookahead in paging is valuable when measured in distinct pages \citep{albers1997lookahead,breslauer1995lookahead},
and learning-augmented paging is most robust with predictions of whether a page will be evicted before its next
use \citep{antoniadis2023succinct}; learned replacement imitates Belady in CPU and content-delivery
caches \citep{jain2016hawkeye,song2020lrb} and in an SSD-backed expert cache \citep{flashmoe2026}.

\section{Limitations}\label{sec:limits}
\begin{itemize}\setlength\itemsep{1pt}
\item \textbf{Hardware and sample.} Rented consumer cards in desktop hosts; clocks could not be locked and power limits
differed. The panel has \pnHosts{} hosts, one launch each (\jcRelaunchN{} relaunched); the full 2$\times$2 ran at every
budget on two hosts. Few hosts have a link slower than half their CPU rate, and the greedy copy loses on one machine
only, so the crossover in \cref{fig:hostdep} rests on few points; the line at half was drawn after the panel ran.
The fewest-admission schedule ran on three machines at two budgets.
\item \textbf{Workload.} Batch-1 decode of two models on AIME prompts, also used while developing the system,
teacher-forced on each model's own greedy text. Prompts and batches above one bypass the cache.
\item \textbf{The bound.} Exact routing and per-layer slots (pooling slots across layers lowers MIN's reads by
\globalReadsMin--\globalReadsMax\%). It drops latencies and lets the read paths share the best probed rate, so a faster
reader than our probe could exceed it. Its read time is nearly reachable in a microbenchmark that runs no compute;
whether the GPU's work overlaps the reads as the bound assumes we have not measured.
\item \textbf{The oracles and the model.} Oracles read a recorded trace. In the engine the fewest-admission schedule
misses \jfMissDevMin--\jfMissDevMax\% more than the greedy one, more than its replay predicts; its measured gains
include that cost. The time model is optimistic for copies in the step on slow links and for background copies, and
it fails on a 12-channel server host (\cref{app:law}). Degraded windows draw errors independently; a real forecaster's
errors are correlated with what the cache holds.
\item \textbf{Outputs.} Parity is measured by teacher-forced KL divergence against an all-VRAM reference (mean
\klMeanGpt{} and \klMeanQwen{} nats), not by task accuracy.
\end{itemize}

\section{Conclusion}
With experts in host memory, batch-1 MoE decode is bound by host bytes, and the bound is nearly reachable in host reads:
MIN's reads, replayed alone, reach at least \jdLayerMin\% of its read time. Our cache stands at
\bothLimitPctMin--\bothLimitPctMax\% of it, and published systems at a median of \audInclassMed\%. Knowing the future
recovers part of the gap when it is spent as MIN spends it, caching MIN's set and reading each expert once, and the
choice among MIN's hit-optimal schedules matters: the fewest-admission one gains on every machine we ran, including the
slow-link machine where the greedy one loses. How much it gains is a property of the machine that its two read rates
largely predict. The rest of the gap, \fsBalRestMin--\fsBalRestMax\% on fast-link hosts, includes an imprecise
prefetch, partial overlap and a GPU at half its datasheet rate. And foresight has a measurable price: about
\dcMed$\,C$ distinct experts of each layer's coming routing, at accuracies no forecaster we know of or trained reaches.
That is the target for the next MoE cache, and for router designs that make routing predictable in advance.
