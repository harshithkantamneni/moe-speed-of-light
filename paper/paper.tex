\author{Harshith Kantamneni\\Independent Researcher}

\begin{abstract}
A Mixture-of-Experts model too large for one consumer GPU keeps most experts in host memory; each decoded token
waits for the ones it needs. How fast could it decode, and what separates real systems from that? A
\emph{host-memory law}, a GPU term plus host bytes over the probed rate of the paths that read them, predicts decode
time blind on \lawHosts{} machines with \lawMedian\% median error. Belady's optimum with bypass on the model's routing
gives a \emph{speed limit} in seconds, a ceiling under stated assumptions for exact-routing systems: our llama.cpp
expert cache reaches \bothLimitPctMin--\bothLimitPctMax\% of the per-layer-budget limit on two RTX 5090 hosts,
FreeToken and llama.cpp \ftPoolPctMin--\ftPoolPctMax\% and \llPoolPctMin--\llPoolPctMax\% of the pooled one their
designs allow, and \audAllN{} published measurements a median \audAllMed\% of theirs (\audTraceN{} scored on their
own traces, \audTraceMed\%). A Shapley \emph{accounting} says the rest is mostly missing routing foresight where the
host binds and serialised host reads where the GPU does. In the engine, the limit's own policy given future routing
runs \fsbGainHighMin--\fsbGainHighMax\% faster at expert budgets of 25\% and above, mostly on the bytes it saves,
and a hit-optimal oracle \fsHitGainMin--\fsHitGainMax\% faster on two hosts by overlapping copies with the step; at
the lowest budgets the first gains \fsbGainLowMaxAny\% at most and the second loses. Nine models' traces give the horizon foresight needs,
$W_{50}\approx\fsPre\,(C/k)^{\fsExp}$ tokens, which speculative batching does not supply. The cache leads FreeToken
at \bothLeadCells{} of \bothCells{} cells (\bothLeadQwenMin{} to $+$\bothLeadGptMax\%) and runs
\bothLlamaXMin--\bothLlamaXMax$\times$ llama.cpp. Every prediction was committed before its measurement;
\scHeldAnyPct\% of \scScored{} scored clauses held on the point estimate, \scHeldStrictPct\% with their intervals.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}
Open-weight Mixture-of-Experts models now carry 30--120\,B parameters and activate a few billion per token; a desktop
GPU has 24--32\,GB. Running such a model at batch 1 therefore means keeping most experts in host DRAM, and each
decoded token then reads its routed experts from one of three places: GPU memory, host memory by the CPU that runs the
expert, or host memory across PCIe into a GPU slot~\cite{ivanov2021datamovement,hoefler2021sparsity}. A line of
systems has built on this: GPU expert caches with demand fetch~\cite{eliseev2023offloading,xue2024moeinfinity,song2024promoe},
CPU execution of misses~\cite{kamahori2024fiddler,ktransformers,zhong2025hybrimoe,huang2025cpugpu,daop2025,dali2026},
per-miss choices between the two~\cite{freetoken}, and expert prediction~\cite{hwang2024pregated,zhu2025preattention,wang2026seqmoe}.
Each reports a speed-up over a baseline of its choosing. None states how fast its machine \emph{could} decode the model
within the same GPU memory, or how much of the remaining time any design could remove, and the trace-level evaluation
contracts proposed for this field~\cite{zhang2026reproducible} count cache misses, not seconds.

We answer those questions on consumer hardware (RTX 5090, 4090 and 3090 hosts with desktop CPUs, rented by the hour)
with two models, gpt-oss-120b~\cite{openai2025gptoss} and Qwen3-30B-A3B~\cite{yang2025qwen3}, routing traces of
nine, and \audAllN{} published measurements from \audSources{} sources. The method is Hoefler and
Belli's~\cite{hoefler2015benchmarking}: a performance model with bounds, statistically sound comparisons, and every
prediction committed to a public repository before the machine it concerns was started (\cref{app:prereg,app:rules}).

\paragraph{Contributions.}
\begin{itemize}\setlength\itemsep{1pt}
\item \textbf{A host-memory law, validated as a predictor} (\cref{sec:law}): time per token
$=G+\max(X_c/B_c,\,X_p/B_p,\,(X_c{+}X_p)/B_{cp})$, host bytes $X$ from the engine's counters and rates $B$ from a
probe, predicted blind on \lawHosts{} machines and tested under leave-one-host-out validation against the additive
and per-layer forms, with each failure traced to a measured cause.
\item \textbf{A speed limit in seconds} (\cref{sec:limit}): a bound on any exact-routing system with $C$ expert slots
per layer, from the exact Belady optimum with bypass, two host read paths and measured ceilings, tightened four ways,
reported beside every measured speed on four machines, and applied to \audAllN{} published measurements (\cref{sec:audit}).
\item \textbf{An accounting, and its check} (\cref{sec:limit}): Shapley attribution of the gap to the limit, which no
ordering of fixes can bias, with the regime it predicts (foresight where the host binds, overlap where the GPU does),
and its largest term tested in the engine with two oracles of future routing\fsMeasuredContrib.
\item \textbf{What the traces say} (\cref{sec:traces}): across nine models, which policy earns its reads, how fast
the hot set drifts, how many tokens of foresight are worth half the optimum, and what batched speculative
verification cannot buy.
\item \textbf{The instrument} (\cref{sec:system}): a llama.cpp expert cache whose per-machine miss split comes from
the law, measured against FreeToken and llama.cpp on two RTX 5090 hosts, an RTX 4090 and an RTX 3090, with an ablation
of its parts and a measurement of its scope.
\end{itemize}

\paragraph{What we do not claim.} Running missed experts on the CPU~\cite{kamahori2024fiddler,ktransformers},
caching hot experts with CPU-executed misses~\cite{zhong2025hybrimoe,huang2025cpugpu,llamacppdisc24528}, splitting a
layer's misses between CPU and PCIe from a bandwidth model~\cite{freetoken,dali2026}, a PCIe turnover
ceiling~\cite{wisp2026}, bytes over bandwidth as a bound~\cite{budgetingbytes2026}, Belady with bypass as a cache-level
oracle~\cite{zhang2026reproducible} and a table of systems against the all-in-VRAM speed~\cite{wang2026seqmoe} are
all published. Our system uses the first three; what is new is the limit in seconds, the accounting and its
measurement, the trace results, the audit, and the measurements themselves.

\section{Setting and Method}\label{sec:setting}
\paragraph{Workload.} Single-request decode. gpt-oss-120b (MXFP4 experts; 36 MoE layers of 128 experts, top-4,
13.25\,MB per expert) and Qwen3-30B-A3B in BF16 (48 layers of 128 experts, top-8, 9.44\,MB per expert). Prompts are
the 30 AIME-25 problems in FreeToken's own benchmark format; each system decodes 256 tokens per problem, greedily,
after one warm-up request on a fixed held-out text, in one session in which the cache carries over between problems.
One client measures every system with FreeToken's own metric, (tokens$-$1) over the time between the first and last
token's arrival.

\paragraph{Machines and probes.} RTX 5090 (32\,GB, 1.79\,TB/s datasheet) hosts with desktop CPUs, rented by the hour.
Each job first runs a probe (\texttt{concur}) that reads host memory from $t$ CPU threads, over PCIe with zero-copy
kernels and with the copy engine, and from both at once; FreeToken's own probe (\texttt{ft bench bw}) runs too. We call
the CPU rate at the helper thread count $B_c$, the zero-copy PCIe rate $B_p$ and the combined rate $B_{cp}$. GPU clocks
cannot be locked on these rentals; we report each card's power limit. \Cref{tab:headline} is measured on two RTX
5090 hosts: \emph{listing B}, a Ryzen 9 9950X3D rental whose card reports a \memClkHeadline\,MHz memory clock,
\memClkRatio$\times$ the bandwidth the limit assumes, against \memClkCommon\,MHz on \memClkCommonN{} of the
\memClkOtherRentals{} other RTX 5090 rentals that recorded one; and the \emph{stock-clock host}, a Ryzen 9 9950X
rental at the common clock with a \scCpuBwDrop\% slower CPU. The ablation and the long-output runs use \emph{listing
A}, a second rental of the 9950X3D model at the common clock.

\paragraph{Equal memory.} llama.cpp keeps whole layers' experts on the GPU (\texttt{--n-cpu-moe}~$n$), our cache keeps
$C$ experts of every layer, and FreeToken takes a cache rate; we match the number of experts on the GPU (within
2.5\%) and report measured VRAM. FreeToken preallocates 28.4--30.1\,GiB whatever its rate.

\paragraph{Statistics and protocol.} Ratios are of mean speeds, paired by problem, with 95\% percentile intervals from
10{,}000 bootstrap resamples; they cover problem sampling within one launch on one machine, not rentals (two rentals
of one CPU model differed by 19--22\% in decode speed, 24--32\% in host bandwidth). Where a system has variants (our FETCH tables, FreeToken's
offload and hybrid backends), a first launch picks each system's faster variant per budget and a second launch, which
the comparison uses, runs only those (one row of \cref{tab:headline} has no second launch; on the stock-clock host
FreeToken's variants were carried over from listing B's selection rather than re-picked). Every job's predictions
are in the header of its script, committed and pushed before the machine starts; \cref{app:prereg} scores every
clause under one rule.

\section{A Host-Memory Law}\label{sec:law}
With experts partly in host memory, a decode step does two kinds of work: GPU work that every token needs (attention,
dense projections, resident experts, the output head, launches), and reads of the experts that are not resident. The
second is limited by host memory. The CPU reads $X_c$ bytes per token (the experts it runs), PCIe reads $X_p$ bytes
(the experts copied to the GPU, whether on demand or ahead of time), and when both run at once they share the DRAM:
\begin{equation}
T \;=\; G \;+\; \max\!\Big(\frac{X_c}{B_c},\ \frac{X_p}{B_p},\ \frac{X_c+X_p}{B_{cp}}\Big).
\label{eq:law}
\end{equation}
$X_c$ and $X_p$ come from the engine's counters of misses, fetches and admissions, which are counts, not timings.
$B_c$, $B_p$ and $B_{cp}$ come from the machine's probe. $G$ is one constant per engine and model, fitted once on one
host (4.82\,ms for our cache on gpt-oss-120b, 5.06\,ms for llama.cpp) and then frozen in the repository. The form is
FreeToken's residual-bandwidth split of one layer~\cite{freetoken}, lifted to the token; the difference is that the
three rates are probed rather than assumed to add, and that the law is tested as a predictor.

\paragraph{Blind test.} On every machine of the blind test the job wrote the law's prediction from its own probe
before any model ran (\cref{tab:law}); all of its rows are our cache on gpt-oss-120b on RTX 5090 hosts. Over \lawCfg{} configurations of our cache on \lawHosts{} machines (\lawMeas{} measurements; one
machine ran twice) the median error is \lawMedian\% per configuration (\lawRowsMedian\% per measurement); without the 12-channel EPYC 9655 it is \lawDeskMedian\% over
\lawDeskN{} configurations (90th percentile \lawDeskPninety\%). llama.cpp \texttt{--n-cpu-moe} is predicted within 4\% on
the Ryzen hosts, including two Qwen3 configurations predicted on the headline machine (+3.6\%, +4.0\%; for Qwen3,
$G{=}4.3$\,ms, the mean residual of three llama.cpp runs on another host); on the hybrid-core and server hosts it
misses by \lawLlamaOffMin--\lawLlamaOffMax\%. With its constants frozen, the law scores the cache on the
\lawFrozenHosts{} RTX 5090 hosts rented later (\lawFrozenN{} measurements at the six cells of \cref{tab:headline},
not written before the runs) at a median \lawFrozenMed\%, 90th percentile \lawFrozenPninety\%, worst
\lawFrozenMax\% (\lawFrozenWorstCell).

\paragraph{Its form and its failures.} In 19 of the \lawCfg{} configurations nothing is copied, so they test one
roof; refit under leave-one-host-out validation, the token-level max has a median error of \lohoMaxMed\% (90th
percentile \lohoMaxPninety\%), the additive form $G+X_c/B_c+X_p/B_p$ \lohoAddMed\% (\lohoAddPninety\%) and a
per-layer max summed over layers \lohoLayerMed\% (\lohoLayerPninety\%); the max beats the sum only on the three rows
that copy misses on demand behind a 53\,GB/s link, where both paths carry most of the bytes, and ties elsewhere. Each
failure has a measured cause (\cref{app:law}): on-demand copies behind a slow link share DRAM less evenly than the
probe's combined rate says (the Core Ultra 7 and the EPYC 7352); llama.cpp splits every matrix evenly across threads
and runs at 46\% of the law on a hybrid-core host, where our helpers claim work dynamically and stay on it; and on a
12-channel EPYC 9655 host memory stops being the limit and a per-layer latency floor that no desktop host has takes
over. $G$ (\lawGRefit\,ms refit) exceeds the whole all-in-VRAM token (\vramMs\,ms) because it absorbs a shortfall the
probe does not see: the helpers read at \helperRateMin--\helperRateMax$\times$ the probe's time per expert, and a
fitted rate factor is collinear with $G$ at this sample size. FreeToken's own runs are not predicted, because its
counters are not exposed; the law covers our engine and llama.cpp.

\paragraph{What the law says.} For an engine that reads host memory in sequence with its GPU work, as these do,
every mechanism acts only through the bytes read from host memory per token and the number of read paths in use. A second path adds bandwidth on every host we
measured but the 12-channel EPYC ($B_{cp}$ against $\max(B_c,B_p)$: \secondMin--\secondMax\% on the Ryzen hosts, \secondUltra\% on the
Core Ultra, \secondEpyc\% on the Zen~2 EPYC), and that is the whole gain available to fetching or splitting misses
between the CPU and PCIe. Prefetching never reduces the bytes; what it can buy is overlap, which the law does not
price and \cref{sec:limit} measures. And the ``host lottery'' is the DRAM: two rentals of the same CPU differed by
19--22\% in speed, which is 62 against 47\,GB/s of host bandwidth (24--32\%).

\section{The Speed Limit, and Where the Seconds Go}\label{sec:limit}
\paragraph{Bound.} Consider any system that executes the model's exact routing with at most $C$ experts of each layer
resident on the GPU. Per token, each of the $Lk$ routed experts is either served by a resident copy (the GPU reads it
from VRAM) or not, in which case its $S$ bytes are read from host memory once more: by the CPU that runs it, or by the
copy that brings it into a slot. Let $R^\star$ be the reads per token of Belady's MIN with bypass~\cite{belady1966study,hasslinger2018,bypassopt}
applied per layer to the routing trace, the fewest reads of any policy, even one that knows the future (an expert may
be evicted after serving the current token; a policy that keeps every requested expert until the token ends reads
\exactReadsMoreMin--\exactReadsMoreMax\% more). If $c$ experts per token run on the CPU, host memory supplies at least
$\max(R^\star,c)\,S$ bytes and the GPU reads the dense bytes $D$ plus $(Lk-c)\,S$. With everything overlapped,
\begin{equation}
\bar T \;\ge\; \min_{0\le c\le Lk}\ \max\!\Big(\frac{D+(Lk-c)S}{B_{\mathrm{gpu}}},\ \frac{\max(R^\star\!,c)\,S}{B_{\mathrm{host}}}\Big),
\label{eq:limit}
\end{equation}
with $B_{\mathrm{gpu}}$ the GPU's datasheet rate and $B_{\mathrm{host}}$ the highest host read rate the probe measured
by any method (\bHost\,GB/s on listing B). Its class: exact routing, whole experts of unmodified bytes, at most $C$
of each layer resident, one token per forward pass. A system free to pool its $LC$ slots across layers has a looser
bound, \poolOverLayerMin--\poolOverLayerMax\% higher (\cref{tab:limit}); FreeToken keeps one LRU over all layers
and llama.cpp pins whole layers, so \cref{tab:headline} scores them against the pooled bound, and speculative
decoders, which verify several tokens per pass, lie outside it. The bound assumes that an expert run on the CPU is read from DRAM
(the optimum's shortest re-read distance is \lthreeReuseMin\,GB of host traffic, far beyond any CPU cache), that
computation runs at memory speed, and that the two read paths share $B_{\mathrm{host}}$ freely; it drops all
latencies and starts from an empty cache. $B_{\mathrm{host}}$ is the highest rate our probe reached, not a hardware
peak, so a system that reads host memory faster than the probe could exceed the limit (a second rental of the same
CPU probed 90.3\,GB/s), and listing B's card, whose memory clock is 1.21$\times$ the datasheet's, read
1,847\,GB/s in our probe against the 1,792 the bound assumes, which raises its GPU-bound limits by 3\%. The limit
exceeds the all-in-VRAM speed at the GPU-bound cells because it uses the datasheet rate, of which batch-1 kernels
reach 52--61\%; with the measured GPU rate (\cref{tab:limit}) it is 9\% above all-in-VRAM, the CPU's share. The traces are the models' own greedy text on the 30 AIME-25 prompts, templated and tokenised exactly as the
server does, with routing recorded teacher-forced by our llama.cpp instrumentation; each system's own greedy text
drifts from it within the first few hundred characters on gpt-oss (\cref{sec:system}).

\paragraph{Tightening it.} \Cref{tab:limit} changes one assumption at a time. A single pool of $LC$ slots shared
across layers, the true equal-memory constraint, needs \globalReadsMin--\globalReadsMax\% fewer reads than per-layer
slots and raises the four host-bound limits by 5--10\%; it leaves the two largest budgets unchanged, where the limit
is the machine's aggregate bandwidth and read counts no longer matter. Starting the optimum warm, after
\warmSteps{} tokens, changes it by at most 1.4\%. $B_{\mathrm{host}}$ is one probe sample; the six concurrent samples
on this machine read \bHostMed\,GB/s at the median (mean \bHostMean, 95\% interval \bHostMeanLo--\bHostMeanHi), and
the median lowers the host-bound limits by 8\%. The GPU term is the one that moves most: at batch 1 the all-in-VRAM
runs reach only \gpuEffPctGpt\% (gpt-oss) and \gpuEffPctQwen\% (Qwen3) of the datasheet rate, and with the measured
rate the limit at 25\% and above collapses to one aggregate line, \limMeasGpuGpt{} and \limMeasGpuQwen\,tok/s. A
per-layer sum, $\sum_l\max(\text{GPU}_l,\text{host}_l)$ with the optimum's reads per layer, which no system without
cross-layer prefetch can beat, sits \twoFormsMin--\twoFormsMax\% of a measured token below the token-level maximum
(\twoFormsSeqMin--\twoFormsSeqMax\% if dense work is also serialised). With every tightening at once, our cache stands
at \limAllPctMin--\limAllPctMax\% of the limit, FreeToken at \ftLimAllPctMin--\ftLimAllPctMax\% and stock llama.cpp at
\llLimAllPctMin--\llLimAllPctMax\%; against the limit as published, \limExPctMin--\limExPctMax\%,
\ftLimExPctMin--\ftLimExPctMax\% and \llLimExPctMin--\llLimExPctMax\%. Even with every weight in VRAM, our cache with
every expert in its slots matches or beats stock llama.cpp (\cOneTwoEight$\times$ and \cOneTwoEightQ$\times$): the
cache costs nothing when the model fits. The traces represent the measured runs: replayed through a simulator of our
deployed policy, they give hit rates within \simHitGptMax{} points of the engine's own counters on gpt-oss and within
\simHitQwenMin--\simHitQwenMax{} points on Qwen3.

\paragraph{The accounting.} Adding one cost at a time to the limit in a fixed order finds foresight largest in
every cell; costed after GPU efficiency instead, it is smaller than that in two cells, and costed after overlap it
vanishes at the largest budgets. Shapley values remove the choice: each fix's share is its
average marginal cost over every order (\cref{fig:shapley}), a method with a precedent in performance debugging of
microservices~\cite{shapleyiq2024}. On listing B the result has two regimes, and \cref{eq:limit} says which one a cell is in (on the two oracle
hosts of the next paragraph, whose host memory is weaker, foresight edges overlap at the GPU-bound cells too). In the
four cells where the limit binds on host reads ($c^\star{=}0$: gpt-oss at 11 and 25\%, Qwen3 at 12.5 and 25\%),
missing foresight is the largest cost, \shFsHostMin--\shFsHostMax\% of the gap (\shFsHostTokMin--\shFsHostTokMax\%
of the token); in the two where it binds on the GPU (gpt-oss 40\%, Qwen3 43.75\%, balanced by running 13 and 30
experts per token on the CPU), host reads serialised with GPU work is largest, \shOvlGpuMin--\shOvlGpuMax\% of the gap
(\shOvlGpuTokMin--\shOvlGpuTokMax\% of the token), and foresight second at \shFsGpuMin\%. Policy and read paths cost
\shPolMin--\shPolMax\% of the gap, of which most is the difference between the probe's highest rate and the
\shBhostZc\,GB/s the engine's own law uses (at that rate the term falls to \shPolZcMin--\shPolZcMax\%); per-token
host work \shHostMin--\shHostMax\%; and GPU kernels below datasheet, net of the overlap the engine achieves,
\shGpuMin--\shGpuMax\%. Under this accounting, in every order of every cell the largest cost is foresight or overlap;
GPU efficiency, policy and host work never are. Two of the five terms are definitions rather than measurements, and
the reader should weigh them as such. The GPU term is the residual: its factor is calibrated per cell so that the
model with no fix applied reproduces the measured time, so the five shares close the gap by construction; charging
the all-in-VRAM shortfall in full instead raises it to \shGpuGrossMin--\shGpuGrossMax\% of the gap in the GPU-bound
cells, where it is then the largest cost in \shGpuGrossOrdersGHigh{} and \shGpuGrossOrdersQHigh{} of the
\shOrders{} orders, and changes no host-bound cell. And ``overlap'' is \cref{eq:limit}'s form, which both overlaps
host reads with GPU work and rebalances 13--30 experts per token onto the CPU; the profile below finds that overlap
alone, with the engine's present split, would save 5--10\%. The interior states of the model are not measured, with
one exception: \cref{sec:traces} and the oracle measurement below test the foresight term, the one no engineering of
read paths can recover, and those measurements probe two other parts of the model on the way.
Foresight is largest in every one of the \shOrders{} orders at Qwen3 12.5\%, in \shOrdersGLow{} of them at gpt-oss
11\% and Qwen3 25\%, and ties overlap within \shTieMs\,ms at gpt-oss 25\%.

\paragraph{One token, profiled.} On Qwen3 at 43.75\% with our earlier fixed FETCH table (listing A), Nsight
Systems splits a token of our cache (FreeToken) into
4.93 (5.20)\,ms of expert copies over PCIe, 2.29 (2.21) of resident-expert products, 1.65 (1.34) of attention
projections, 0.49 (0.37) of attention, 0.38 of output head and 0.97 (0.40) of norms, small kernels and cache
control; our host-side time is 0.27\,ms. In that configuration neither system overlaps its PCIe copies with GPU
compute; doing so would save 5--10\%, against the \shOvlGpuTokMin--\shOvlGpuTokMax\% of the token the accounting
charges to its overlap-and-rebalance fix at these cells: the rest of that term is the rebalancing. Our extra 0.5\,ms
of GPU work comes from unfused batch-1 kernels: we launch 1.6$\times$ as many.

\section{What the Traces Say}\label{sec:traces}
The accounting names foresight as the largest cost where the host binds. What would recover it, and what would not?
We replay the models' own sampled text for \fsModels{} MoE models~\cite{muennighoff2024olmoe,yang2025qwen3,openai2025gptoss}
(\polModels{} traces of 33{,}000--152{,}000 tokens, the cache carried across conversations) through an event-atomic
simulator: each token requests its $k$ experts per layer as one set, and no expert of the current token is evicted to
serve it, the accounting that cache-level studies of this problem call for~\cite{zhang2026reproducible}.

\input{tab_policy}

\paragraph{Policies.} \Cref{tab:policy} scores LRU, LFU, decayed frequency, ARC, S3-FIFO and a profiled static cache
against the exact optimum and against Belady within a window of $W$ future tokens, at three budgets per model. Three
things hold across models. First, the online policies are close to one another and far from the optimum: the best of
them reads \polBestOnlineMin--\polBestOnlineMax\% more than it, while decayed frequency is the best online policy
in \polDfBest{} of \polCells{} cells and S3-FIFO in \polSsfBest{} (within 1\% of the best in \polDfNear{} and
\polSsfNear{}). Where the cache turns over every token ($C/k\le1.5$,
\polLruLowCells{} cells) the six online policies are within \polLowSpreadMax\% of one another; from $C/k{=}2$ LRU reads
\polLruMoreMin--\polLruMoreMax\% more than decayed frequency on eight of nine models, Qwen2-57B being the exception,
and classic LFU fails at large budgets (\polLfuMax$\times$ the optimum). The literature's disagreement on recency
against frequency~\cite{si2026expertcache,flashmoe2026,mira2026,specmd} is consistent with this regime dependence. Second, a static
cache profiled on other text reads \polStaticMin--\polStaticMax$\times$ the optimum, because the hot set moves: the
$C$ most requested experts of a layer change by \driftHundredMin--\driftHundredMax\% between consecutive windows of
100 tokens and \driftThousandMin--\driftThousandMax\% between windows of 1{,}000 (median \driftThousandMed\%), more
over the longer window on \driftRisingModels{} of nine models, so popularity follows the conversation; the skew is
mild, the top quarter of experts taking \skewQuarterMin--\skewQuarterMax\% of requests. Third, four tokens of
foresight beat every online policy in every cell (median \polWfourEquarter$\times$ the optimum at $E/4$), so foresight
is worth several times the choice of policy. Our deployed hysteresis costs \polKappaMin--\polKappaMax\% reads over
plain decayed frequency (up to \polKappaQwenMax\% on Qwen3); it was tuned for time, where it rations
copies, not for reads.

\paragraph{How far ahead.} The optimum reads \fsSaveMin--\fsSaveMax\% fewer experts from host memory than our deployed
decayed-frequency policy (median \fsSaveMed\%). The lookahead needed scales with the cache's turnover time $C/k$, slots per layer over
experts per token: half of the gap closes at $W_{50}\approx\fsPre\,(C/k)^{\fsExp}$ tokens ($r{=}\fsR$ in log--log over
\fsFitN{} points; the exponent's 95\% interval over a bootstrap of models is \fsExpLo--\fsExpHi, its leave-one-model-out
range \fsExpLomoMin--\fsExpLomoMax, and without the \fsInterp{} points whose $W_{50}$ lies below one token it is
\fsExpNoInterp), and 90\% needs a median of \fsNinety$\,C/k$. Small caches ($C/k\le2$) recover half of the gap with
one or two tokens of foresight; gpt-oss-120b at 25\% ($C/k{=}8$) needs about \fsGptQuarter{} tokens, and at 50\%
about \fsGptHalf{} (both measured on its trace; the fit gives 9 and 24); in the engine, half of the hit-optimal oracle's
gain at gpt-oss 25\% arrived by $W{\approx}\fsmWfiftyGMid$ (\cref{sec:limit}). The law adds the condition: foresight is worth these reads only where the host binds; where the
bus is already saturated, as on the slow devices of~\cite{wisp2026,budgetingbytes2026}, it is worth nothing.
Predicting the next layer's experts, the usual form of prediction~\cite{hwang2024pregated,zhu2025preattention},
gives no cross-token foresight: it can raise the hit rate by prefetching but never lowers host bytes, which is what we
measured when we built it.

\paragraph{Speculation is not foresight.} Speculative decoding verifies $K$ draft tokens in one forward pass, so a
layer's experts for $K$ positions are requested together and a missed expert is read once for all of them, which
several systems propose as the source of foresight for a cache~\cite{spmoe,moespac2026,cascade2025}. On the same
traces, with $K$ positions per step and drafts accepted at rate $\alpha$ until the first rejection, batched
verification saves an online cache a median \bkFreeMin--\bkFreeMax\% of its reads across models at $K{=}8$ when
rejected drafts cost nothing (\bkFreeModelMax\% on the model that gains most), the equivalent of a window of two to
four tokens; once the rejected drafts' experts are counted, the saving
is \bkSameNineMin--\bkSameNineMax\% at $\alpha{=}0.9$ and becomes a loss of \bkSameEightLossMin--\bkSameEightLossMax\% at
$\alpha{=}0.8$, because the union of $K$ positions' expert sets is \bkUnionMin--\bkUnionMax\% of $Kk$, several times
$C$ (\cref{app:prereg} lists the variants). The optimum gains little from batching (\bkBeladyMin{} to
\bkBeladyMax\%). Foresight must come from knowing future routing, as a router decoupled from the
backbone~\cite{readme2024} or a forecaster~\cite{wang2026seqmoe} would provide, and $W_{50}$ says how far ahead.

small models at batch 1. Even so, against a latency-free datasheet ceiling the trace-scored measurements sit at a
median tenth, and three quarters of them below a fifth, of what their machines could deliver, and \cref{fig:shapley}
says what the missing part is made of.

\section{The Expert Cache as the Instrument}\label{sec:system}
\paragraph{Design.} Our cache is a 3{,}700-line patch to llama.cpp. Each MoE layer has $C$ GPU slots. Routing is
decided on the GPU; resident experts run there, and each miss either runs on CPU helper threads, which the GPU
signals through a mailbox in mapped memory, or is copied into a slot and run on the GPU (FETCH). Admission follows a
decayed-frequency score (half-life 16 tokens, an admission margin $\kappa{=}1$; a llama.cpp pull request admits by a
decayed heat with hysteresis in the same spirit~\cite{llamacpppr26563}) and copies in the background. Sampling runs
on the GPU. The cache serves single-token steps; prompts and batches above one run llama.cpp's stock graph with
every expert in host memory and pay for the resident experts and a one-thread CPU side
(\bbPrefillMin--\bbPrefillMax\% of stock llama.cpp's prefill speed, \bbBatchMin--\bbBatchMax\% of its decode at 2--4
sequences; \cref{app:system}). The one per-machine decision is the FETCH table $f(n)$: of a layer's $n$ missed
experts, how many to copy. A per-layer form of \cref{eq:law} gives it,
\begin{equation}
T(n,f)=\max\!\big(\tfrac{fS}{B_p}+g,\ [n{>}f](L_c+\tfrac{(n-f)S}{B_c}),\ [0{<}f{<}n]\tfrac{nS}{B_{cp}}\big),
\label{eq:split}
\end{equation}
where $g$ is the GPU's expert work per layer (48\,$\mu$s for Qwen3, 37\,$\mu$s for gpt-oss, from profiles) and
$L_c{=}20\,\mu$s the hand-off; the table picks the $f$ with the smallest $T$ and is computed from the probe when the
job starts. On listing B the law's table beats the fixed table we used before at every budget of both models, by
\lawGainMin--\lawGainMax\%; on hosts whose link we measured at 27--30\,GB/s instead of 53 it fetches less and gains
\hpGptLawMin--\hpGptLawMax\% on gpt-oss and \hpQwenLawMin--\hpQwenLawMax\% on Qwen3, the per-machine split mattering
most where the link is slow (\cref{app:system}, \cref{tab:split}).

\paragraph{Where the gain comes from.} \Cref{fig:ablation} builds the system up from stock llama.cpp on one machine.
Placing each layer's hot experts is the first gain: a static cache of the 32 experts per layer most
requested on other text (the models' own MATH-500 outputs) runs \ablStaticGpt$\times$ and \ablStaticQwen$\times$ stock,
which pins whole layers instead. Its hit rate (\ablStaticHitGpt\% and \ablStaticHitQwen\%) depends on how well the
profile matches the workload: profiled in hindsight on the measured problems it hits \ablHindHitGpt\% and
\ablHindHitQwen\% and runs faster (hatched). Replacing experts online closes that gap without a profile, if admissions
are rationed: LRU admits every miss, and on Qwen3 its copies raise host reads from \rdStaticQwen{} to \rdLruQwen{}

\section{Related Work}\label{sec:related}
\paragraph{Offloading systems.} Demand-paging systems cache experts per layer and copy misses over
PCIe~\cite{eliseev2023offloading,xue2024moeinfinity,song2024promoe,tang2024hobbit,pagingexperts2026}. CPU-execution systems run
non-resident experts on the host~\cite{kamahori2024fiddler,ktransformers,zhong2025hybrimoe,daop2025,wang2026cloudgrade};
a set-associative GPU cache with CPU-executed misses~\cite{huang2025cpugpu}, a llama.cpp fork~\cite{leloch2026,llamacppdisc24528}
and two llama.cpp pull requests~\cite{llamacpppr26563,llamacpppr27861} are the closest designs to ours, and
FreeToken~\cite{freetoken} and DALI~\cite{dali2026} split each layer's misses between CPU and PCIe, FreeToken from a
closed-form bandwidth model. Pipelined Sharding~\cite{pipelinedsharding2026} plans sub-layer placement under a VRAM
budget. Predictors guess upcoming experts from the previous layer's state~\cite{hwang2024pregated,zhu2025preattention},
several steps ahead with a learned forecaster~\cite{wang2026seqmoe,mira2026}, or a batch's routes across
layers~\cite{expertflow}; Read-ME~\cite{readme2024} makes routing known in advance by decoupling the router and then
caches in Belady's manner. Speculative decoding has been combined with expert offloading as a source of
batching~\cite{spmoe,moespac2026,cascade2025}. Batched-throughput systems~\cite{flexgen2023,cao2024moelightning,yuan2025moelens}
and SSD tiers~\cite{ssdllama,edgezero} are outside our single-request, host-DRAM scope.

\paragraph{Models, bounds and audits.} FreeToken's split and our \cref{eq:split} are bandwidth models of one layer;
\cref{eq:law} is a whole-token model tested blind across machines and against alternatives. Max-of-paths cost models
go back to FlexGen~\cite{flexgen2023} and MoE-Lightning~\cite{cao2024moelightning}; WiSP~\cite{wisp2026} bounds decode by
PCIe turnover, Budgeting Bytes~\cite{budgetingbytes2026} by bytes over bandwidth, and MoE-CAP~\cite{moecap} measures
bandwidth utilisation against a single peak. Qiu et al.~\cite{qiu2026gguf} predict llama.cpp throughput from GGUF
metadata for models held entirely in GPU or unified memory; Suram~\cite{cacheable} matches one offloaded configuration
to bytes per token over bandwidth; Si et al.~\cite{si2026expertcache} derive when a copy pays off and find recency
competitive with frequency at large budgets. The roofline~\cite{williams2009roofline} is the single-memory ancestor of
our bound. Zhang~\cite{zhang2026reproducible} proposes a trace-level evaluation contract for expert caching, decomposes
the online-to-optimal gap in cache blocks in one fixed order, and audits the reporting of ten papers; SeqMoE~\cite{wang2026seqmoe}
reports four systems against the all-in-VRAM speed. Trace studies of policies cover two or three models
each~\cite{zhang2026reproducible,si2026expertcache,flashmoe2026,specmd}. Shapley attribution of a performance gap has a
precedent in ShapleyIQ~\cite{shapleyiq2024}.

\paragraph{Lookahead caching.} Limited lookahead in paging goes back to Albers~\cite{albers1997lookahead}; Hasslinger et
al.~\cite{hasslinger2018} combine Belady within a window with LRU or LFU beyond it for web caches, the policy family we
use to measure the horizon.

\section{Limitations}\label{sec:limits}
\begin{itemize}\setlength\itemsep{1pt}
\item \textbf{Hardware.} Consumer cards (RTX 5090 for the headline comparisons, a 4090 and a 3090 at the cells that
fit 24\,GB; an RTX PRO 6000 for all-in-VRAM runs) and desktop Ryzen and Intel
hosts, rented; GPU clocks could not be locked, power limits differed (350--575\,W), and listing B's memory runs
\memClkRatio$\times$ faster than the other cards'; \cref{tab:headline} therefore reports a stock-clock host beside
it, where our lead is \scRatioDropMin--\scRatioDropMax{} smaller and FreeToken leads at one cell. A 12-channel server
CPU breaks the law's bandwidth assumption (\cref{sec:law}). Intervals cover problem sampling on one machine, not
machine-to-machine variance, which \cref{fig:ratio} shows across \ratioHostsN{} hosts.
\item \textbf{Workload.} Single-request decode; AIME prompts, which were also used while developing the system;
greedy decoding; 130--186-token prompts. Long outputs are measured on held-out problems. Prompts and batches above
one bypass the cache and run \bbPrefillMin--\bbPrefillMax\% and \bbBatchMin--\bbBatchMax\% of stock llama.cpp's
speed (\cref{sec:system}); speculative verification bypasses it and is not measured.
\item \textbf{Competitor settings.} FreeToken ran its faster backend per budget with its own bandwidth calibration,
default thread count and fetch cap, while our FETCH table was swept; its CPU backend failed on a hybrid-core host at its default
thread count and was rerun on the performance cores.
Each comparison uses one confirmation launch, except Qwen3 at 25\%, which is from the selection launch for both
systems.
\item \textbf{The bound, the accounting and the audit.} Exact routing only: expert skipping, substitution or
deferral change the model and fall outside it; a per-layer budget, which a pooled cache can beat by 5--10\%. Two of
the accounting's five terms are definitions (\cref{sec:limit}). The oracles read a recorded trace, not a predictor, on
two rented hosts with weak host memory, and their copies land about three steps late: they measure this engine's
foresight, not the limit's. The published rows use datasheet ceilings and, for
eleven of them, i.i.d. routing.
\item \textbf{Constants and outputs.} $G$, $g$ and $L_c$ are fitted once and frozen; the per-layer model overstates
differences between FETCH tables about 1.8$\times$. Output parity is measured by teacher-forced KL divergence, agreement and loss
against an all-VRAM reference, not by task accuracy.
\end{itemize}

\section{Conclusion}
On a consumer machine, MoE decode time is a fixed GPU term plus host-memory bytes over the rate of the paths that read
them. That law, predicted blind and tested against its alternatives, is accurate to a few percent on desktop hosts;
it tells a system which path to use for a miss on a given machine; and with the optimum's bytes it gives a speed
limit in seconds that published systems reach a median tenth of. An accounting that no order of fixes can bias
says what the rest is: where the host binds, the policy's ignorance of future routing, and where the GPU binds, host
reads that wait for it. Nine models' traces say that neither an online policy nor batching alone buys that
foresight; it must come from knowing the routing a few to tens of tokens ahead. Given it by an oracle, the engine
runs \fsbGainHighMin--\fsbGainHighMax\% faster, mostly on the bytes the limit's policy saves, and on the same host
\fspGainHighMin--\fspGainHighMax\% faster when the same foresight copies experts ahead of their use: the overlap the
accounting prices arrived only with foresight. Where the host binds hardest neither form gains more than
\fsbGainLowMaxAny\%.
