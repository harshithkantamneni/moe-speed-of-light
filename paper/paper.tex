\title{Where the Seconds Go:\\[2pt]\large A Speed Limit and a Measured Accounting for Mixture-of-Experts Decode\\ on One Consumer GPU}
\author{Harshith Kantamneni\\Independent Researcher}
\date{}

\begin{document}
\maketitle


\begin{abstract}
A Mixture-of-Experts model too large for one consumer GPU keeps most experts in host memory. How fast could it decode,
and where does the rest of the time go? We bound the time per token by the fewest expert reads any exact-routing policy
can make, Belady's optimum with bypass on the model's own routing, over the machine's host and GPU rates. On RTX 5090
hosts our llama.cpp expert cache reaches \bothLimitPctMin--\bothLimitPctMax\% of this limit, and published systems in
its class a median of \audInclassMed\% of theirs. Oracles of future routing inside the engine then measure where the
gap goes. On three hosts, foresight spent as the optimum spends it, admitting only what it admits and reading each
admitted expert once, is worth \fxFetchAllGainMin--\fxFetchAllGainMax\% at every budget, and
\fxPacedAllGainMin--\fxPacedAllGainMax\% with the overlap it allows; spent as caches usually spend it, by Belady
prefetch or by copying what the CPU has just served, it reads \fxUsualReadsOverOptMin--\fxUsualReadsOverOptMax$\times$ the
optimum's bytes, loses at the lowest budgets, and trails the optimum's way at every host-bound budget. Without foresight, a learned admission order fitted on
other text reads \lreFewerMin--\lreFewerMax\% fewer experts but runs \lreOverBaseMin--\lreOverBaseMax$\times$ the
deployed cache at the host-bound budgets; nine models' routing traces give the horizon a forecaster must reach.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}
Open-weight Mixture-of-Experts models now carry 30--120\,B parameters and activate a few billion per token; a desktop
GPU has 24--32\,GB. Running such a model at batch 1 therefore means keeping most experts in host DRAM, and each
decoded token reads its routed experts from one of three places: GPU memory, host memory by the CPU that runs the
expert, or host memory across PCIe into a GPU slot~\cite{ivanov2021datamovement,hoefler2021sparsity}. A line of systems
builds on this: GPU expert caches with demand fetch~\cite{eliseev2023offloading,xue2024moeinfinity,song2024promoe}, CPU
execution of misses~\cite{kamahori2024fiddler,ktransformers,zhong2025hybrimoe,huang2025cpugpu,daop2025,dali2026},
per-miss choices between the two~\cite{freetoken}, and expert prediction~\cite{hwang2024pregated,zhu2025preattention,wang2026seqmoe}.
Each reports a speed-up over a baseline of its choosing. None states how fast its machine \emph{could} decode the
model within the same GPU memory, or what the rest of the time is made of.

\paragraph{Thesis.} Where host memory binds, the time of offloaded decode is its host bytes per token over the host's
read rate. Belady's optimum with bypass (MIN) sets the fewest such bytes; the best online policy reads
\polBestOnlineMin--\polBestOnlineMax\% more on the routing traces, and our deployed cache, which reads its admissions
twice, \fxOnlineReadsOverOptMin--\fxOnlineReadsOverOptMax$\times$ as many in the engine. Knowing future routing
recovers that excess at every budget and on every host when it is spent as MIN spends it: admit what MIN admits, and
read each admitted expert from host memory once. Spent the usual ways, it pays at some budgets and loses at others. We establish this in three steps. A \emph{speed limit} in seconds
(\cref{sec:limit}) bounds every exact-routing system with $C$ expert slots per layer on a given machine.
\emph{Oracles} of future routing built into a real engine (\cref{sec:gap}) measure, on three hosts, two of them in
randomised order, what foresight is worth under each way of spending it, separating the bytes it saves, the overlap
it allows, the experts it admits that MIN would bypass, and the second read that copying a CPU-served miss costs.
\emph{Routing traces} of nine models (\cref{sec:traces}) say what would supply that foresight: no online policy does,
batched speculative verification does not, and a forecaster must see $W_{50}\approx\fsPre\,(C/k)^{\fsExp}$ tokens
ahead to recover half of it.

The method is Hoefler and Belli's~\cite{hoefler2015benchmarking}: bounds beside measurements, paired statistics, and
every prediction committed to a public repository before the machine it concerns was started (\cref{app:prereg}
scores all \scClauses{} clauses). We work on rented RTX 5090, 4090 and 3090 hosts with desktop CPUs, with
gpt-oss-120b~\cite{openai2025gptoss} and Qwen3-30B-A3B~\cite{yang2025qwen3}. One example runs through the paper:
gpt-oss-120b with 32 of each layer's 128 experts resident on an RTX 5090, a quarter of its expert bytes.
\Cref{tab:glossary} defines the terms.

\paragraph{Contributions.}
\begin{itemize}\setlength\itemsep{1pt}
\item \textbf{A speed limit in seconds} (\cref{sec:limit}) for exact-routing systems with $C$ slots per layer, from the
exact MIN on the model's routing and the machine's measured ceilings, varied four ways, stated for pooled caches, and
placed beside our cache, FreeToken, llama.cpp and \audAllN{} published measurements.
\item \textbf{A measured accounting of the gap} (\cref{sec:gap}): a factorial of oracle policies in the engine on
\amHostsN{} hosts, which measures the terms a model of the gap prices and finds the model wrong about their sizes:
at the host-bound cells the bytes foresight saves are \amBytesHostMin--\amBytesHostMax\% of the gap, the overlap it
allows \amOverlapHostMin--\amOverlapHostMax\%, and the rest, which no oracle touches, \amRestHostMin--\amRestHostMax\%.
\item \textbf{What would recover it} (\cref{sec:traces}): across nine models' traces, which online policy earns its
reads, how fast the hot set drifts, how far ahead foresight must see, and why speculative batching cannot supply it
once rejected drafts are paid for; and a learned admission order, fitted on other text with features the engine has
when it plans a step, which removes \lrnFetchHostMin--\lrnFetchHostMax\% of a single-read policy's excess reads
offline, and in the engine reads fewer experts but runs \lreOverBaseMin--\lreOverBaseMax$\times$ the deployed policy,
gaining only where host memory is weakest.
\end{itemize}
The instrument is our llama.cpp expert cache (\cref{sec:setting}); it runs \bothLlamaXMin--\bothLlamaXMax$\times$
stock llama.cpp and leads FreeToken~\cite{freetoken} at \bothLeadCells{} of \bothCells{} cells on two RTX 5090 hosts.
Running missed experts on the CPU~\cite{kamahori2024fiddler,ktransformers}, caching hot experts with CPU-executed
misses~\cite{zhong2025hybrimoe,huang2025cpugpu,llamacppdisc24528}, splitting a layer's misses between CPU and PCIe from a
bandwidth model~\cite{freetoken,dali2026}, bytes over bandwidth as a bound~\cite{budgetingbytes2026,wisp2026}, and
Belady with bypass as a cache-level oracle~\cite{zhang2026reproducible} are published; our cache uses the first three.

\section{Setting and Instrument}\label{sec:setting}
\paragraph{Workload.} Single-request decode. gpt-oss-120b (MXFP4 experts; 36 MoE layers of 128 experts, top-4,
13.25\,MB per expert) and Qwen3-30B-A3B in BF16 (48 layers of 128 experts, top-8, 9.44\,MB per expert). Prompts are
the 30 AIME-25 problems in FreeToken's own benchmark format; each system decodes 256 tokens per problem greedily,
after one warm-up request, in one session in which the cache carries over between problems. One client times every
system with FreeToken's own metric, (tokens$-$1) over the time between the first and last token's arrival.

\paragraph{Machines.} RTX 5090 (32\,GB, 1.79\,TB/s datasheet) hosts with desktop CPUs, rented by the hour and named
by a letter. \Cref{tab:headline} is measured on \emph{host B} (Ryzen 9 9950X3D; its card's memory clock is
\memClkRatio$\times$ the common one, and its probe read device memory 3\% above the datasheet rate) and \emph{host S}
(Ryzen 9 9950X at the common clock; its CPU reads host memory \scCpuBwDrop\% slower, its card has a lower power limit
and SM clock). The oracle measurements of \cref{sec:gap} use further rentals, O1--O5. Every job first runs a probe
that reads host memory from $t$ CPU threads, over PCIe, and from both at once: $B_c$ is the CPU rate at the helper
thread count, $B_p$ the PCIe rate, $B_{cp}$ the combined rate, and $B_{\mathrm{host}}$ the highest rate the probe
reached by any method. GPU clocks cannot be locked on these rentals (power limits 350--600\,W).

\paragraph{The instrument.} Our cache is a 3{,}500-line patch to llama.cpp. Each MoE layer has $C$ GPU slots. Routing
is decided on the GPU; resident experts run there, and each miss either runs on CPU helper threads, which the GPU
signals through a mailbox in mapped memory, or is fetched: copied into a slot and run on the GPU in the same step.
Admission follows a decayed-frequency score (half-life 16 tokens, margin $\kappa{=}1$; a llama.cpp pull request admits
by a decayed heat in the same spirit~\cite{llamacpppr26563}); an admitted miss is served by the CPU and then copied
into its slot in the background, so it is read from host memory twice. The one per-machine decision is how many of a
layer's $n$ misses to fetch. A model of time per token gives it. With $X_c$ the bytes the CPU reads per token and
$X_p$ the bytes the link reads,
\begin{equation}
T \;=\; G \;+\; \max\!\Big(\frac{X_c}{B_c},\ \frac{X_p}{B_p},\ \frac{X_c+X_p}{B_{cp}}\Big),
\label{eq:law}
\end{equation}
with one constant $G$ per engine and model, fitted once on one host and frozen; its per-layer form picks, for each
$n$, the number $f(n)$ of misses to fetch (\cref{app:law}). In the running example on host B the table copies one
of two misses and runs a single miss on the CPU, hidden behind the GPU's resident experts. Written down on each
machine from its probe before any model ran, \cref{eq:law} predicted our cache on gpt-oss within a median
\lawRowsMedian\% per measurement on \lawHosts{} RTX 5090 hosts (90th percentile \lawRowsPninety\%; \cref{tab:law}). A one-path
bytes-over-bandwidth model with the same $G$ has the same median but a 90th percentile of \lawNaivePninety\%: the
maximum over paths matters where both paths carry many bytes. With its constants frozen it scores \lawFrozenN{} later
measurements on \lawFrozenHosts{} more hosts at a median \lawFrozenMed\%, within \lawFrozenGptMin{} to
\lawFrozenGptMax\% on gpt-oss and over-predicting Qwen3's speed by \lawFrozenQwenMin{} to \lawFrozenQwenMax\%, whose
$G$ was borrowed from llama.cpp; on those later measurements the one-path model does as well (median
\lawFrozenNaiveMed\%, 90th percentile \lawFrozenNaivePninety{} against \lawFrozenPninety\%). It is a calibrated model of engines that read host memory in sequence with their GPU
work, and \cref{sec:gap} shows where it stops: it prices no overlap and no per-layer fetch latency.

\paragraph{Protocol.} We match the number of experts on the GPU across systems (within 2.5\%; llama.cpp keeps whole
layers, \texttt{--n-cpu-moe}~$n$, and FreeToken takes a cache rate and preallocates most of the card). Ratios are of
mean speeds, paired by problem, with 95\% percentile intervals from 10{,}000 bootstrap resamples; except in
\cref{sec:gap}, they cover problem sampling within one launch on one machine, not rentals (two rentals of one CPU
model differed by 19--22\% in speed). A first launch picks each system's faster variant per budget and a second
launch, which the comparison uses, runs only those. Every job's predictions are in its script's header, committed
before the machine starts. A model's error is its prediction over the measurement, minus one, of the quantity named,
speed or time per token; a model that over-predicts time under-predicts speed.

\paragraph{The instrument against its peers.} \Cref{tab:headline} compares three systems at six cells on two RTX 5090
hosts. On host B ours leads FreeToken at every budget, by \ftLeadGptMin--\ftLeadGptMax\% on gpt-oss and
\ftLeadQwenMin--\ftLeadQwenMax\% on Qwen3; on host S, with a slower CPU, it leads at \scOursLeadCells{} of six cells
and FreeToken leads at Qwen3 43.75\%. Tuned per cell on a third host (a Ryzen 9 9950X) over five settings (its fetch cap, its
thread count and its backend), FreeToken's best runs at most \fttBestOverDefaultMax$\times$ its \cref{tab:headline}
setting, and ours runs \fttMin--\fttMax$\times$ it, ahead at \fttLeadCells{} of six cells (job 098; \cref{app:system}).
FreeToken reserves \fttVramFtMin--\fttVramFtMax\,GiB of VRAM at every budget, ours uses \fttVramOursMin--\fttVramOursMax\,GiB.
With every expert on the CPU, stock llama.cpp, whose kernels our helpers use, runs \fttCpuGpt$\times$ (gpt-oss) and
\fttCpuQwen$\times$ (Qwen3) FreeToken's CPU backend, so CPU kernels explain none of the Qwen3 lead and at most part of
the gpt-oss one. Most of the lead is admission rationing: with LRU in place of decayed frequency
the cache runs \scLruSlowMin--\scLruSlowMax\% slower and trails FreeToken at \scLruTrailCells{} cells (\cref{app:system}).
Ours runs \grThreekLlamaMin--\grFourkLlamaMax$\times$ llama.cpp on an RTX 4090 and an RTX 3090, ties FreeToken on its
own headline model within 3\%, and ties llama.cpp on Mixtral-8x7B, where 2 of 8 experts resident leave little locality
to earn (\cref{app:grid}). Its outputs diverge from an all-VRAM reference as much as stock llama.cpp's own CPU
placement does (mean KL \klMeanGpt{} and \klMeanQwen{} nats; \cref{app:system}).

\section{The Speed Limit}\label{sec:limit}
\paragraph{Bound.} Consider any system that executes the model's exact routing with at most $C$ experts of each layer
resident on the GPU. Per token, each of the $Lk$ routed experts is either served by a resident copy, which the GPU
reads from VRAM, or not, in which case its $S$ bytes are read from host memory: by the CPU that runs it, or by the
copy that brings it into a slot. Let $R^\star$ be the reads per token of MIN, Belady's optimum with
bypass~\cite{belady1966study,hasslinger2018,bypassopt}, applied per layer to the routing trace: the fewest reads of any
policy, even one that knows the future. If $c$ experts per token run on the CPU, host memory supplies at least
$\max(R^\star,c)\,S$ bytes and the GPU reads the dense bytes $D$ plus $(Lk-c)\,S$. With everything overlapped,
\begin{equation}
\bar T \;\ge\; \min_{0\le c\le Lk}\ \max\!\Big(\frac{D+(Lk-c)S}{B_{\mathrm{gpu}}},\ \frac{\max(R^\star\!,c)\,S}{B_{\mathrm{host}}}\Big),
\label{eq:limit}
\end{equation}
with $B_{\mathrm{gpu}}$ the GPU's datasheet rate. A cell is \emph{host-bound} when the minimum is at $c^\star{=}0$,
and \emph{GPU-bound} otherwise: the GPU binds with every expert it can take. On host B gpt-oss at 11 and 25\% and
Qwen3 at 12.5 and 25\% are host-bound. The bound's class is exact routing, whole experts of unmodified bytes, at most
$C$ of each layer resident, and one token per forward pass. A system free to pool its $LC$ slots across layers has a
bound \poolOverLayerMin--\poolOverLayerMax\% higher; FreeToken keeps one LRU over all layers and llama.cpp pins whole
layers, so we score both against the pooled bound. The bound assumes an expert run on the CPU is read from DRAM (the
optimum's shortest re-read distance is \lthreeReuseMin\,GB of host traffic), that computation runs at memory speed,
and that the two read paths share $B_{\mathrm{host}}$ freely; it drops all latencies. $B_{\mathrm{host}}$ is the
highest rate our probe reached, not a hardware peak, so a faster reader could exceed it. The traces are each model's
own greedy text on the 30 prompts, with routing recorded teacher-forced; replayed through a simulator of our deployed
policy they reproduce the engine's hit rates within \simHitQwenMax{} points.

\paragraph{Varying it.} \Cref{tab:limit} changes one assumption at a time. Pooled slots need
\globalReadsMin--\globalReadsMax\% fewer reads and raise the host-bound limits by 5--10\%. Starting the optimum warm
changes it by at most 1.4\%; the median probe sample instead of the highest lowers the host-bound limits by 8\%. The
GPU term moves most: at batch 1 the all-in-VRAM runs reach only \gpuEffPctGpt\% (gpt-oss) and \gpuEffPctQwen\%
(Qwen3) of the datasheet rate, and with the measured rate the limits at 25\% and above fall to \limMeasGpuGpt{} and
\limMeasGpuQwen\,tok/s, 8--9\% above the all-in-VRAM speed: the CPU's share. In the running example the limit on
host B is \limExMidG\,tok/s (\limMeasGpuGpt{} with the measured GPU rate) and the cache runs 109\,tok/s.

\paragraph{Applying it.} Against the limit our cache stands at \bothLimitPctMin--\bothLimitPctMax\% on the two hosts
of \cref{tab:headline}, and at \limAllPctMin--\limAllPctMax\% with every variation of \cref{tab:limit} at once;
FreeToken and llama.cpp stand at \ftPoolPctMin--\ftPoolPctMax\% and \llPoolPctMin--\llPoolPctMax\% of the pooled bound
their designs allow. With every expert in its slots the cache matches stock llama.cpp (\cOneTwoEight$\times$ and
\cOneTwoEightQ$\times$): it costs nothing when the model fits. We also scored \audAllN{} published batch-1 measurements
from \audSources{} sources against a bound with the same ingredients at each paper's datasheet rates, in a per-layer
form that charges dense work in sequence (\texttt{scripts/audit.py}; every row in \cref{app:audit}). The
\audInclassN{} that are inside the bound's class and have a routing trace of their model reach a median
\audInclassMed\% of it (quartiles \audInclassQone--\audInclassQthree\%). The ceilings are datasheet ones and
\audImputedN{} rows' capacities are imputed upper bounds, so these are lower than a probed limit would give; even so,
three quarters of the in-class published systems stand below a quarter of a latency-free ceiling, and the next section asks what the
missing part is made of.

\section{Where the Seconds Go}\label{sec:gap}
Between a cell's measured time and its limit, \cref{eq:law,eq:limit} price five fixes; their Shapley values
(\cref{app:accounting}) name missing foresight across tokens the largest cost at host B's four host-bound cells,
\shFsHostMin--\shFsHostMax\% of the gap, and host reads serialised with GPU work the largest at its two GPU-bound
cells. That is a model, and the states it values in between are not realised by any online policy. This section
realises them: oracles of future routing built into the engine, on three hosts, measure what foresight is worth under
each way of spending it, and find the model right about which terms matter and wrong about their sizes.

\paragraph{The experiment.} Each oracle reads the routing of the rest of the sequence from a trace recorded on the same
machine over the same teacher-forced text, and the engine runs it beside the online policy at every cell
(\cref{fig:factorial}). On O4 (Ryzen 9 9950X) and O5 (Ryzen 9 9950X3D) the ten configurations of a cell ran in an
order shuffled per cell, each in a fresh context, with the predictions for both hosts committed before either started
(\cref{app:prereg}); a second launch on O4's machine (job 097), in another random order, reproduced the online,
single-read, fetch and paced-prefetch ratios within \fxReplicateMax. The configurations cross two choices a cache with foresight makes. What to admit: what MIN with
bypass admits, or every expert the coming steps will request, which is what a Belady prefetch does, admitting experts
MIN would bypass. How to read an admitted expert: once, by fetching it into its slot in the step that needs it or by
copying it ahead of its first use; or twice, by serving it on the CPU and then copying it, the way caches that execute
misses on the CPU admit, ours included. One more configuration reads the online policy's own admissions once, with no
foresight.

\paragraph{Spent as the optimum spends it, foresight pays.} MIN with bypass with each admission fetched once runs
\fxFetchFourMin--\fxFetchFourMax$\times$ the online policy at the four host-bound cells on O4 and
\fxFetchFiveMin--\fxFetchFiveMax$\times$ on O5, and gains \fxFetchAllGainMin--\fxFetchAllGainMax\% at every cell on
the three hosts. It reads \fxFetchReadsOverOptMin--\fxFetchReadsOverOptMax$\times$ $R^\star$, within \fxFetchSimDiffMax\% of what the
simulator predicts for it. With a paced single-read prefetch three steps ahead the gain is
\fxPacedAllGainMin--\fxPacedAllGainMax\% (\fxPacedFourMin--\fxPacedFourMax$\times$ on O4 and
\fxPacedFiveMin--\fxPacedFiveMax$\times$ on O5 at the host-bound cells), bringing the engine to
\fxBestFracMin--\fxBestFracMax\% of the limit there against the online policy's \fxBaseFracMin--\fxBaseFracMax\%.
Every interval is within $\pm$\fxHalfWidthMax. On both hosts the paced prefetching state beats fetch and fetch beats
the online policy with single reads at every host-bound cell; the sizes depend on the host. Fetch's ratio differs between
O4 and O5 by \fxFetchSpreadMin--\fxFetchSpreadMax{} (we had predicted at most 0.15), consistent with the in-step fetch
waiting on O5's slower link, though the two hosts also differ in their CPU.

\paragraph{Spent the usual ways, it pays less, and at the lowest budgets nothing.} Two habits waste it. The first is
over-admission: Belady prefetch admits what MIN bypasses and reads \fxNbReadsOverOptMin--\fxNbReadsOverOptMax$\times$
$R^\star$ even with each admission read once. The second is serve-then-copy: MIN with bypass whose admissions the CPU
serves and then copies reads \fxBypassReadsOverOptMin--\fxBypassReadsOverOptMax$\times$ $R^\star$. Their value depends on
the budget and the host. At the two lowest budgets the best of the usual ways (Belady prefetch read once or twice, paced
or not, and serve-then-copy MIN) runs \fxUsualLowMin--\fxUsualLowMax$\times$ the online policy on O4 and O5, against
\fxFetchLowMin--\fxFetchLowMax$\times$ for MIN fetched once; on O5 the best of them runs \fxUsualFiveMin--\fxUsualFiveMax$\times$
at the four host-bound cells. At the two 25\% cells on O4 they gain more (\fxUsualQuarterFourMin--\fxUsualQuarterFourMax$\times$,
the paced Belady prefetch once beating MIN fetched in the step), as the paced Belady prefetch did on O1 and O2
(\cref{app:foresight}). At every host-bound cell on O3--O5, MIN with its paced single-read prefetch beats the best of the
usual ways by \fxMinOverUsualMin--\fxMinOverUsualMax$\times$. Reading Belady's admissions a second time costs little at
gpt-oss (the two forms are within \fxNbOverHitGptMin--\fxNbOverHitGptMax$\times$ of each other) and a factor of
\fxNbOverHitQlowMin--\fxNbOverHitQlowMax{} at Qwen3 12.5\%, where admissions are most frequent. The online policy's own
second read, by contrast, costs it little: at the host-bound cells MIN admits \fxMinAdmitsOverOnlineMin--\fxMinAdmitsOverOnlineMax{}
times as many experts per token as the online policy, whose \fxOnlineAdmitsMin--\fxOnlineAdmitsMax{} admissions per token
the engine rations, and reading those once removes \fxFoaFewerReadsHostMin--\fxFoaFewerReadsHostMax\% of its host reads but
moves its speed by only \fxFoaHostMin--\fxFoaHostMax$\times$, consistent with their background copies overlapping later
steps. The online policy's time goes to not knowing the future, and knowing it pays at every budget only when spent on
what MIN admits, each admission read once.

\paragraph{The accounting, measured.} With the limit, the states online, fetch and the faster prefetching state divide
the gap at the host-bound cells of O3--O5 into the bytes foresight saves, \amBytesHostMin--\amBytesHostMax\% of it; the
overlap foresight allows, \amOverlapHostMin--\amOverlapHostMax\%; and the rest, which no oracle touches,
\amRestHostMin--\amRestHostMax\% (\cref{tab:accounting_measured}). On O4 and O5, where the online policy also ran with
single reads, reading its admissions once accounts for \amSingleHostMin{} to \amSingleHostMax\% of the gap and foresight
for \amFsGivenSingleHostMin--\amFsGivenSingleHostMax\%. What the oracles recover, bytes and overlap together, is most of
the gap on O3 and O4 (\amRecovThreeMin--\amRecovThreeMax\% and \amRecovFourMin--\amRecovFourMax\%); on O5, with its
slow link, the rest is the larger share (\amRestFiveMin--\amRestFiveMax\%). The split between bytes and overlap depends on the order in which they are taken: taken overlap first, with the
Belady prefetch standing for overlap at about the online policy's bytes, the overlap share ranges from
\amOvlFirstHostMin{} to \amOvlFirstHostMax\% (it is negative where that prefetch loses) and the bytes share from
\amBytesGivenOvlHostMin{} to \amBytesGivenOvlHostMax\%; only their sum, \amRecovHostMin--\amRecovHostMax\%, and the rest are
free of the order. Against the first order, the model over-prices the
bytes \amBytesMultMin--\amBytesMultMax$\times$ and the overlap \amOverlapMultMin--\amOverlapMultMax$\times$ and
under-prices the rest \amRestMultMin--\amRestMultMax$\times$. We did not separate the rest, but three candidates are
visible. The prefetch is imprecise: on O3 its reads are \fsbpReadsOptMin--\fsbpReadsOptMax$\times$ $R^\star$ across the
six cells, the cost of choosing victims steps early and of the misses that fall inside the lead. The overlap is
partial: the law, which charges every host read in sequence with the GPU's work, over-predicts the prefetching state's
time by \fxPacedLawTimeMin{} to \fxPacedLawTimeMax\% at the host-bound cells, so some reads overlap and some do not.
And the GPU runs at \gpuEffPctGpt--\gpuEffPctQwen\% of the datasheet rate the limit counts, a large part of what remains
at the GPU-bound cells: on Qwen3 at 43.75\% Nsight Systems splits a token of our cache (FreeToken) into 4.93 (5.20)\,ms
of expert copies over PCIe, 2.29 (2.21) of resident-expert products, 1.65 (1.34) of attention projections, 0.49 (0.37)
of attention, 0.38 of output head and 0.97 (0.40) of norms, small kernels and cache control, with neither system
overlapping its copies with GPU compute. Two things the model prices as overlap are worth nothing here: turning off
the within-layer overlap of a layer's CPU misses with its GPU hits changes speed by under 1\% at every cell, and
predicting the next layer's experts~\cite{hwang2024pregated,zhu2025preattention} never lowers host bytes. Foresight
across tokens is the lever; on O1, half of the Belady prefetch's gain at gpt-oss 25\% arrived between windows of 4 and
16 tokens (\fsmWfiftyGMid{} by log interpolation), the horizon \cref{sec:traces} finds on the traces.

\section{What the Traces Say}\label{sec:traces}
The accounting names foresight across tokens as the cost the oracles can remove where the host binds.
What would recover it, and what would not? We replay the models' own sampled text for \fsModels{} MoE
models~\cite{muennighoff2024olmoe,yang2025qwen3,openai2025gptoss} (\polModels{} traces of 33{,}000--152{,}000 tokens,
the cache carried across conversations) through an event-atomic simulator: each token requests its $k$ experts per
layer as one set, and no expert of the current token is evicted to serve it, the accounting that cache-level studies
of this problem call for~\cite{zhang2026reproducible}.

\input{tab_policy}

\paragraph{Policies.} \Cref{tab:policy} scores LRU, LFU, decayed frequency, ARC, S3-FIFO and a profiled static cache
against the exact optimum and against Belady within a window of $W$ future tokens, at three budgets per model. Three
things hold across models. First, the online policies are close to one another and far from the optimum: the best of
them reads \polBestOnlineMin--\polBestOnlineMax\% more than it, while decayed frequency is the best online policy
in \polDfBest{} of \polCells{} cells and S3-FIFO in \polSsfBest{} (within 1\% of the best in \polDfNear{} and
\polSsfNear{}). Where the cache turns over every token ($C/k\le1.5$, \polLruLowCells{} cells) the six online policies
are within \polLowSpreadMax\% of one another; from $C/k{=}2$ LRU reads \polLruMoreMin--\polLruMoreMax\% more than
decayed frequency on eight of nine models, Qwen2-57B being the exception, and classic LFU fails at large budgets
(\polLfuMax$\times$ the optimum). The literature's disagreement on recency against
frequency~\cite{si2026expertcache,flashmoe2026,mira2026,specmd} is consistent with this regime dependence. Second, a
static cache profiled on other text reads \polStaticMin--\polStaticMax$\times$ the optimum, because the hot set
moves: the $C$ most requested experts of a layer change by \driftHundredMin--\driftHundredMax\% between consecutive
windows of 100 tokens and \driftThousandMin--\driftThousandMax\% between windows of 1{,}000 (median
\driftThousandMed\%), so popularity follows the conversation; the skew is mild, the top quarter of experts taking
\skewQuarterMin--\skewQuarterMax\% of requests. Third, four tokens of foresight beat every online policy in every cell
(median \polWfourEquarter$\times$ the optimum at $E/4$), so foresight is worth several times the choice of policy.
Hysteresis as the trace study sets it ($\kappa{=}k/4$: 1 for gpt-oss, 2 for Qwen3; the engine runs $\kappa{=}1$) costs
\polKappaMin--\polKappaMax\% reads over plain decayed frequency on eight models and up to \polKappaQwenMax\% on Qwen3;
it is tuned for time, where it rations copies, not for reads.

\paragraph{How far ahead.} The optimum reads \fsSaveMin--\fsSaveMax\% fewer experts from host memory than decayed
frequency with each admitted miss fetched once, the single-read form of our policy (median \fsSaveMed\%; against
the serve-then-copy form, which reads its admissions twice, the saving is larger). The lookahead needed scales with the cache's turnover time $C/k$, slots per layer over
experts per token: half of the gap closes at $W_{50}\approx\fsPre\,(C/k)^{\fsExp}$ tokens ($r{=}\fsR$ in log--log over
\fsFitN{} points; the exponent's 95\% interval over a bootstrap of models is \fsExpLo--\fsExpHi, its leave-one-model-out
range \fsExpLomoMin--\fsExpLomoMax, and without the \fsInterp{} points whose $W_{50}$ lies below one token it is
\fsExpNoInterp), and 90\% needs a median of \fsNinety$\,C/k$. Small caches ($C/k\le2$) recover half of the gap with
one or two tokens of foresight; gpt-oss-120b at 25\% ($C/k{=}8$), the running example, needs about \fsGptQuarter{}
tokens, and at 50\% about \fsGptHalf{} (both measured on its trace; the fit gives 9 and 23). A prefetching system
needs that horizon plus the lead of its copy path, two to five steps in ours. The law adds the condition: fewer
reads are worth most where host memory is the binding term, and a system that streams every expert through a
saturated bus each token, as on the devices of~\cite{wisp2026,budgetingbytes2026}, has no slots to fill and nothing
to foresee.

\paragraph{Foresight from history.} No forecaster here sees $W_{50}$ tokens ahead, but part of the saving needs none.
We trained a logistic model of whether an expert is requested within the next $C/k$ tokens on features the engine has
when it plans a step: decayed request counts at five half-lives, the time since the last request, whether the expert is
requested now, and transition scores from the previous token's experts at the same layer and, through an 8-bit table,
at every layer. It is fitted on each model's mixed-domain trace and replayed on the AIME routing the engine runs, so
no test text is seen in training. Ordering admissions and victims by it, with each admitted expert read once, reads
\lrnFewerFetchHostMin--\lrnFewerFetchHostMax\% fewer experts than decayed frequency with the same single-read
admission at the four host-bound cells, closing \lrnFetchHostMin--\lrnFetchHostMax\% of that policy's excess over
MIN (\cref{tab:learned}; \lrnNoCrossFetchHostMin--\lrnNoCrossFetchHostMax\% without the cross-layer scores), and at
the \lrnBeatsWOne{} cells with $C/k\ge7$ it reads less than Belady with one token of perfect foresight. It still
reads \lrnOverOptMin--\lrnOverOptMax$\times$ MIN at the host-bound cells. The replay, the model's weights and a gate for
measuring it in the engine were committed before any engine run of it (\texttt{prereg/learned\_gate\_097.md}). In the
engine (job 097, predictions committed before launch) it reads \lreFewerMin--\lreFewerMax\% fewer experts than the
online policy with single reads at the host-bound cells, on both hosts, but its speed depends on the host. On O4's
machine (host memory \lreBestFour\,GB/s at best) it runs \lreOverFoaFourMin--\lreOverFoaFourMax$\times$ the single-read
online policy and \lreOverBaseFourMin--\lreOverBaseFourMax$\times$ the deployed one; on host O6, a Ryzen 9 9950X3D whose
host memory reads \lreBestSix\,GB/s, it runs \lreOverFoaSixMin--\lreOverFoaSixMax$\times$ and
\lreOverBaseSixMin--\lreOverBaseSixMax$\times$, slower. The reads it saves are CPU reads; it buys them with
\lreFetchMoreFourMin--\lreFetchMoreSixMax$\times$ as many fetches in the step, each a copy its layer waits on, and with
\lreUsMin--\lreUsMax\,$\mu$s of host time per token for its logits, a trade that paid on the host with the weaker
host memory and lost on the stronger one. A
realisable order from routing history is worth at most a few percent here, and nothing on a stronger host; the value
\cref{sec:gap} measures needs foresight.

\paragraph{Speculation is not foresight.} Speculative decoding verifies $K$ draft tokens in one forward pass, so a
layer's experts for $K$ positions are requested together and a missed expert is read once for all of them, which
several systems propose as the source of foresight for a cache~\cite{spmoe,moespac2026,cascade2025}. On the same
traces, with $K$ positions per step and drafts accepted at rate $\alpha$ until the first rejection, batched
verification saves an online cache a median \bkFreeMin--\bkFreeMax\% of its reads across models at $K{=}8$ when
rejected drafts cost nothing (\bkFreeModelMax\% on the model that gains most), the equivalent of a window of two to
four tokens; once the rejected drafts' experts are counted, the saving
is \bkSameNineMin--\bkSameNineMax\% at $\alpha{=}0.9$ and becomes a loss of \bkSameEightLossMin--\bkSameEightLossMax\% at
$\alpha{=}0.8$, because the union of $K$ positions' expert sets is \bkUnionMin--\bkUnionMax\% of $Kk$ across models, budgets and
variants (\cref{app:prereg} lists the variants). The optimum gains little from batching (\bkBeladyMin{} to
\bkBeladyMax\%). Foresight must come from knowing future routing, as a router decoupled from the
backbone~\cite{readme2024} or a forecaster~\cite{wang2026seqmoe} would provide, and $W_{50}$ says how far ahead.

\section{Related Work}\label{sec:related}
\paragraph{Offloading systems.} Demand-paging systems cache experts per layer and copy misses over
PCIe~\cite{eliseev2023offloading,xue2024moeinfinity,song2024promoe,tang2024hobbit,pagingexperts2026}. CPU-execution systems run
non-resident experts on the host~\cite{kamahori2024fiddler,ktransformers,zhong2025hybrimoe,daop2025,wang2026cloudgrade};
a set-associative GPU cache with CPU-executed misses~\cite{huang2025cpugpu}, a llama.cpp fork~\cite{leloch2026,llamacppdisc24528}
and two llama.cpp pull requests~\cite{llamacpppr26563,llamacpppr27861} are the closest designs to ours, and
FreeToken~\cite{freetoken} and DALI~\cite{dali2026} split each layer's misses between CPU and PCIe, FreeToken from a
closed-form bandwidth model. Pipelined Sharding~\cite{pipelinedsharding2026} plans sub-layer placement under a VRAM
budget. Predictors guess upcoming experts from the previous layer's state~\cite{hwang2024pregated,zhu2025preattention},
several steps ahead with a learned forecaster~\cite{wang2026seqmoe,mira2026}, or a batch's routes across
layers~\cite{expertflow}; Read-ME~\cite{readme2024} makes routing known in advance by decoupling the router and then
caches in Belady's manner. Speculative decoding has been combined with expert offloading as a source of
batching~\cite{spmoe,moespac2026,cascade2025}. Batched-throughput systems~\cite{flexgen2023,cao2024moelightning,yuan2025moelens}
and SSD tiers~\cite{ssdllama,edgezero} are outside our single-request, host-DRAM scope.

\paragraph{Models, bounds and audits.} FreeToken's split and our \cref{eq:split} are bandwidth models of one layer;
\cref{eq:law} is a whole-token model tested blind across machines and against alternatives. Max-of-paths cost models
go back to FlexGen~\cite{flexgen2023} and MoE-Lightning~\cite{cao2024moelightning}; WiSP~\cite{wisp2026} bounds decode by
PCIe turnover, Budgeting Bytes~\cite{budgetingbytes2026} by bytes over bandwidth, and MoE-CAP~\cite{moecap} measures
bandwidth utilisation against a single peak. Qiu et al.~\cite{qiu2026gguf} predict llama.cpp throughput from GGUF
metadata for models held entirely in GPU or unified memory; Suram~\cite{cacheable} matches one offloaded configuration
to bytes per token over bandwidth; Si et al.~\cite{si2026expertcache} derive when a copy pays off and find recency
competitive with frequency at large budgets. The roofline~\cite{williams2009roofline} is the single-memory ancestor of
our bound. Zhang~\cite{zhang2026reproducible} proposes a trace-level evaluation contract for expert caching, decomposes
the online-to-optimal gap in cache blocks in one fixed order, and audits the reporting of ten papers; SeqMoE~\cite{wang2026seqmoe}
reports four systems against the all-in-VRAM speed. Trace studies of policies cover two or three models
each~\cite{zhang2026reproducible,si2026expertcache,flashmoe2026,specmd}. Shapley attribution of a performance gap has a
precedent in ShapleyIQ~\cite{shapleyiq2024}. The choice between prefetching what the optimum would bypass and
admitting conservatively is the integrated prefetching-and-caching trade-off of Cao et al.~\cite{cao1995prefetching},
whose offline schedules are near-optimal for one and several disks~\cite{kimbrel2000prefetching,albers2000stall}; our
oracles re-derive it in this setting. Learned replacement imitates Belady from past accesses in CPU
caches~\cite{jain2016hawkeye} and content-delivery caches~\cite{song2020lrb}, and FlashMoE~\cite{flashmoe2026} learns a
recency--frequency blend for an expert cache on SSD; \cref{sec:traces} measures what such an order recovers of the
optimum's saving once each admitted expert is read once. Limited lookahead in paging goes back to Albers~\cite{albers1997lookahead};
Hasslinger et al.~\cite{hasslinger2018} combine Belady within a window with LRU or LFU beyond it for web caches, the
policy family we use to measure the horizon.

\section{Limitations}\label{sec:limits}
\begin{itemize}\setlength\itemsep{1pt}
\item \textbf{Hardware.} Consumer cards (RTX 5090 for the headline comparisons, a 4090 and a 3090 at the cells that
fit 24\,GB; an RTX PRO 6000 for all-in-VRAM runs) and desktop Ryzen and Intel hosts, rented; GPU clocks could not be
locked, power limits differed (350--600\,W), and host B's card reads device memory 3\% above the datasheet rate,
where the common-clock cards read below it; \cref{tab:headline} therefore reports host S beside it, where our lead is \scRatioDropMin--\scRatioDropMax{}
smaller and FreeToken leads at one cell. A 12-channel server CPU breaks the law's bandwidth assumption
(\cref{app:law}). Intervals cover problem sampling on one machine, not machine-to-machine variance, which
\cref{fig:ratio} shows across \ratioHostsN{} hosts; the oracle factorial ran on three hosts, one launch each; fetch's
gain differs between O4 and O5 by up to \fxFetchSpreadMax{} and other states' by up to \fxSpreadAnyMax.
\item \textbf{Workload.} Single-request decode; AIME prompts, which were also used while developing the system;
greedy decoding; prompts of 52--879 tokens (median 130 for Qwen3, 186 for gpt-oss). Long outputs are measured on held-out problems. Prompts and batches above
one bypass the cache and run \bbPrefillMin--\bbPrefillMax\% and \bbBatchMin--\bbBatchMax\% of stock llama.cpp's
speed; speculative verification bypasses it and is not measured.
\item \textbf{Competitor settings.} \Cref{tab:headline} runs FreeToken's faster backend per budget with its own
bandwidth calibration, default thread count and fetch cap; tuned over five settings on another host (job 098) it gains
at most \fttBestOverDefaultMax$\times$. Its CPU backend failed on a hybrid-core host at its default thread count and
was rerun on the performance cores. Each comparison uses one confirmation launch,
except Qwen3 at 25\%, which is from the selection launch for both systems.
\item \textbf{The bound, the accounting and the audit.} Exact routing only: expert skipping, substitution or
deferral change the model and fall outside it; a per-layer budget, which a pooled cache can beat by 5--10\%. Two of
the model's five terms are definitions (\cref{app:accounting}). The oracles read a recorded trace, not a predictor;
they measure what this engine does with foresight, and their prefetch still reads \fsbpReadsOptMin--\fsbpReadsOptMax$\times$
the optimum's bytes. The published rows use datasheet ceilings and, for eleven of them, i.i.d. routing.
\item \textbf{Constants and outputs.} $G$, $g$ and $L_c$ are fitted once and frozen; the per-layer model overstates
differences between FETCH tables about 1.8$\times$. Output parity is measured by teacher-forced KL divergence,
agreement and loss against an all-VRAM reference, not by task accuracy.
\end{itemize}

\section{Conclusion}
On a consumer machine, the optimum's host bytes over the probed host rate and the datasheet GPU rate give a speed
limit in seconds; our cache reaches \bothLimitPctMin--\bothLimitPctMax\% of it, and published systems in its class a
median of \audInclassMed\% of a datasheet version. A calibrated host-memory model, predicted blind, places an engine in
the limit's coordinates and picks the path for a miss on a given machine. Where the host binds, the piece of the gap
that knowing future routing removes is recovered at every budget, on every host we measured, only when that knowledge
is spent as the optimum spends it, admitting what MIN with bypass admits and reading each admitted expert once: so
spent, it runs our cache \fxFetchAllGainMin--\fxFetchAllGainMax\% faster on the bytes alone and
\fxPacedAllGainMin--\fxPacedAllGainMax\% faster with the overlap it allows. Spent as a Belady prefetch or through
serve-then-copy admission, it reads \fxUsualReadsOverOptMin--\fxUsualReadsOverOptMax$\times$ the optimum's bytes, gains
at some budgets and loses at the lowest, and trails the optimum's way at every host-bound cell. The online policy's own
second read is not where its time goes. What remains after foresight is spent well is \amRestHostMin--\amRestHostMax\% of the
gap, which we did not separate: the prefetch's excess reads, reads that do not overlap, and GPU kernels at half the
datasheet rate. Nine models' traces say that neither a hand-built online policy nor speculative batching buys that
foresight, that a learned order fitted on other text removes \lrnFetchHostMin--\lrnFetchHostMax\% of a single-read
policy's excess reads, and that in the engine such an order is worth \lreOverBaseMin--\lreOverBaseMax$\times$, so the rest must come from knowing the routing a few to tens of tokens ahead; $W_{50}$ says
how many.
