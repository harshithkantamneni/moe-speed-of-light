\author{Harshith Kantamneni\\Independent Researcher}
\date{}

\begin{abstract}
Running a large Mixture-of-Experts (MoE) model on one consumer GPU means keeping most experts in host memory, and dozens of systems now claim faster decoding in this setting, often against llama.cpp. These claims are stated as speed-ups over baselines whose configuration is rarely reported, and the evaluation contracts proposed so far count cache blocks, not seconds. We build the missing yardstick and use it.
\textbf{(1)} A lower bound on batch-1 decode time, in seconds, for any policy that executes the model's exact routing within a per-layer or a shared all-layer GPU expert budget, computed from exact routing traces.
\textbf{(2)} A bytes-over-bandwidth decode model, validated out of sample on \nRows{} third-party measurements from \nSources{} sources (\cvMedian\% median error, leave-one-group-out) and tested on pre-registered first-party and anchor runs, where its transferred constants proved conservative in the offloaded regime; calibrated with two runs, it predicted \hFiveN{} configurations on a new platform with \hFiveMed\% median error, as registered.
\textbf{(3)} An audit of \auditRows{} published measurements from \auditSystems{} systems: after an independent re-check against every source, \auditAdj{} are adjudicable. None of the \auditLlamaPapers{} papers with a llama.cpp baseline configured its partial expert offload (\texttt{--n-cpu-moe}); \auditWeak{} of \auditLlamaRows{} adjudicated llama.cpp baselines fall below the band of the model's equal-memory prediction, and \auditSurvive{} of \auditAdj{} claimed gains survive against it. The median adjudicated system reaches \auditMedianSol\% of the physical speed-of-light.
\textbf{(4)} A trace-provenance study on \provNAll{} models: routing traces taken by teacher-forcing dataset text, the norm in this literature, overstate cache hit rates at small budgets against the models' own generations on every model (by \provDropMin--\provDropMax{} points on the \provNModels{} pre-registered models, by up to \provExtDropMax{} on the audit's models, two of which cross our registered threshold for a changed conclusion), while the bound on our platform does not move; and for gpt-oss-120b, dataset text without the model's own reasoning is off-distribution (NLL \oneTwentyVz{} vs \oneTwentyVone{} nats with its reasoning inserted), reproduced in three implementations.
We also report our own measurement mistakes, and turn all of it into a time-level reporting contract.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}
Batch-1 decode of an MoE model with experts in host memory is a data-movement problem: every token reads the routed experts' weights from wherever they are, GPU memory, host DRAM or across PCIe~\cite{ivanov2021datamovement,hoefler2021sparsity}. The systems literature has answered it with mechanisms: GPU expert caches with demand fetch~\cite{eliseev2023offloading,xue2024moeinfinity,song2024promoe}, CPU execution of misses~\cite{kamahori2024fiddler,ktransformers,zhong2025hybrimoe,huang2025cpugpu,freetoken}, expert prediction~\cite{hwang2024pregated,zhu2025preattention,wang2026seqmoe}, speculative decoding with prefetch~\cite{spmoe,moespeq}. They report speed-ups over baselines of their choosing (median \auditClaimMedian$\times$ and up to \auditClaimMax$\times$ in the rows we audit); \auditLlamaPapers{} of the \auditPaperSystems{} papers we audit include llama.cpp~\cite{llamacpp}, the engine desktop users actually run.

Two things are missing. First, a ceiling: no paper we found states how fast its hardware \emph{could} decode the model within the same GPU memory, so a 2$\times$ speed-up cannot be told apart from closing half the gap or a tenth of it. Second, baseline strength: llama.cpp can place experts per layer (\texttt{--n-cpu-moe}, since mid-2025) as well as whole layers (\texttt{-ngl}), and the difference at equal memory is large. The most careful methodology paper in this area proposes a trace-level evaluation contract but works in cache blocks and explicitly leaves ``absolute transferred bytes per output token together with the bandwidth budget'' unfilled~\cite{zhang2026reproducible}.

This paper fills that field. We state the bound in seconds, validate the model that computes it three ways, and use it to re-express published results as fractions of what their hardware allows, next to the baseline they should have run. Following Hoefler and Belli~\cite{hoefler2015benchmarking}, we pre-registered hypotheses and predictions before measuring, and we report where they failed (\cref{app:prereg}) and the mistakes in our own earlier measurements that the process exposed (\cref{sec:pitfalls}).

\paragraph{Contributions.}
\begin{itemize}\setlength\itemsep{1pt}
\item \textbf{A speed-of-light in seconds} (\cref{sec:bound}) for any exact-routing policy with a per-layer or a shared all-layer expert budget, static or dynamic, demand or prefetching, with misses fetched or executed on the CPU, computed from exact routing traces taken without a GPU-resident model (\cref{sec:traces}), on \provNArch{} architectures.
\item \textbf{A three-way validation of the decode model} (\cref{sec:validation}): third-party measurements, pre-registered first-party runs, and pre-registered anchors.

\item \textbf{A reporting contract in seconds} (\cref{sec:contract}), informed by the pitfalls we hit (\cref{sec:pitfalls}).
\end{itemize}

\section{Background and Related Work}\label{sec:related}
\paragraph{Offloading systems.} Demand-paging systems keep a per-layer GPU cache and copy misses over PCIe~\cite{eliseev2023offloading,xue2024moeinfinity,song2024promoe,tang2024hobbit}. CPU-execution systems run non-resident experts on the host~\cite{kamahori2024fiddler,ktransformers,wang2026cloudgrade,zhong2025hybrimoe,huang2025cpugpu}, and FreeToken chooses per miss between the two with a closed-form split~\cite{freetoken}. Predictors guess upcoming experts from the previous layer's state~\cite{hwang2024pregated}, from pre-attention activations~\cite{zhu2025preattention}, or several steps and layers ahead~\cite{wang2026seqmoe}; speculative systems prefetch the experts a draft will need~\cite{spmoe,moespeq}. Batched-throughput systems~\cite{cao2024moelightning,yuan2025moelens} and SSD tiers~\cite{ssdllama,edgezero} are outside our scope (single request, host DRAM).

\paragraph{Evaluation methodology.} Zhang~\cite{zhang2026reproducible} shows that replay semantics, workload contamination and operating regime change which caching policy wins, and proposes a trace-level contract with a Belady gap; every result there is a block count, and it declines to relabel published speed-ups. MoE-CAP~\cite{moecap} defines sparsity-aware bandwidth utilisation against a single peak, which is ill-defined when expert bytes come from three memories. ``Cacheable by Design?''~\cite{cacheable} matches bytes over bandwidth to one configuration. Si et al.~\cite{si2026expertcache} derive the reuse count at which a copy pays off. Qiu et al.~\cite{qiu2026gguf} predict single-sequence llama.cpp throughput from GGUF metadata with roofline-shaped models (13--36\% error on three hosts), for models run fully on one device; our model covers the offloaded configurations and serves as the base of a bound. Hoefler and Belli's rules~\cite{hoefler2015benchmarking}, in particular ``show upper performance bounds'', frame our approach; the roofline~\cite{williams2009roofline} is its single-memory ancestor.

\paragraph{Our earlier system.} We previously built a GPU expert cache with CPU-executed misses into llama.cpp. A novelty check afterwards found the mechanism taken~\cite{zhong2025hybrimoe,huang2025cpugpu,freetoken,ktransformers,llamacppdisc24528}; we keep it here only as a system under test (\cref{sec:casestudy}).

\section{Exact Routing Traces}\label{sec:traces}
In a causal decoder the hidden state of token $t$ depends only on tokens $1..t$, so the experts that decode selects for token $t$ are those that teacher-forced prefill selects at position $t$, up to floating-point ties. Prefill lets us invert the loop order: load one decoder layer, push the whole corpus through it, record the router's top-$k$, free the layer. Weights are read once per corpus and peak memory is one layer, so a 2-vCPU VM traces a 120B model by reading tensors over HTTP range requests, and a GPU traces a corpus in minutes.

\paragraph{Exactness.} On tiny random checkpoints of \provNArch{} architectures (Qwen3-MoE, Qwen2-MoE, OLMoE, DeepSeek-V2, Mixtral, PhiMoE, gpt-oss with MXFP4 and bf16 experts), the collector's selections equal those of the reference \texttt{transformers} model run as prefill \emph{and} as token-by-token decode with a KV cache, and the per-token loss agrees within $10^{-3}$ nats, on CPU and on GPU. On real models, fp32 traces taken on a GPU differ from CPU traces in at most \gpuCpuMaxPct\% of expert selections (near-ties), and on the real Mixtral-8x7B, Phi-3.5-MoE and Qwen2-57B checkpoints the stock \texttt{transformers} model, run whole on a CPU, picks the same experts as the collector on a GPU for all but \refCheckMaxPct\% of (token, layer) pairs (\refCheckTokens{} tokens).

\paragraph{Corpora and provenance.} Prompts are 160 two-turn conversations from four public datasets (chat, math, code, multilingual), formatted with each model's own chat template. For each of \provNTraceModels{} models (OLMoE-1B-7B, gpt-oss-20b and -120b, Qwen3-30B-A3B, and the audit's Mixtral-8x7B, DeepSeek-V2-Lite, Qwen1.5-MoE-A2.7B, Qwen2-57B-A14B and Phi-3.5-MoE, chat or instruct variants) we trace three arms over the same prompts (for gpt-oss-120b, G and S cover the first \oneTwentyN{}): \textbf{D}, the dataset's reference answer, teacher-forced, as in most trace studies; \textbf{G}, the model's own greedy answer; \textbf{S}, the model's own answer sampled with its generation-config defaults (seed = prompt index), each up to 1024 new tokens, generated with vLLM. Decode steps are the answer tokens only, replayed back to back as one session.

\section{A Speed-of-Light in Seconds}\label{sec:bound}
\paragraph{Decode model.} For one decode step,
\begin{equation}
T = \frac{D + X_{g}}{\eta_g B_g} + \frac{X_{c}}{\eta_c B_c} + n_c\tau_e + \frac{X_{f}}{\eta_p B_p} + n_f\tau_x ,
\label{eq:model}
\end{equation}
with $D$ the dense per-token bytes (attention, shared experts, routers, norms, head, KV reads), $X_g,X_c,X_f$ routed-expert bytes read from GPU memory, executed on the CPU ($n_c$ executions) and fetched over PCIe ($n_f$ transfers), $B$ datasheet bandwidths, $\eta$ efficiencies and $\tau$ latencies. Bytes are exact: every parameter is classified on PyTorch's meta device, or read from GGUF headers. For llama.cpp's \texttt{--n-cpu-moe}~$n$, $X_c$ is the selected experts of the first $n$ MoE layers and $n_c=nk$.

\paragraph{Bound.} Let a policy keep at most $C$ experts of each layer on the GPU (per-layer budget), or at most $LC$ over all layers (global budget). Each routed expert execution runs from a resident copy on the GPU (cost $a$) or on the CPU from host memory (cost $b$); each resident copy costs one load over PCIe ($p$), which may overlap anything.
\begin{proposition}\label{prop:bound}
Let $M^\star$ be the misses of Belady's MIN with bypass on the routing trace under the same budget (per layer, or on the interleaved all-layer stream for a global budget), $N$ the layer-steps, and $\ell(m)=\max((k-m)a,\,mb)$. For any exact-routing policy under that budget, the mean time per token satisfies
\begin{equation}
\bar T \ \ge\ \min_{x\ge 0}\ \max\Big(T_D + L\min_{m\ge (M^\star/N-x)^+}\ell(m),\ \ L\,x\,p\Big).
\end{equation}
\end{proposition}
\emph{Proof sketch.} Group each expert's requests into maximal runs served by one resident copy; a run of $r$ requests needs one load and bridges $r-1$ reuse gaps. MIN with bypass maximises bridged gaps under the budget, and a prefetching schedule bridges no more gaps than some demand schedule with bypass, so GPU executions $\le G^\star+{}$loads, i.e.\ CPU executions $\ge M^\star-x N$ with $x$ loads per layer-step. Per layer-step the time is at least $\ell(m)$ when CPU and GPU run concurrently; $\ell$ is convex, so by Jensen the mean over layer-steps is bounded at the mean $m$. The link must carry the loads: $\bar T\ge Lxp$. Hand-offs, latencies and the capacity cost of prefetching are dropped, so the bound stays valid.\qed

\paragraph{Two consequences.} (i) The bound is often \emph{independent of the budget}: when $M^\star/N$ is below $ka/(a{+}b)$, the optimum runs a share of even resident experts on the CPU, so the speed-of-light is CPU+GPU bandwidth aggregation, above all-GPU speed. (ii) A shared all-layer budget can only lower $M^\star$; on our traces the global variant is \globalGainMin--\globalGainMax\% lower in misses at 12.5\% of experts (\cref{tab:prov}). We test \cref{prop:bound} on a Qwen3-30B-A3B trace (its first 12 layers tiled to 48; 4,000 steps for the global budget), on six platforms and four parameter sets, against LRU, LFU, MIN, MIN-bypass, our online admission policy and an oracle prefetcher per layer, and against LRU, LFU, MIN and MIN-bypass on the shared budget: no policy beats it; the tightest reaches \boundTight\% of it per layer and \boundTightGlobal\% globally. \emph{Physical} speed-of-light sets every $\eta=1$ and every $\tau=0$ with datasheet peaks; the \emph{implementation-relative} version uses the GPU efficiency of a measured all-GPU run.

\section{Validating the Model}\label{sec:validation}
\paragraph{Third-party.} On \nRows{} published measurements from \nSources{} independent sources (CPU-only, static offload in four engines, all-in-VRAM, demand fetch; \nModelsVal{} models), cross-validation that holds out one of \nGroups{} groups at a time (one source's demand-fetch rows are split by GPU) gives \cvMedian\% median error (MAPE \cvMape\%; static-offload rows \cvMedianStatic\%). The one term the data support beyond bytes over bandwidth is a per-CPU-expert latency ($\tau_e=\tauE$\,\si{\micro s}). The model variant was chosen by the same cross-validation, so these errors are slightly optimistic.

\paragraph{First-party, pre-registered.} On an NVIDIA A10 (24\,GB, PCIe~4, 30-vCPU Xeon 8358 VM) we registered predictions before measuring (H0--H4, \cref{app:prereg}). No configuration beat the roofline, and decode time under \texttt{--n-cpu-moe} is affine in $n$ for every model ($R^2\ge0.996$), as \cref{eq:model} requires. The registered accuracy test failed: with constants transferred from third-party data every prediction was too slow, 27\% median error against 20\% registered. On the later llama.cpp runs of our case study the transferred constants under-predict by \underMin--\underMax\%; \cref{sec:audit} discusses what this conservatism means for the audit. Post hoc, fixing $\eta_g$ and $\eta_c$ from two gpt-oss-20b runs (all-GPU and CPU-only) brought the median error to 4.9\% over 29 configurations. We registered that procedure for the next platform (H5, pass at $\le$10\%) and ran it on the GH200 with the phase-1 sweep unchanged: it \hFiveVerdict, with \hFiveMed\% median error on \hFiveN{} held-out configurations (maximum \hFiveMax\%, Qwen3-30B-A3B all-GPU, where per-layer fixed costs dominate; with its experts offloaded Qwen3 is under-predicted by up to \hFiveQwenUnder\%, because the per-CPU-expert latency $\tau_e$, kept from the third-party fit as registered, is too large for this CPU).

\section{Auditing Published Speed-Ups}\label{sec:audit}
\paragraph{Rows.} Three extraction passes, run by AI agents (one per group of papers), collected \auditRows{} measurements from \auditSystems{} systems published between 2023 and September 2026 (\auditPaperSystems{} papers and \auditCommunitySystems{} llama.cpp pull requests, forks and discussions), each with a verbatim quote or table row and its location, so that every value can be checked by a person. Values that exist only in figures were read from the vector drawing or pixel-measured and flagged. We admitted rows under rules fixed before any audit number was computed: single-request decode, experts partly in host DRAM (no SSD tier, one GPU), the model, quantisation, GPU and host named, and at most two of \{DRAM configuration, PCIe generation, expert budget\} imputed as a band. \auditNormalized{} rows qualified. Three further agent passes, which had not seen the extraction, re-read the sources of all \auditChecked{} rows field by field and asked for corrections to \auditCorrected; applying them, and two rules we then applied uniformly (FreeToken's measured host bandwidth and context), changed \auditChanged{} rows, and we excluded one that one checker flagged as effectively all-in-VRAM (\texttt{data/audit/verification.md} lists every change). \auditModelled{} rows have a model we can account for byte by byte.

\paragraph{Method.} For each row we compute (i) the physical speed-of-light for its model, budget and hardware, with $M^\star$ from our on-policy traces where we have the model (\auditTraced{} rows) and from independent routing otherwise; (ii) the \emph{predicted equal-memory baseline}: llama.cpp \texttt{--n-cpu-moe} with the MoE layers that do not fit the row's expert budget on the CPU, from \cref{eq:model} refitted with the row's own system left out; its band combines the model's cross-validated 10th--90th percentile error (\auditBandLo--\auditBandHi$\times$) with any imputation band, and an imputed budget contributes its whole range down to no experts on the GPU. A reported baseline below the band is \emph{weak}; a normalised speed-up $S_n$ (system over predicted baseline) whose band lies above 1 \emph{survives}; rows whose band spans more than $\pm$40\%, that claim less than 1.2$\times$ or that report no baseline are not adjudicated. No row is called wrong. Two analysis choices were fixed after a first pass over the rows and before this final one (protocol 4.7): a row is labelled by its strongest reported llama.cpp baseline, which can only reduce weak calls, and $M^\star$ comes from our new traces of the audit's models.

\paragraph{Findings.} (1) \textbf{No paper configured llama.cpp's partial expert offload as its baseline.} \auditLlamaPapers{} of the \auditPaperSystems{} papers report a llama.cpp baseline; where its configuration is stated, llama.cpp ran whole layers on the CPU (\texttt{-ngl}), all experts on the CPU (\texttt{-cmoe}), a custom fork, or through Ollama; most papers state neither version nor flags. Only two community threads compared against llama.cpp's automatic placement (\texttt{--fit on}), which does split experts per layer. (2) \textbf{\auditWeak{} of \auditLlamaRows{} adjudicated llama.cpp baselines are weak} (below the band); \auditWeakHalfOrLess{} of them reach at most half the predicted equal-memory speed (\cref{tab:audit}). (3) \textbf{\auditSurvive{} of \auditAdj{} claimed gains survive} against the predicted baseline; the survivors include designs whose mechanism is not caching at all, such as KTransformers' CPU kernels. (4) \textbf{Systems are far from the physical speed-of-light}: the median adjudicated system reaches \auditMedianSol\% of it, and the best \auditBestSol\%.

\paragraph{How much to trust this.} The model is conservative for llama.cpp: on our A10 it under-predicts current llama.cpp by \underMin--\underMax\%. Our ``weak baseline'' calls are therefore conservative, and our ``survives'' calls generous. Refitting with our first-party runs, a sensitivity analysis we did not register, gives \auditSensSurvive{} survivors and \auditSensWeak{} weak baselines. On the GH200 anchors (\cref{sec:validation}) the model also under-predicts llama.cpp in the offloaded regime; the registered A100 anchors, on the GPU several audited papers used, remain to be run. The audit cannot see implementation quality inside a system, only its speed against two references.

\section{Pitfalls We Hit}\label{sec:pitfalls}
\begin{itemize}\setlength\itemsep{1pt}
\item \textbf{Prefill caps put decode steps in the prompt.} Our earlier harness prefilled at most 128 prompt tokens and teacher-forced the next 192 tokens as decode, so \winGptPct\% of gpt-oss's and \winQwenPct\% of Qwen3's ``decode'' steps were the user's own text. On the same instance, fixing it changed speed-ups at 25\% by at most \winEffectMax$\times$; it mattered more for fidelity checks, where it mixed user-text prediction into loss.
\item \textbf{Instances of one type are not one machine.} Two A10 VMs differed by \bwDiffPct\% in host bandwidth and up to \instLlamaMax\% in llama.cpp speed, moving our speed-ups by up to \instSpeedupMax$\times$. Report host bandwidth measured on the instance used.
\item \textbf{Format is part of the workload.} A reasoning model scored on answers without its reasoning is off-distribution (\cref{sec:provenance}).
\item \textbf{Hit rate is not speed.} Our first cache matched the simulator's hit rates and was no faster than no cache; three coordination costs, not bandwidth, ate the gain.
\item \textbf{Novelty is cheap to check and expensive to skip.} We built a system before searching; HybriMoE, FreeToken, an unnamed CPU-GPU system~\cite{huang2025cpugpu} and four llama.cpp forks and pull requests~\cite{llamacppdisc24528} had converged on the same design.
\end{itemize}

\section{Limitations}\label{sec:limits}
\begin{itemize}\setlength\itemsep{1pt}
\item \textbf{The model's error bounds what the audit can adjudicate.} Effects below about 1.3$\times$ are inside the band.
\item \textbf{Hardware coverage.} Our first-party runs used an A10, an A100 and a GH200 in the cloud; most audited rows are desktop RTX 4090/5090 machines with dual-channel DRAM, which we model but did not measure.
\item \textbf{Generation checkpoints.} Where the bf16 model did not fit the available GPU, own-text arms were generated (and scored) with the vendor FP8 or on-the-fly FP8 weights; traces use the original weights (bf16, MXFP4 experts for gpt-oss) in fp32 arithmetic.
\item \textbf{Extraction by agents.} The audit's extraction and re-check passes were run by AI agents; the protection is that every value carries a verbatim quote and location, and every change is logged, so any row can be checked by hand.
\item \textbf{Scope of the bound.} Exact routing only: expert skipping, substitution or deferral fall outside it by definition and are flagged in the audit.
\end{itemize}

\section{Conclusion}
Stated in seconds and against a baseline configured the way users run it, the literature on MoE offloading looks different: most reported llama.cpp baselines are weak, a minority of claimed gains survive, and the median system reaches \auditMedianSol\% of what its hardware physically allows. The bound, the validated model, the traces and the audit set are released so that the next paper can report where it stands in one line.
