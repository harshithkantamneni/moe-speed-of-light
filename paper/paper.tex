\begin{abstract}
A Mixture-of-Experts model too large for one consumer GPU keeps most of its experts in host memory, and decoding one
token at a time then waits on host reads. We ask how fast such decoding could be on a given machine, where the rest of
the time goes, and what knowing the future routing is worth. No cache of $C$ experts per layer reads fewer experts from
host memory than Belady's MIN with bypass, so MIN's reads at the machine's probed read rate bound every system that
executes the exact routing. Our llama.cpp expert cache, faster than two other systems at most configurations, runs at
\dcShareMinLowMin--\dcShareMinLowMax\% of that bound's speed on \dcMachinesLow{} machines with consumer processors.
Oracles built into the engine then remove the gap to the bound one part at a time. At the smallest gpt-oss budget on
\dmFastN{} machines, about a fifth of the gap is caching the wrong experts, a fifth reading admitted experts twice, a
sixth reading them late, and a quarter the GPU's own work waiting in series with the reads. Knowing the future closes
about half of the gap, but only when each admitted expert is read once and the PCIe link is fast. Exact routing of the
next token would recover at most a third of what full foresight saves in host reads; half of it needs the routing of
about \dcMed$\,C$ distinct experts per layer ahead.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}
Open-weight Mixture-of-Experts (MoE) models now carry 30--120\,B parameters and activate a few billion per token; a
desktop GPU has 24--32\,GB. Running such a model for one user therefore means keeping most experts in host DRAM. Each
decoded token reads its routed experts from GPU memory if they are resident, and otherwise from host memory: either the
CPU runs the expert, or the expert is copied over PCIe into a GPU slot \citep{ivanov2021datamovement,hoefler2021sparsity}.

Many systems build on this: GPU expert caches \citep{eliseev2023offloading,xue2024moeinfinity,song2024promoe}, CPU
execution of misses \citep{kamahori2024fiddler,ktransformers,zhong2025hybrimoe}, a per-miss choice between the
two \citep{freetoken,dali2026} and expert prediction \citep{hwang2024pregated,zhu2025preattention,wang2026seqmoe}. Each
reports a speed-up over a baseline of its choosing. None says how fast the same machine could decode the model, or which
part of the remaining time a better design could remove. Without that, a builder cannot tell whether to work on what is
cached, how it is read, when it is read, or how far ahead the routing must be predicted.

We answer these questions for batch-1 decode of gpt-oss-120b \citep{openai2025gptoss} and Qwen3-30B-A3B
\citep{yang2025qwen3} on rented RTX 5090 machines. We derive a bound, build oracles into a real engine that remove one
part of the gap at a time, and run them on many machines, with every prediction committed before its machine started.
\Cref{tab:claims} lists the answers and the evidence behind each.

\textbf{The headroom is large} (\cref{sec:limit}). MIN's host reads at the machine's probed rate bound the time per
token of any system with $C$ slots per layer, and that read time is nearly reachable in a microbenchmark. Our cache,
which leads FreeToken and llama.cpp at most configurations, runs at roughly a third to a half of the bound's speed.
Scored the same way, published batch-1 systems reach a median \audInclassMed\% of theirs.

\textbf{The gap has four measured parts} (\cref{sec:gap}). Removing them one at a time in the engine shows that caching
the wrong experts is only about a fifth of the gap. Reading admitted experts twice, reading them late, and leaving the
GPU's own work in series with the reads make up most of the rest (\cref{fig:staircase}).

\textbf{Foresight pays only if it is spent right} (\cref{sec:foresight}). Knowing the future routing helps when each
admitted expert is read once, when the schedule admits as rarely as optimality allows, and when the read path suits the
machine: where the PCIe link reads at \jkSlowRatioMax{} of the CPU rate or less, copies belong in the background.

\textbf{Foresight has a price} (\cref{sec:horizon}). Half of its value needs the routing of about \dcMed$\,C$ distinct
experts per layer ahead, on \fsModels{} models. Exact routing of the next token recovers at most a third of it, and
no forecaster we tested comes close.

\Cref{sec:builders} turns these results into rules for system builders.

\paragraph{Contributions.}
\begin{itemize}\setlength\itemsep{1pt}
\item \textbf{A bound on time per token} for exact-routing systems with $C$ slots per layer, shown to be nearly reachable
in its read time, and the distance of our cache and of published systems from it (\cref{sec:limit}).
\item \textbf{A measured decomposition of the gap}, obtained by removing each part with oracles inside a real engine on
\dmMachines{} machines (\cref{sec:gap}).
\item \textbf{How foresight must be spent}: read once, with the fewest admissions, on the path the machine favours
(\cref{sec:foresight}).
\item \textbf{A price on foresight} by horizon and accuracy, and a horizon rule in distinct experts across \fsModels{}
models (\cref{sec:horizon}).
\item \textbf{An open record}: every prediction committed before the machine it concerns started, including the ones
that failed, and every number generated by a script (\cref{app:prereg,app:artifact}).
\end{itemize}

\section{Setting}\label{sec:setting}
\paragraph{Models and workload.} gpt-oss-120b has 36 MoE layers of 128 experts, top-4, in MXFP4 (13.25\,MB per
expert). Qwen3-30B-A3B has 48 layers of 128 experts, top-8, in BF16 (9.44\,MB per expert). Prompts are AIME-25
problems, and the cache carries over between problems, as in one user's session. The system comparison decodes 256
tokens of each of the 30 problems through each system's server. The engine experiments replay the model's own greedy
output, teacher-forced, so that every configuration sees the same routing. A \emph{budget} is the share $C/E$ of each
layer's experts that fit in GPU slots. The two smallest budgets of each model are \emph{host-bound}: host reads, not the
GPU, limit their speed. Most results are at the two host-bound gpt-oss budgets, 11\% and 25\%.

\paragraph{The engine.} Our cache is a 3{,}500-line patch to llama.cpp \citep{llamacpp}. Each MoE layer has $C$ GPU
slots. A \emph{miss} is read from host memory in one of two ways: CPU helper threads run it, or it is \emph{fetched}
(copied into a slot over PCIe and run on the GPU in the same step). The \emph{deployed} policy admits a miss into a slot
when its decayed request count beats the lowest resident's by a margin $\kappa{=}1$. The CPU serves the admitted miss
and a background copy then fills its slot, so the expert is read from host memory twice (\cref{fig:step}).

\begin{figure}[t]\centering
\includegraphics[width=\linewidth]{figs/step.pdf}
\caption{One decode step of the deployed cache, schematically. Blue: the GPU's own kernels. Grey: host reads of the
misses, which the step waits for. Orange: the background copy that fills an admitted expert's slot, a second read of
the same expert.}\label{fig:step}
\end{figure}

\paragraph{Terms.} \emph{MIN} is Belady's optimum with bypass \citep{belady1966study,bypassopt}: it \emph{admits} a miss
only if some resident is needed later than the miss, which gives the most hits possible with $C$ slots
\citep{carlisle1995kcoloring,jain2016hawkeye}. Many schedules reach that optimum. The \emph{greedy} one admits whenever
it may; the \emph{fewest-admission} one admits as rarely as any of them. An admitted expert is read \emph{once}, copied
into its slot before the GPU runs it, or \emph{twice}, served by the CPU and copied in the background. It is read
\emph{late} if the copy waits for the router, and \emph{ahead} if the copy is issued a few steps early. \emph{Oracles}
read the future routing from a trace recorded on the same machine. \Cref{tab:configs} lists every configuration;
\cref{app:names} maps the codes of the appendix tables to these names.

\paragraph{Machines.} Every machine is a rented host with one RTX 5090. Each runs our bandwidth probe, which measures
how fast it reads host memory from CPU threads ($B_c$), over PCIe ($B_p$), and by any method ($B_{\mathrm{host}}$, the
highest single reading). The \emph{link-to-CPU ratio} is $B_p/B_c$. Ryzen, Core and Threadripper parts are
\emph{consumer} processors; EPYC, Xeon and engineering-sample parts are \emph{server} processors.

\input{tab_configs}

\paragraph{Statistics and registration.} Time per token is averaged over problems, and speed ratios are ratios of mean
times. Machines differ far more than problems: two panel machines with the same CPU model differ by up to
\pnSameCpuDiffMax\% in the deployed cache's time, while a machine rented again came within \jiRelaunchMax\% of its earlier
run. The machine is therefore the unit. Claims are about orderings that hold on every machine of a stated class, and
summaries over machines are bootstrapped over machines \citep{kalibera2013rigorous}. A \emph{launch} is one rental; in
the later experiments each configuration also ran in two or three separate processes per launch. A machine
\emph{ran stably} when its outputs matched the reference and, where it ran more than one process, their times agreed
within 2\%. Every experiment's predictions were committed to a public branch before its machine started;
\cref{app:prereg} scores all of them.

\section{How Fast Could It Be?}\label{sec:limit}
Consider any system that executes the model's exact routing with at most $C$ experts of each layer resident on the
GPU. Per token, each of the $Lk$ routed experts is served by a resident copy or read from host memory. Let $R^\star$ be
MIN's host reads per token, summed over layers; no policy, even one that knows the future, reads fewer. If $c$ experts
per token run on the CPU, host memory supplies at least $\max(R^\star,c)\,S$ bytes for experts of $S$ bytes, and the GPU
reads the dense weights $D$ plus $(Lk-c)\,S$. With everything overlapped,
\begin{equation}
\bar T \;\ge\; \min_{0\le c\le Lk}\ \max\!\Big(\frac{D+(Lk-c)S}{B_{\mathrm{gpu}}},\ \frac{\max(R^\star\!,c)\,S}{B_{\mathrm{host}}}\Big).
\label{eq:limit}
\end{equation}
At a host-bound budget this is MIN's read time, $R^\star S/B_{\mathrm{host}}$. The bound assumes exact routing, whole
experts, at most $C$ residents per layer and one token per step. It drops all latencies, lets the two read paths share
$B_{\mathrm{host}}$, and is relative to the probe: a reader faster than the probe could beat it (\cref{sec:limits}).

\paragraph{A tighter bound when reads wait for the router.} A system that reads an expert only after its layer's router
has chosen it cannot overlap that read with the GPU's work: layer $l$'s misses are known only after its attention and
router, and layer $l{+}1$ starts only after layer $l$'s experts. Its time is at least the GPU's non-expert work plus the
reads, in series:
\begin{equation}
\bar T_{\text{demand}} \;\ge\; T_{\text{GPU}} + \frac{R^\star S}{B_{\mathrm{host}}},
\label{eq:demand}
\end{equation}
where $T_{\text{GPU}}$ is the GPU's attention, dense layers, norms and routers per token: \dmTgpu\,ms for gpt-oss, the
smallest of our engine's Nsight profiles. Every system we compare reads on demand; only reads issued before routing can go below
\cref{eq:demand}.

\paragraph{The read time is nearly reachable (registered).} A microbenchmark replays MIN's per-layer reads of gpt-oss on
\jdHosts{} machines without the model: CPU threads read part of each layer's experts while the copy engine copies the rest.
It reaches \jdLayerMin--\jdLayerMax\% of the bound's read time (\cref{tab:readsched}). The GPU term of \cref{eq:limit} is
looser, but it binds only at the largest budgets (\cref{tab:limit}).

\paragraph{Where systems stand.} \Cref{tab:headline} places three systems against the bounds on two machines. Our
cache leads FreeToken \citep{freetoken} at \bothLeadCells{} of \bothCells{} configurations and runs
\bothLlamaXMin--\bothLlamaXMax$\times$ as fast as stock llama.cpp with the same GPU memory for experts. Tuned on a third
machine, FreeToken still trails at \fttLeadCells{} of six cells (\cref{app:system}). Across \dcMachinesLow{} machines
with consumer processors, our cache runs at \dcShareMinLowMin--\dcShareMinLowMax\% of the bound's speed at gpt-oss 11\%.

\paragraph{The field leaves most of the machine unused.} To compare with published systems, we score each one against
the same bound computed from its paper's own hardware at datasheet rates. Over \audInclassN{} published batch-1
measurements, the median is \audInclassMed\% of the bound; our cache, scored the same way, reaches a median
\audOursMed\% (\cref{app:audit}). Where we lack a model's routing trace, uniform routing stands in, which overstates the
bound's reads and so the published percentages.

\section{Where the Seconds Go}\label{sec:gap}
The deployed cache's time beyond \cref{eq:limit} has parts that a design could remove. We measure them by removing them
one at a time with oracles in the engine. Starting from the deployed cache, an oracle caches MIN's set instead (what is
cached), reads each admitted expert once instead of twice (how it is read), and issues the copies a few steps ahead
(when it is read). What remains above \cref{eq:limit} is the GPU's non-expert work in series with the reads,
$T_{\text{GPU}}$ of \cref{eq:demand}, and whatever the engine still spends beyond \cref{eq:demand} with full foresight.
The order of the first two steps matters, so we split them order-free over the four combinations of what is cached and
how it is read. \Cref{fig:staircase} shows every machine that ran all of these states, \dmMachines{} in all, and
\cref{tab:gap} the mean shares.

\begin{figure*}[t]\centering
\includegraphics[width=0.92\textwidth]{figs/decomp_measured.pdf}
\caption{Where the seconds go, measured: the time per token of each state, relative to the deployed cache on the same
machine, as the oracles remove one part of the gap after another, down to the two bounds. One line per machine that ran
every state, one launch per GPU. On the two machines with slow links (orange), reading once in the step is slower than
reading twice.}
\label{fig:staircase}
\end{figure*}

\paragraph{Caching the wrong experts is about a fifth of the gap.} MIN's set accounts for \dmChoiceLow\% of the gap at
gpt-oss 11\% and \dmChoiceMid\% at 25\%. Its value depends on how it is read: with two reads, MIN's set barely beats the
deployed one at 11\% (\cref{fig:staircase}), because each of its extra admissions costs a second read.

\paragraph{Reading admitted experts twice costs as much.} Reading each admission once accounts for \dmTwiceLow\% of the
gap at 11\%, where admissions are frequent, and \dmTwiceMid\% at 25\%. Any design that serves a miss on the CPU and then
copies it pays this cost.

\paragraph{Reading late costs a sixth.} Issuing MIN's copies a few steps ahead accounts for \dmLateLow\% of the gap at
11\% and \dmLateMid\% at 25\%. With all three parts removed, the engine's time is \dmPrefEqTwoLowMin--\dmPrefEqTwoLowMax{} times
\cref{eq:demand} at 11\%.

\paragraph{The GPU's own work in series is the largest part.} The GPU's non-expert work, in series with the reads, is
\dmSerialLow\% of the gap at 11\% and \dmSerialMid\% at 25\%. No oracle we built removes it: the misses that remain are
still read after their router runs. Removing it needs reads issued before routing for every miss, which is what a
predictor of the routing would buy.

\paragraph{Slow links change the picture.} On the \dmSlowN{} machines whose link reads at most \dmSlowRatioMax{} of
their CPU rate, reading once in the step is slower than reading twice, and the best oracle closes at most
\dmClosedSlowLowMax\% of the gap at 11\%. Where the link is fast, the best oracle closes a mean \dmClosedLow\% (95\%
interval over machines \dmClosedLowLo--\dmClosedLowHi). \Cref{sec:foresight} shows what does pay on slow links.

\paragraph{A closed-form account (exploratory, failed).} On consumer processors, the deployed cache's time is close to
the GPU's profiled kernel time plus its counted host reads at $B_{\mathrm{host}}$: the read rate its time implies is
\jmConsEffMin--\jmConsEffMax{} of $B_{\mathrm{host}}$ over \jmConsN{} launches. We registered this account before
\rtN{} experiments on machines new to it, and it failed all of them, by up to \jlNewDeskErrMax\% on a new consumer
machine (\cref{app:relation}). The measured decomposition above does not rely on it.

\section{How Foresight Must Be Spent}\label{sec:foresight}
This section measures each way of spending foresight as a speed relative to the deployed cache on the same machine. A
running example: on a Ryzen 9 9950X at gpt-oss 11\%, \cref{eq:limit} gives \slExBoundMs\,ms per token and
\cref{eq:demand} \slExDemandMs. The deployed cache takes \fsExBaseMs, MIN read once in the step \fsExFetchMs, and MIN
read once and ahead \fsExPacedMs.

\begin{figure*}[t]\centering
\includegraphics[width=\textwidth]{figs/factorial.pdf}
\caption{What foresight is worth, by how it is spent: speed of each configuration of \cref{tab:configs} relative to the
deployed cache on the same machine, at the six budgets ($^\dagger$host-bound). Three machines at every budget (O3--O5:
two Ryzen 9 9950X and a 9950X3D); the \pnHosts{} panel machines (grey ticks) at the two host-bound gpt-oss budgets, and
\pnHostsQlow{} of them at Qwen3 12.5\%. \emph{MIN, 1 read} gains at every budget on these machines; reading twice or
admitting what MIN bypasses gains at some budgets and loses at others.}
\label{fig:factorial}
\end{figure*}

\paragraph{Read each admitted expert once (registered).} MIN read once in the step is faster than the deployed cache at
every budget on the three factorial machines, by \fxFetchAllGainMin--\fxFetchAllGainMax\%, and by up to
\fxPacedAllGainMax\% when read ahead (\cref{fig:factorial}). The usual ways of spending foresight waste reads.
Prefetching without bypass, Belady's original rule, admits experts MIN would bypass. Serving a miss on the CPU before
copying it reads every admission twice. At the smallest budget of each model these gain at most \fxUsualLowGainMax\% or
lose, a trade-off known for disks \citep{cao1995prefetching} and CPU caches \citep{jain2018prefetch}.

\paragraph{Admit as rarely as optimality allows (registered).} The greedy schedule admits whenever MIN allows and may
evict an expert again before its next use. The fewest-admission schedule makes \jfCopyRatioMin--\jfCopyRatioMax{} of
the greedy one's copies for the same hits. Read once in the step at gpt-oss 11\%, it beat the deployed cache on all
\rbPlanMachines{} machines that ran it stably, by a geometric mean of \rbPlanMean$\times$ (95\% interval over machines
\rbPlanLo--\rbPlanHi). The gain was registered on \rbPlanRegMachines{} of them and observed on the others. Counting the
\rbPlanUnstN{} machines that ran unsteadily as well, the mean is \rbPlanAllMean$\times$ over \rbPlanAllN; it lost on
\rbPlanUnstLost{} of them, the two with the slowest links.

\paragraph{The machine decides the read path (exploratory).} Across machines, the gain of every configuration that moves
reads from the CPU to the link follows the link-to-CPU ratio: for MIN read once in the step, Spearman \rcFetchRatio{}
[\rcFetchRatioLo, \rcFetchRatioHi] over \rcFetchRatioN{} machines (\cref{fig:hostdep}). The machine carries
\ppVarHostsFetchMax\% of the variance of that gain; problems carry at most \ppVarProbFetchMax\%. Where the link reads at
\jkSlowRatioMin--\jkSlowRatioMax{} of the CPU rate, the greedy schedule read once in the step lost at 11\%
(\jkSlowFetchMin--\jkSlowFetchMax$\times$).
There the fewest-admission set pays when the CPU serves it and the copies run in the background:
\jkSlowBypassPlanMin--\jkSlowBypassPlanMax$\times$ (registered).

\begin{figure*}[t]\centering
\includegraphics[width=\textwidth]{figs/hostdep.pdf}
\caption{The machine decides which way of spending foresight pays: speed relative to the deployed cache on the same
machine against the machine's link-to-CPU ratio, at the two host-bound gpt-oss budgets. Every marker is a machine that
passed our checks. Stars: MIN's fewest-admission schedule read once in the step. Circles and triangles: MIN's greedy
schedule read once (in the step) or twice (by the CPU, then copied). Crosses: the deployed cache with the engine's
layer-ahead copy. Lines: least-squares fits in the log of the ratio, for the trend only.}
\label{fig:hostdep}
\end{figure*}

\paragraph{Without foresight: admit less (registered, partly held).} The fewest-admission schedule wins because an
admission costs a read. The deployed cache's admissions are second reads, so the lesson applies to it without foresight.
Raising its admission margin to the value that minimises its reads on other text cuts its host reads by
\jiDkReadsGLowMin--\jiDkReadsGLowMax\% at gpt-oss 11\%. On the \rbDkMachines{} machines that ran it stably, it runs
\rbDkLow$\times$ the deployed cache at 11\% and \rbDkMid$\times$ at 25\% (geometric means over machines). The gain is
small because admissions are a small share of the deployed cache's reads (\cref{app:more}).

\section{How Much Foresight Is Needed}\label{sec:horizon}
The oracles know the whole future; a predictor would know part of it, imperfectly. We give the cache a \emph{window},
the routing of the next $W$ tokens, exact or with each future expert right only with probability $r$. Throughout this
section, the value of a window is its share of MIN's gain over admitting every miss, the online policy that reads least
at these budgets (\cref{app:value} gives the policies).

\begin{figure*}[t]\centering
\includegraphics[width=\textwidth]{figs/value.pdf}
\caption{The value of partial foresight at the four host-bound budgets: the share of MIN's gain over \emph{admit every
miss} recovered by exact routing of the next $W$ tokens (solid), by a window whose experts are each right with
probability 0.5 (dashed), and by a linear forecaster fitted on other text (dotted), in host reads on the routing the
engine runs. Markers: the same share in time per token on the panel machines (mean and range over machines;
\pnHostsLow{} machines for gpt-oss, \pnHostsQlow{} for Qwen3 at 12.5\%). The grey line marks half of the gain.}
\label{fig:value}
\end{figure*}

\paragraph{Next-token prediction buys little.} Exact routing of the next token recovers \vmShareOneLow{} of MIN's gain at
gpt-oss 11\% and \vmShareOneMid{} at 25\%, and at most \vmShareOneQlow{} at any host-bound budget (\cref{fig:value}). Half of the gain needs \vmHalfLow{} and \vmHalfMid{} tokens.
In the engine, an exact 16-token window recovers \pnShareSixteenLowMin--\pnShareSixteenLowMax{} of the gain in time at
11\% (registered).

\paragraph{Accuracy matters as much as horizon.} An 8-token window that is right half the time recovers
\vmEightHalfLow{} of the gain at 11\%, about what 2 exact tokens buy (\vmShareTwoLow).

\paragraph{The horizon is measured in distinct experts (exploratory).} On \fsModels{} models' own sampled text, the
window that closes half of the read gap ranges from \dcWMin{} to \dcWMax{} tokens. Paging theory measures lookahead in
distinct pages \citep{albers1997lookahead,breslauer1995lookahead}, and so does the rule that fits: within that window a
layer requests about \dcMed$\,C$ distinct experts (quartiles \dcLo--\dcHi). On the AIME routing the engine runs, the
same quantity is \dcAimeMin--\dcAimeMax$\,C$. Given a model's routing trace, this one-constant rule predicts its horizon
as well as a two-constant power law in tokens (\cref{app:traces}).

\paragraph{Nothing realisable reaches it.} Forecasters trained on routing histories, linear or recurrent, close at
most \lfShareMaxGpt\% of the read gap on gpt-oss: their precision falls with the horizon. Speculative batches save an
online cache \bkSameNineMin--\bkSameNineMax\% of its reads at 90\% draft acceptance and cost
\bkSameEightLossMin--\bkSameEightLossMax\% at 80\% \citep{spmoe,moespac2026,cascade2025}. Layer-ahead predictors
\citep{hwang2024pregated,zhu2025preattention} reach \laRecallK\% recall in our engine but see less than one token ahead.
The engine's layer-ahead copy gains \jgPfLowFastMin$\times$ where the link is as fast as the CPU and loses elsewhere,
down to \jgPfLowMin$\times$ at 11\% (registered; \cref{tab:job105}).

\section{What This Means for System Builders}\label{sec:builders}
\begin{enumerate}\setlength\itemsep{1pt}
\item \textbf{Report the distance to the machine's bound.} A speed-up over a chosen baseline hides how much is left.
\Cref{eq:limit} needs only the routing trace and one probe run, and on our machines the best system sits at a third to a
half of it.
\item \textbf{Read each expert once.} Serving a miss on the CPU and then copying it into a slot reads it twice. At the
smallest gpt-oss budget that costs about as much as caching the wrong experts.
\item \textbf{Admit less.} Every admission is a read. Among equally good schedules, the one with the fewest admissions
wins; online, a higher admission margin gains about \rbDkLow$\times$ at no cost.
\item \textbf{Choose the read path by the link-to-CPU ratio.} On fast links, copy misses into slots in the step. At a
ratio of \jkSlowRatioMax{} or less, serve them on the CPU and copy in the background. The probe that measures the ratio takes seconds.
\item \textbf{Predict far enough ahead to hide the GPU's own work.} A quarter to a third of the gap is the GPU waiting
in series with reads that could have been issued earlier. Predictors should target the next $\approx$\dcMed$\,C$ distinct
experts of each layer, not the next token, and accuracy counts as much as horizon.
\end{enumerate}

\section{Related Work}\label{sec:related}
\paragraph{Offloading systems.} Demand-paging systems cache experts per layer and copy misses over
PCIe \citep{eliseev2023offloading,xue2024moeinfinity,song2024promoe,tang2024hobbit,swapmoe2024,adapmoe2024,edgemoe2023,expertflow,pagingexperts2026},
or use predicted expert activations to decide what to keep \citep{sida2024}; an upstream llama.cpp pull request adds
such a cache, admitting every miss into an LRU \citep{llamacpppr29887}. CPU-execution systems run non-resident experts on
the host \citep{kamahori2024fiddler,ktransformers,zhong2025hybrimoe,daop2025,wang2026cloudgrade,atsinfer2026}, as
PowerInfer runs a dense model's cold neurons \citep{powerinfer2024}; a set-associative GPU cache with CPU-executed misses
\citep{huang2025cpugpu}, a llama.cpp fork \citep{leloch2026,llamacppdisc24528} and two llama.cpp pull requests
\citep{llamacpppr26563,llamacpppr27861} are the closest designs to ours. FreeToken \citep{freetoken} and DALI
\citep{dali2026} split each layer's misses between CPU and PCIe. Predictors guess upcoming experts from the previous
layer's state \citep{hwang2024pregated,zhu2025preattention}, several tokens ahead with a learned forecaster
\citep{wang2026seqmoe,mira2026}, or from draft tokens \citep{spmoe,moespac2026,cascade2025}; Read-ME decouples the router
so routing is known in advance \citep{readme2024}. Batched-throughput systems
\citep{flexgen2023,cao2024moelightning,yuan2025moelens} and flash or SSD tiers \citep{llminflash2024,ssdllama,edgezero}
are outside our scope.

\paragraph{Bounds, oracles and caching theory.} WiSP \citep{wisp2026} bounds decode by PCIe turnover, Budgeting Bytes
\citep{budgetingbytes2026} by bytes over bandwidth for a given fetch set, and MoE-CAP \citep{moecap} measures bandwidth
utilisation against a single peak; the roofline \citep{williams2009roofline} is the single-memory ancestor of our bound.
\citet{zhang2026reproducible} propose a trace-level evaluation contract and report a 44--46\% gap between causal
policies and MIN with bypass; \citet{liang2026lrc} measure, across 20 models, the hit rate of an oracle cache that sees
$m$ tokens ahead. We add the time domain: oracles inside a real engine, a measured decomposition of the gap, the cost of
how foresight is spent, and a horizon rule in distinct experts. SAEM \citep{saem2026} gives its own stage-level planner
perfect knowledge of the coming stage and finds that worth at most about 14\% on an A100; SpecMD \citep{specmd}
benchmarks expert-cache and speculative prefetch policies across hardware; \citet{angelopoulos2025cache} give competitive
bounds for layered expert paging. With bypass, MIN maximises hits exactly \citep{carlisle1995kcoloring,jain2016hawkeye};
when bypass and admission cost differ, the time-optimal placement is a different problem \citep{bypassopt}, which is
why we use MIN's reads as an input to a bound rather than as a time-optimal policy. Choosing among hit-optimal schedules
by what admissions cost has precedents: Demand-MIN for prefetching \citep{jain2018prefetch}, offline-optimal caching as a
min-cost flow over reuse intervals \citep{berger2018foo}, and admission rationing in flash caches \citep{flashield2019}.
Our fewest-admission schedule is that interval formulation with admissions as a second objective, measured in an
engine. Learning-augmented paging is most robust with predictions of whether a page will be evicted before its next use
\citep{antoniadis2023succinct}; learned replacement imitates Belady in CPU and content-delivery caches
\citep{jain2016hawkeye,song2020lrb} and in an SSD-backed expert cache \citep{flashmoe2026}.

\section{Limitations}\label{sec:limits}
\begin{itemize}\setlength\itemsep{1pt}
\item \textbf{Hardware and sample.} One GPU model, rented, mostly in desktop hosts; clocks could not be locked and power
limits differed. The machines are a convenience sample of the cheapest offers that passed our checks, and few have a
link slower than half their CPU rate, so where the crossovers lie rests on few points. Rented machines are often shared
or unsteady: in the two tests on new machines, \jlGated{} and \jmGated{} machines stopped at the gate and \jlInvalid{}
and \jmInvalid{} ran unsteadily, and of \jmServAll{} launches on server processors only \jmServWord{} passed every check.
\item \textbf{Workload.} Batch-1 decode of two models on AIME prompts, also used while developing the system,
teacher-forced on each model's own greedy text. Prompts and batches above one bypass the cache.
\item \textbf{The bounds.} Exact routing and per-layer slots; pooling slots across layers lowers MIN's reads by
\globalReadsMin--\globalReadsMax\%. $R^\star$ is counted on the 30-problem trace; on the first 20 problems, which the
later experiments time, it changes by \rsDiffLow\%. The bounds drop latencies and are relative to one run of our probe: the engine's
implied read rate exceeded the probe's highest reading by more than 5\% on \rbAboveMax{} of \rbLaunchBudgets{}
launch-budgets (\cref{tab:robust}). $T_{\text{GPU}}$ is our engine's kernel time, not a property of the machine alone.
\item \textbf{The decomposition.} The parts are measured on machines with fast links, at two budgets of one model. Its
last two parts lean on $T_{\text{GPU}}$, which is profiled rather than removed. The first two parts interact, which is
why we split them order-free.
\item \textbf{The oracles.} Oracles read a recorded trace. In the engine the fewest-admission schedule misses
\jfMissDevMin--\jfMissDevMax\% more than the greedy one, more than its replay predicts; its measured gains include that
cost. Degraded windows draw errors independently, while a real predictor's errors correlate with what the cache holds.
\item \textbf{Outputs.} Parity is measured by teacher-forced KL divergence against an all-VRAM reference (mean
\klMeanGpt{} and \klMeanQwen{} nats), not by task accuracy.
\end{itemize}

\section{Conclusion}
MIN's host reads at a machine's probed rate bound batch-1 MoE decode with experts in host memory, and the bound's read
time is nearly reachable. The systems we measured, ours included, run at a third to a half of it. Oracles inside the
engine show where the rest goes: caching the wrong experts, reading admitted experts twice, reading them late, and the
GPU's own work waiting in series with the reads. Foresight removes about half of the gap when each admitted expert is
read once, with the fewest admissions, on the read path the machine favours. Its value has a price: half of it needs the
routing of about \dcMed$\,C$ distinct experts per layer ahead, far beyond the next token that current predictors target.
