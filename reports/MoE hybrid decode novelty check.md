# The bound survives; the cache doesn't

Bluntly: as a *system*, this work arrives late. Most of what it builds was public before its first commit on **25 September 2026** and its system commits of **26–27 September 2026**: a dynamic GPU expert cache whose misses run on CPU threads, decayed-frequency admission, CPU and GPU working concurrently inside a layer, and a decode step captured as one CUDA graph all appear in HybriMoE (2025), the ASP-DAC'26 paper 2512.16473 (December 2025), KTransformers (SOSP'25), FreeToken (August 2026) and four unmerged llama.cpp implementations by three authors (June–August 2026). Of sixteen separable claims, **seven are already done, four are partly new and five are new**, and the five new ones are almost all analysis and diagnosis rather than system design. The headline **1.41–1.81x over `--n-cpu-moe`** is an ordinary gain for this literature (Fiddler 1.26x, KTransformers 1.25–1.93x, FreeToken 1.5–2.3x, community caches +7% to 2.2x), and nothing yet shows it beats a KTransformers-class system on the same machine. What is genuinely distinctive is the measurement science: a time-domain speed-of-light bound for per-layer-capacity placement with CPU-executed misses, a bytes-over-bandwidth model checked against 52 third-party measurements with a pre-registered 2.9% median error on 33 held-out configurations, and an equal-VRAM comparison almost nobody else runs. Add to that a GPU-initiated mailbox hand-off with a GPU-decided CPU skip, which no LLM system is known to use but whose benefit over the obvious host-callback alternative has not been measured. As it stands this is a strong workshop or ISPASS paper; MLSys 2027 (due 30 October) is reachable only with a same-machine KTransformers comparison, a host-callback ablation, confidence intervals on the headline numbers and ideally a second platform. None of this blocks the ETH application, which requires no publications: the asset is an arXiv preprint and code, posted before 27 October, that leads with the bound.

## Seven of sixteen claims were public before September 25

Priority goes by public date. The repository's first commit (the trace collector and MIN simulator) is dated 25 September 2026, the model and bound 26 September, and the llama.cpp system 26–27 September; the date the repository became publicly visible should be confirmed separately. Every item of prior art below predates all of these. The newest, SeqMoE, is 14 days older than the first commit. Unless a public pre-registration record carries an earlier timestamp, the work cannot claim priority on any overlapping element. At most it can call the August–September 2026 papers concurrent, and it must cite every one of them.

The three most damaging overlaps deserve spelling out. First, the system's core concept is described almost word for word by 2512.16473: "when required experts are not cached on GPU, computations are offloaded to CPU while experts are asynchronously fetched to GPU" from a per-layer "N-index, M-way set-associative" cache ([arXiv 2512.16473](https://arxiv.org/abs/2512.16473)). FreeToken goes further, splitting each step's misses into a concurrent fill set and a CPU-execution set sized from measured bandwidths, across **six machines** ([arXiv 2608.16157](https://arxiv.org/abs/2608.16157)). Second, the admission rule is already in llama.cpp PR #26563, and any admission-only rule is fighting over a small slice of the headroom. 2608.07911 attributes **84.3–96.6%** of the remaining gap to Belady to future-victim knowledge, leaving bypass admission roughly 3–16% ([arXiv 2608.07911](https://arxiv.org/abs/2608.07911)). Third, even the methodology has a twin. "Cacheable by Design?" (18 August 2026) is **pre-registered**. It runs llama.cpp with `-ngl 99 -ncmoe 94` on a single RTX 3070, matches a bytes-per-token/bandwidth model, builds an eval-callback trace tool and simulates a per-layer Belady oracle ([arXiv 2608.18261](https://arxiv.org/abs/2608.18261)). It is a single-configuration measurement study with no system, but it removes "first pre-registered study in this niche" as a claim.

On llama.cpp RFC #24528: it was posted by GitHub user **leloch** on 12 June 2026 and says it was "predominantly generated by an AI ... working under my direction" ([discussion #24528](https://github.com/ggml-org/llama.cpp/discussions/24528)). The author's handle is harshithkantamneni, and nothing in the notes links the two, so this report treats it as third-party prior art. The author should confirm this explicitly. A local llama.cpp branch named `expert-cache` is not evidence either way.

## The time-domain bound is the real contribution

The theory ingredients are individually known. Belady-with-bypass per layer is 2608.07911's. The reuse threshold has exactly the form of 2608.12103's breakeven count: one transfer cost divided by the per-use saving ([arXiv 2608.12103](https://arxiv.org/abs/2608.12103)). Bytes over bandwidth is the standard lens, and SPCL's own "Data Movement Is All You Need" is the lineage ([MLSys 2021](https://proceedings.mlsys.org/paper_files/paper/2021/hash/bc86e95606a6392f51f95a8de106728d-Abstract.html)).

What the literature lacks is the step from block counts to service time. 2608.07911 says outright that **"every result here is a block count"**, and that the step to service quality "passes through overlap, topology and contention we do not model" ([arXiv 2608.07911](https://arxiv.org/abs/2608.07911)). Load accounting plus a Jensen step, applied to any per-layer-capacity policy including CPU-executed misses, fills exactly that hole, and no paper examined uses Jensen at all. The cross-system validation is rarer still. MoE-Lens reports 94% accuracy on its own system in the throughput regime ([arXiv 2504.09345](https://arxiv.org/abs/2504.09345)). No paper checks a decode model against dozens of other groups' published numbers. The same 2608.07911 audit found that public reports "do not expose a common evaluation contract sufficient to compare cache-policy numbers across systems" ([arXiv 2608.07911](https://arxiv.org/abs/2608.07911)). A model that predicts 52 third-party measurements is a candidate for that missing contract, and that is the paper's best pitch.

The bound needs honest scoping, because "speed-of-light" invites attack. It covers exact-routing policies with per-layer capacity. FreeToken's shared all-layer cache is outside that class, and in an equal-capacity trace replay its global LRU missed **16% and 39%** of decode expert reads, versus **41% and 59%** for KTransformers' prefill-updated placement ([arXiv 2608.16157](https://arxiv.org/abs/2608.16157)). That comparison mixes global allocation with dynamic replacement, but a reviewer will still ask whether a global policy beats the "bound", and the paper needs an answer. Methods that change routing also sit outside it. One such paper claims to "surpass" the oracle cache bound because it alters routing ([arXiv 2412.00099](https://arxiv.org/abs/2412.00099)), and KTransformers' Expert Deferral is lossy. None of these are flaws. They are scope conditions that must sit in the bound's name and statement.

The bound's most interesting output is the gap it exposes. The mailbox cache reaches 46–63% of speed-of-light, against llama.cpp's 30–42%, so even the best measured system spends **more than a third of each step's time above the bound**. 2608.07911's decomposition suggests that the next gains will come from prediction and overlap rather than admission tuning. That is an inference, but a testable one, and it would be the paper's forward-looking result.

The tracing contribution is thinner than it sounds. Replaying tokens as a prefill is SeqMoE's idea. A llama.cpp eval-callback tracer is a "∼120-line addition" that already runs on models that fit only in host memory ([arXiv 2608.18261](https://arxiv.org/abs/2608.18261)). What remains is the GPU-free, layer-streamed execution, which is worth a tool section with measured cost and memory against that 120-line baseline. The repository contains exact-vs-reference and KV-cache decode exactness tests, and these should be reported prominently, including top-k agreement under the production quantized kernels. A reviewer will also ask why a system claimed to be exact shows a **0.6% teacher-forced NLL difference**. That figure invites comparison with KTransformers' lossy deferral, whose accuracy drop is "within 0.5%" ([SOSP'25](https://madsys.cs.tsinghua.edu.cn/publication/ktransformers-unleashing-the-full-potential-of-cpu/gpu-hybrid-inference-for-moe-models/SOSP25-chen.pdf)). The difference probably comes from kernel accumulation order in the custom AVX-512 MXFP4 path, and it should be shown to be that.

The three diagnoses are the most immediately *useful* part.

**Copy-engine queuing.** No llama.cpp cache effort names it. leloch's v2 fills on a least-priority stream ([moe-cache.cu](https://github.com/leloch/llama.cpp/blob/e3096b046bb809f7f80bc47801f6579aed1cbc60/ggml/src/ggml-cuda/moe-cache.cu)). Stream priority governs SM scheduling, not copy-engine order; that is general CUDA knowledge rather than a sourced finding. #27861 simply caps uploads at two per layer per step ([llama-moecache.cpp](https://github.com/ggml-org/llama.cpp/blob/bccbacdb8945680f1cfc7e6bffd1e59014705750/src/llama-moecache.cpp)), so the finding likely applies to every fork that fills its cache through copy-engine transfers.

**The ~84 µs/layer hand-off.** This measures an overhead class that is known but unquantified per layer. The null-work control is the right design. The colibri project's 54 ms/token "API time" turned out to be a "summation illusion", overlapped with other work ([colibri #431](https://github.com/JustVugg/colibri/issues/431)).

**The scheduler bug.** It duplicates nothing upstream, but it runs against upstream's recent direction. #26040 (merged 20 August 2026) fixed a race in which "splits without input were running concurrently with other splits" by adding a sync. A more aggressive sync reduction, #20793, was reverted after regressions ([commit 8497981](https://github.com/ggml-org/llama.cpp/commit/8497981); [PR #25138](https://github.com/ggml-org/llama.cpp/pull/25138)). The fix must be shown not to reintroduce that race.

The mailbox is a sound application of an old idea. GPU-to-CPU callbacks in 2010 already had the GPU put "a callback request in 0-copy memory and set a flag" before spinning ([PDF](https://escholarship.org/content/qt58j4v6f8/qt58j4v6f8.pdf?t=ptt3z6)). Upstream llama.cpp's own PCIe allreduce spins on pinned host memory with `volatile` stores and `__threadfence_system` ([allreduce.cu](https://github.com/ggml-org/llama.cpp/blob/master/ggml/src/ggml-cuda/allreduce.cu)). SeqMoE uses mapped memory as a GPU-to-CPU control region, but to drive a prefetcher, not CPU compute ([arXiv 2609.12978](https://arxiv.org/abs/2609.12978)). KTransformers' hand-off instead enqueues two host functions per MoE layer, and its `sync_` callback blocks on a condition variable while the stream idles ([cpuinfer.h](https://github.com/kvcache-ai/ktransformers/blob/main/kt-kernel/cpu_backend/cpuinfer.h)). That is a static node, so the GPU cannot skip it per layer. The mailbox's device-side skip is therefore real differentiation.

These are absence-of-evidence findings. HybriMoE's synchronisation code and a full 2026 arXiv sweep were not examined. Reviewers will also raise the costs: a spinning SM, 28 of 30 vCPUs busy-waiting, and liveness that depends on a timeout.

In magnitude the result is unremarkable. In rigour it is better than most, and in one respect it is understated. Fiddler's and KTransformers' llama.cpp baselines predate expert-only offload: KTransformers had to extend llama.cpp "with custom code to enable expert-level offloading" ([SOSP'25](https://madsys.cs.tsinghua.edu.cn/publication/ktransformers-unleashing-the-full-potential-of-cpu/gpu-hybrid-inference-for-moe-models/SOSP25-chen.pdf)). Today's `--n-cpu-moe` also has split-wise CUDA graphs, worth 1.05–1.10x on GPT-OSS 120B ([PR #18934](https://github.com/ggml-org/llama.cpp/pull/18934)). The one recent third-party measurement is suggestive: SeqMoE puts KTransformers at **34.2 tok/s versus 30.9** for llama.cpp static offload on Qwen3-30B-FP8 at 45% residency on an RTX 4090, only about 1.1x ([arXiv 2609.12978](https://arxiv.org/abs/2609.12978)). If that holds, 1.4–1.8x over modern llama.cpp could well beat KTransformers. The hardware differs and the flags are unknown, though, so the claim cannot be made transitively. It has to be measured.

That measurement is both feasible and fair on this VM. KTransformers' decode path already uses AVX-512, since AVX-512 beats AMX at four or fewer tokens per expert, so the missing AMX costs it little in decode. Its NUMA-aware tensor parallelism (up to 1.63x) needs two sockets, and its deferral must be set to zero for an exact comparison ([SOSP'25](https://madsys.cs.tsinghua.edu.cn/publication/ktransformers-unleashing-the-full-potential-of-cpu/gpu-hybrid-inference-for-moe-models/SOSP25-chen.pdf); [kt-kernel README](https://github.com/kvcache-ai/ktransformers/blob/main/kt-kernel/README.md)). kt-kernel has no gpt-oss support string, so the comparison has to run on Qwen3-30B-A3B. KTransformers' own data show where a dynamic cache has room. In batched ShareGPT runs at a 10% GPU-expert ratio, its prefill-time dynamic update reached **70.22 tok/s against 58.60** for frequency placement, with frequency placement winning at 90% ([KT tutorial](https://github.com/kvcache-ai/ktransformers/blob/main/doc/en/kt-kernel/experts-sched-Tutorial.md)). A per-token cache at 25% sits near the regime where, in KTransformers' own batched data, static placement is weakest.

The absolute numbers need the same care. **72.1 versus 39.8 tok/s** on gpt-oss-120b is high next to community reports: 25–28 tok/s on an RTX 4070 with dual-channel DDR5 ([carteakey](https://carteakey.dev/blog/local-inference/optimizing-gpt-oss-120b-local-inference/)), and 36.2 rising to 47.6 tok/s on an RTX 5090 with a hot set added on top of `--n-cpu-moe 25` ([moe-autopilot](https://github.com/JigSawPT/moe-autopilot)). The A10 VM's baseline already exceeds that 5090 baseline (39.8 versus 36.2 tok/s), which points to the server's ~150 GB/s DRAM, not the patch, setting the absolute level. That is why a second platform matters.

Two newer results will frame review. **SeqMoE**, a GPU-only prefetch design, reaches **104.1 tok/s and 80% of full-load speed at 45% residency** ([arXiv 2609.12978](https://arxiv.org/abs/2609.12978)), so reviewers will ask why misses run on the CPU instead of being fetched. The r* threshold and the bound are exactly the tools to answer when each regime wins, and the paper should say so explicitly. **FreeToken** reports 1.5–2.3x over the strongest baseline across six machines ([arXiv 2608.16157](https://arxiv.org/abs/2608.16157)).

The gain is also still unattributed. The hand-off alone (no cache) gives **1.02–1.32x**, which is the same band as the up to 1.23x KTransformers got from one graph built on host callbacks. Without a `cudaLaunchHostFunc` variant and a "llama.cpp plus scheduler fix only" baseline, a reviewer can credit the whole hand-off gain to a bug fix and to one-graph execution, and neither is the novel part. No published per-layer latency exists for host-callback hand-off inside CUDA graphs. An NVIDIA forum report of 12 ms stalls could not be reproduced by NVIDIA staff ([forum](https://forums.developer.nvidia.com/t/culaunchhostfunc-overhead-latency-usage-cpu-gpu-signaling/327066)). A microbenchmark comparing the two would therefore be new data in its own right.

The equal-VRAM control is the result's real strength. The one explicit equal-VRAM community test found a heat cache *losing* to static pinning on DeepSeek-V4-Flash, 12.77 versus 13.45 tok/s, where balanced routing over 256 experts gives an "11% theoretical hit rate" ([PR #26563](https://github.com/ggml-org/llama.cpp/pull/26563)). None of the four evaluated models is in that regime, and a reviewer will notice.

## No top-venue acceptance is possible before the ETH deadline

Every comparable accepted paper evaluated on at least two hardware configurations against two to four named baselines, including KTransformers (A100 and RTX 4080 against Fiddler and llama.cpp) ([SOSP'25](https://madsys.cs.tsinghua.edu.cn/publication/ktransformers-unleashing-the-full-potential-of-cpu/gpu-hybrid-inference-for-moe-models/SOSP25-chen.pdf)) and FineMoE (two testbeds, four baselines) ([EuroSys'26](https://intellisys.haow.us/assets/pdf/Hanfei_FineMoE_EuroSys26.pdf)). The closest precedent is the MLSys 2026 oral "Efficient, VRAM-Constrained xLM Inference on Clients". It is **built on llama.cpp, uses llama.cpp's `-cmoe` offload as its baseline, and runs Qwen3-30B/235B on three client platforms**, reporting an average **3.7x** decode gain ([arXiv 2604.26334](https://arxiv.org/html/2604.26334v1); [MLSys oral](https://mlsys.org/virtual/2026/oral/3802)). A single A10 VM with only llama.cpp as a baseline falls below all of them, and it will be read against that paper.

The bound-first framing fits Hoefler's taste precisely. Rule 11 of his benchmarking rules is **"If possible, show upper performance bounds"**, and his survey found only 2 of 95 papers reporting confidence intervals ([Hoefler & Belli, SC'15](https://htor.inf.ethz.ch/publications/img/hoefler-scientific-benchmarking.pdf)). MLSys reviews arrive on 18 January, before the symposium, so a submission gives real feedback to talk about ([MLSys 2027](https://mlsys.org/Conferences/2027)). The realistic plan is to post an arXiv preprint and code before 27 October, submit to MLSys on 30 October only if the KTransformers comparison, the hand-off ablation and the statistics are done, and otherwise add a platform and target ISPASS in December, with EuroMLSys as the floor.

## Conclusion

Between April 2025 and August 2026 at least seven independent groups converged on the same core design, a GPU expert cache with CPU-executed misses, often with frequency- or decay-based admission: HybriMoE, 2512.16473, DALI, FreeToken and three llama.cpp contributors. KTransformers had already put CPU experts inside one decode graph. The scarce commodity is no longer another cache. It is a trustworthy yardstick that says how far any of them sits from the hardware limit, and why their published speedups do not compare. This work already has that yardstick in a time-domain bound, a model that predicts other groups' numbers, and an equal-VRAM protocol. It should be retitled and reframed so that the system serves as evidence that the yardstick predicts, rather than as the contribution.

Read that way, very little of the effort is useless. What is useless is the novelty *claims* on the cache, the admission rule, the one-graph goal, r*, Belady-with-bypass and teacher-forced replay, and those should be deleted from the abstract, not defended. The mailbox with its GPU-decided skip is the main systems idea with no known precedent in LLM inference. It earns a place only once a host-callback variant shows how much of the 1.02–1.32x belongs to it, and that experiment, together with the KTransformers head-to-head, decides between MLSys and a workshop.
