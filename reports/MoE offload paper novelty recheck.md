# Narrow every claim; keep every measurement

As of 28 September 2026, no published work takes any of the paper's five contributions whole, and nothing public matches the new same-machine race. But **no contribution is new in its general form**: each one survives only as a specific combination, and several sentences in the current draft are false as written. The time-domain bound combines elapsed-time caching theory that is 25–30 years old (Cao 1995, Albers–Garg–Leonardi 2000, Jain & Lin 2018, CHOPT 2020) with MoE paging work from 2025–26. Three September papers ("Budgeting Bytes", "Paging the Experts", WiSP v2) come close enough that they must be cited and set apart. GenZ already validated an analytical LLM model on third-party data in 2024–25, so the model's claim narrows to out-of-sample, multi-source validation of hybrid-offload batch-1 decode, plus a pre-registered numeric prediction. The audit and the reporting contract are intact. The provenance study is the most novel piece, but it must stop calling teacher-forced dataset text "the norm", because most cache-systems papers trace their own generations. In the new first-party work, FETCH and PREFETCH are mechanisms already published by FreeToken, HybriMoE, DALI and Speculating Experts. What is new is measured: FETCH's gain changes sign across hosts, the first published numbers for host-DRAM contention (which contradict FreeToken's paper model, 5 GB/s predicted against 37 GB/s measured), and a protocol that races FreeToken with its own benchmark script, at equal expert memory, against a bound. Thirty-three works named below have no entry in `refs.bib`. Reviewers will not accept the race as it stands. It has one measured request per prompt over five prompts with a ±10% spread and no confidence intervals. Equal memory is set by flags rather than measured. The speed-of-light comes from own-text traces rather than the evaluated AIME text, and from measured bandwidths that the paper's own A100 anchor showed can be beaten. And it covers one model at 256 decode tokens.

## The bound is a synthesis of old caching theory and September's MoE papers

Every ingredient of the bound has an owner. In classic caching, Cao, Felten, Karlin and Li measured prefetching and caching in elapsed time and proved a conservative strategy "within a factor of two of optimal" ([Cao et al. 1995](https://homes.cs.washington.edu/~karlin/papers/sigmetrics.pdf)). Albers, Garg and Leonardi showed that "an optimum prefetching/caching schedule for a single disk problem can be computed in polynomial time" by linear programming, with total elapsed time as the objective ([JACM 2000](https://dl.acm.org/doi/10.1145/355541.355542)). The closest analogue of "load the expert, or serve it from the slower memory, whichever is cheaper" is **CHOPT**. It asks "which data should be cached in faster memory if it could instead be served directly from slower memory?", is proven optimal, and beats Belady on latency by 8.2–44.8% ([CHOPT, SIGMETRICS 2020](https://dl.acm.org/doi/10.1145/3379472)). The proof sketch's key step, "a prefetching schedule bridges no more gaps than some demand schedule with bypass", runs straight into Jain and Lin. They showed that MIN "minimizes the total number of cache misses—including those for prefetched lines—[but] does not minimize the number of demand misses", and introduced Demand-MIN ([ISCA 2018](https://www.cs.utexas.edu/~lin/papers/isca18.pdf)). On the MoE side, 2608.07911 already runs "Belady (offline optimum with bypass admission)" under per-layer and global scope ([arXiv 2608.07911](https://arxiv.org/abs/2608.07911)). Angelopoulos et al. introduced layered paging for MoE with Belady as the offline optimum ([arXiv 2509.02408](https://arxiv.org/abs/2509.02408)). 2608.12103 turned Belady into a miss headroom ("roughly one third of the misses LRU still incurs") and a break-even reuse count of 1.06 ([arXiv 2608.12103](https://arxiv.org/abs/2608.12103)). None of these works appears in `refs.bib` except Cao 1995 and 2608.07911/2608.12103.

September added three near misses, all of which a reviewer could cite against "first".

"**Paging the Experts**" (announced 25 Sep) computes a farthest-next-use oracle on a shared all-layer cache and then disclaims it: "a lower miss bound under equal-size, mandatory-admission paging, not an online speed prediction" ([arXiv 2609.29032](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2609.29032)). That quote is the best available evidence for the title's claim that the literature stops at blocks.

"**Budgeting Bytes**" is the largest risk. It derives "a closed-form windowed roofline: a full-hiding criterion and exposed-latency bound via EDF optimality", states that "no schedule beats bytes/B", and ships a planner that predicts tok/s for llama.cpp static offload ([arXiv 2609.04238](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2609.04238)). Its bound is exposed latency for a *given* fetch set on storage-bound edge devices. It has no miss minimisation, no per-layer or shared GPU budget, no CPU execution of misses, and it was validated against one published microcontroller deployment plus its own pipelines. The paper should call it the zero-cache, all-miss special case of Proposition 1.

**WiSP v2** (30 Aug) converts misses to seconds with "c_exp = M_e t_byte … T(f) = c_exp m_exp(C) + c_kv m_kv(κ)". Its miss curve is LRU's, so it is an estimator, not a bound ([arXiv 2606.21868v2](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2606.21868v2)). Citing all three turns a vulnerable "first" into a defensible sentence: prior bounds are block counts, exposed-latency bounds for a given schedule, or LRU-based estimators, and none is a lower bound in seconds over all exact-routing policies with CPU-executed misses.

The applied half of C1, expressing systems as a fraction of a physical bound, has partial precedents too. A May 2026 paper reports batch-1 *dense* decode as a fraction of the memory floor: "an L4 reaches roughly 81% of its analytic memory floor, while an H100 reaches only 27%" ([arXiv 2605.30571](https://arxiv.org/abs/2605.30571)). SeqMoE reports "80.22% of full-load performance", an implementation-relative ceiling ([arXiv 2609.12978](https://arxiv.org/abs/2609.12978)). MoE-CAP defines S-MBU against a single peak ([arXiv 2412.07067](https://arxiv.org/abs/2412.07067)). The physical speed-of-light for offloaded MoE remains unclaimed. The paper's *implementation-relative* version is SeqMoE-like, though, and should not be presented as new. The intro's "no paper we found states how fast its hardware *could* decode the model within the same GPU memory" survives only in the narrower form "no MoE-offloading paper reports a policy-independent lower bound at the same GPU memory". The title's shape echoes "Caching for Dollars, Not Hits", an exact offline reference for cloud-egress caching ([arXiv 2606.20539](https://arxiv.org/abs/2606.20539)). Citing it pre-empts a "derivative framing" remark.

Two technical challenges follow. First, a caching theorist will ask why the paper bounds rather than solves, since Albers et al. solve by LP and CHOPT by min-cost flow. The answer the notes support: CPU and GPU run concurrently, which puts a max(·) per layer-step, bandwidths are shared, and prefetching must be covered. A closed-form bound valid over all policies is what an audit needs, not an exact optimum for one execution model. The full proof, including the Jensen step and the prefetch reduction with Jain & Lin cited, belongs in an appendix rather than a sketch. Consequence (i), that the optimum runs part of even resident experts on the CPU, is close in spirit to FreeToken's residual-bandwidth split ([arXiv 2608.16157](https://arxiv.org/abs/2608.16157)) and Fiddler's per-miss rule. The novelty is validity over all policies, not the split.

Second, the denominator must be unbeatable. The paper's own A100 anchor registered A1 ("no configuration may beat the physical floor computed with STREAM Triad"), and A1 **failed**: seven Mixtral configurations beat the Triad-based floor by up to 16%. Against datasheet peaks every configuration stayed below 54% ([numbers4.tex](/home/claude/moe-speed-of-light/paper/numbers4.tex)). The new first-party fractions use a speed-of-light "from measured bandwidths" ([Progress log](/home/claude/moe-speed-of-light/reports/Progress%20log.md)), so they carry the same risk. A related trap is host DRAM shared between CPU reads and DMA. On the 62 GB/s host the two together delivered **73–77 GB/s, above the CPU-alone 62** ([prefetch_lookahead.md](/home/claude/moe-speed-of-light/research_notes/MoE%20offload%20system%20race%20plan/prefetch_lookahead.md)). A joint DRAM constraint calibrated on CPU-alone STREAM would therefore make the bound invalid, not tighter. This is an inference from the measurement: the bound should either keep the CPU and link terms independent, as now, or use the measured concurrent total.

C2 narrows more sharply than C1. GenZ validated a roofline-with-efficiency-factors model against third-party measurements: "We were unable to get access to the physical node for these architecture, so we used the number from LLM-Inference-Bench". It reports "a geomean error of 5.82% across all different architectures", with per-platform efficiencies (0.9, 0.25, 0.6) that appear to be set on the same data ([arXiv 2406.01698](https://arxiv.org/abs/2406.01698)). Qiu et al. use medians of bandwidth-efficiency ratios and report "Leave-one-host-out coefficients fitted on the other two systems yield 11.6%, 16.8%, and 36.0% test MAPE" on all-GPU and unified-memory hosts ([arXiv 2609.14864](https://arxiv.org/abs/2609.14864)). The draft's "13–36% error on three hosts" should be checked against those figures. LIMINAL fits exposed latency to its own H100 runs, reaching 7.6% MAPE ([arXiv 2507.14397](https://arxiv.org/abs/2507.14397)). WattGPU explicitly defers MoE ([arXiv 2607.02391](https://arxiv.org/abs/2607.02391)). Pre-registration is precedented in this niche: 2608.07911 froze its thresholds before measurement, and 2608.18261 is titled "A Pre-Registered Negative Result" ([arXiv 2608.18261](https://arxiv.org/abs/2608.18261)). The ML community held pre-registration workshops in 2020–21 ([PMLR v148](https://proceedings.mlr.press/v148/)).

The defensible C2 claim therefore has four parts. It is out of sample (leave-one-group-out), where GenZ is in sample. It draws on many independent sources rather than one benchmark repository. It covers hybrid CPU/GPU offload at batch 1, which GenZ, LIMINAL, WattGPU and Qiu do not. And it pre-registers a *numeric accuracy threshold* for a new platform (H5 ≤10%, met at 6.7%), which no source found does for performance predictions. Reviewers will also see that the H5 median rests on the 13 gpt-oss configurations of the calibration model's family, while the 16 Qwen3 configurations err by 3–45%. Report the median per family.

## FreeToken's own script makes the race new; FETCH and PREFETCH are not

Running baselines on one's own machine is the norm, so "same machine" is not a contribution. What is unoccupied is the combination. The referee is a competitor's unmodified benchmark script: FreeToken's `bench_decode_moe.py`, which the harness matches within 1.3%. llama.cpp is tuned at equal expert memory. And three systems are reported against a bound. Published comparisons fall short of that. FreeToken's llama.cpp baseline is a "routing-blind static split" with no flags. SeqMoE's is "Static Offload" at 30.9 tok/s. KTransformers extended a Feb-2025 llama.cpp by hand. Pipelined Sharding ran `-cmoe` ([arXiv 2608.16157](https://arxiv.org/html/2608.16157v1); [arXiv 2609.12978](https://arxiv.org/abs/2609.12978); [arXiv 2604.26334](https://arxiv.org/html/2604.26334)). FreeToken's paper evaluates no gpt-oss model, and SeqMoE runs GPT-OSS-120B only on a 96 GB RTX PRO 6000. The only public same-box FreeToken-vs-llama.cpp figure is an issue report of 22 vs 53 tok/s on an RTX 5090, with undocumented flags and no memory parity ([llama.cpp #27685](https://github.com/ggml-org/llama.cpp/issues/27685)). The paper should cite it as the informal claim being tested.

The results revise FreeToken's baseline story more than they crown a winner. On two RTX 5090 hosts, the llama.cpp cache with FETCH led FreeToken's better mode by **+18–21% at 11% of experts, +9–11% at 25% and +2–3% at 40%**, and it reached a 44% budget that FreeToken could not fit ([Progress log](/home/claude/moe-speed-of-light/reports/Progress%20log.md)). Every system varied by about ±10% across the five AIME problems. Only the 11% gap is clearly outside that spread, the 25% gap is borderline, and the 40% gap is noise. Against llama.cpp at its best `--n-cpu-moe`, the cache was 1.9× / 2.7× / 3.2–3.3×.

The stock baseline looks sound against outside data. On a near-identical 5090 with a 9950X3D, the community measured about 36 tok/s at `--n-cpu-moe 25` ([moe-autopilot](https://github.com/JigSawPT/moe-autopilot)). Here, llama.cpp ran 28.2–29.2 tok/s at `-ncmoe 27` and 36.4–38.9 at `-ncmoe 20`. Because the cache is the author's own, reviewers applying Agrawal et al.'s "Neglecting Parameter Tuning" anti-pattern will expect three things ([arXiv 2507.09019](https://arxiv.org/html/2507.09019)). First, a conflict disclosure. Second, a tuning budget for FreeToken at least equal to the cache's: its `ft bench bw` overlapped profile and a `--moe-hybrid-max-fetch` sweep, not only its two named modes. Third, the fact that the FETCH table was chosen on one host and ran out of sample on the other, which is already true and worth stating.

FETCH is FreeToken's hybrid mode re-implemented inside llama.cpp. FreeToken "dynamically divides the m missing experts between PCIe transfer and CPU execution based on the balance of two measured bandwidths". It derives q⋆ ≈ m·B_P/B_H and runs the fill through a zero-copy gather kernel captured in the CUDA graph ([arXiv 2608.16157](https://arxiv.org/abs/2608.16157)). HybriMoE split by simulation in April 2025 ([arXiv 2504.05897](https://arxiv.org/abs/2504.05897)), and DALI by 0-1 integer programming ([arXiv 2602.03495](https://arxiv.org/abs/2602.03495)). Differences such as an integer table instead of a rounded fraction, or a CPU-only baseline instead of an always-≥1 fill, are cosmetic.

Two findings are new. The first is that **FETCH's gain changes sign across hosts**. In the serial form it was +2–6% on a 62/57 GB/s host and +13–17% on a 46.5/46.5 GB/s host; overlapped it was +14–19% on the latter; and it lost 16% on a PCIe 4.0 host whose link is about half its DRAM bandwidth ([Progress log](/home/claude/moe-speed-of-light/reports/Progress%20log.md)). No paper reports a loss from splitting misses. FreeToken's PCIe 4.0 wins are against other engines, not against its own `--moe-hybrid-max-fetch 0`.

The second is the **first published DRAM-contention numbers**. FreeToken's paper equation assumes full contention, "B_R = max(B_H − B_P, 0)". On the 62/57 host that predicts the CPU keeps 5 GB/s under DMA. It measured 37 GB/s, and the pair together delivered about 75. The paper's q⋆/m = 0.92 would fetch almost every miss, where the measured-contention split gives 0.51. FreeToken's code already moved away from the paper equation: `measure_overlap_bw` says full contention "over-penalizes a CPU kernel that never saturated DRAM" ([FreeToken benchbw.py](https://github.com/FlashML-org/FreeToken/blob/main/python/freetoken/moe/benchbw.py)). So the correction is to FreeToken's paper, and the paper should say so plainly.

The start-up bandwidth probe the progress log plans for is FreeToken's `ft bench bw`, which switches to hybrid mode at a 2× CPU:PCIe threshold. That is prior art to cite, not a feature to claim. One point of tension remains open. On a link ≈ DRAM host such as the 9950X, FreeToken's rule picks pure offload, fetching every miss, while FETCH keeps some misses on the CPU and gains. The missing arm is **fetch-all inside the same cache at equal memory**.

PREFETCH has a long lineage. Mixtral-offloading applied "next layer's gating function to previous layer's hidden states" in December 2023 ([arXiv 2312.17238](https://arxiv.org/abs/2312.17238)). HOBBIT stacked several next-layer gates into one matmul ([arXiv 2411.01433](https://arxiv.org/abs/2411.01433)). Speculating Experts applies layer l+1's own norm, "q_l = LN_{l+1}(d_l + r_l)", on gpt-oss-20b/120b ([arXiv 2603.19289](https://arxiv.org/abs/2603.19289)). The paper's folded norm-ratio matrix computes the same quantity. Absorbing RMSNorm gains into adjacent weights is QuaRot's standard move ([arXiv 2404.00456](https://arxiv.org/abs/2404.00456)), so the fold earns one sentence and no claim. The measured recall (84% / 97% at top-4 / top-8 on gpt-oss-120b) sits above Speculating Experts' trained estimator (83% / 88%) and the 64.7% recall of 2608.12103's production predictor ([arXiv 2608.12103](https://arxiv.org/abs/2608.12103)). Context-length dependence is already reported: "overlapping compute with copy through prefetching yields greater benefits at longer sequence lengths" ([arXiv 2603.19289](https://arxiv.org/abs/2603.19289)). The paper's +10–16% on long own-text prompts against +4–6% on 109-token chat prompts confirms it rather than discovers it.

What is new is the host dependence against a CPU-miss baseline. Every prefetch paper compares against on-demand copying, where prefetch cannot turn negative the way it did here (−16% on PCIe 4.0). WiSP's finding that prefetch "lowers decode throughput by 38–60%" on an RTX 3090 is the matching data point from the other side ([arXiv 2606.21868](https://arxiv.org/abs/2606.21868)). Two further details are new but minor: waiting for a late prefetch beats CPU fallback, and the side-stream plumbing is captured inside the decode graph. The latter should cite llama.cpp's own event fork/join inside graphs ([PR #16991](https://github.com/ggml-org/llama.cpp/pull/16991)). The pre-registered prediction (+17/+17/+14% against a measured +14/+16/+10%) is a clean example of the paper's method applied to its own system, and it should be shown as such.

The host lottery has solid precedent. Maricq et al. found that "an unbalanced DIMM configuration caused newer c220g2 servers to underperform older c220g1 servers by a factor of nearly 3", with STREAM CoV of 14.5–16% on one server type ([OSDI'18](https://users.soe.ucsc.edu/~carlosm/dev/publication/maricq-osdi-18/maricq-osdi-18.pdf)). Sinha et al. found "8% (max 22%) average performance variation" across identical GPUs ([arXiv 2208.11035](https://arxiv.org/abs/2208.11035)). Vast.ai verification checks that PCIe "provide[s] full bandwidth", but no provider exposes DIMM count or speed ([Vast.ai docs](https://docs.vast.ai/documentation/host/understanding-verification)).

The first-party result extends this to a setting no one has characterised. Two RTX 5090 + 9950X rentals differed by **19–22% in decode speed at 46.6 vs 62 GB/s** of DRAM bandwidth, while re-runs on one host repeated within 0.8%. A third rental of the same class moved experts over its link at only 28.9 GB/s against 57, so the lottery covers the link as well as memory ([Progress log](/home/claude/moe-speed-of-light/reports/Progress%20log.md)). The sampled text was bit-identical across hosts (hit rates equal to six digits), which makes the cross-host comparison a true paired design. With two hosts per class, though, this is an observation, not a characterisation.

## Reviewers will ask for twelve things before believing the race

The statistics are the most likely rejection reason, and they are cheap to fix. Hoefler and Belli found only 2 of 95 HPC papers reporting confidence intervals, and their rules require nonparametric CIs, harmonic means for rates and no averaged ratios ([SC'15](https://htor.inf.ethz.ch/publications/img/hoefler-scientific-benchmarking.pdf)). Georges et al. showed that "Best of N" misled in up to 16% of comparisons and prescribe CIs across independent invocations ([OOPSLA'07](https://dri.es/files/oopsla07-georges.pdf)). Mytkowicz et al. moved a speed-up across 0.92–1.10 by link order alone ([ASPLOS'09](https://users.cs.northwestern.edu/~robby/courses/322-2013-spring/mytkowicz-wrong-data.pdf)). The race currently has one warm-up and one measured request per problem, over five problems.

Two non-experimental fixes belong on the same list. The project's GitHub repository is publicly readable, and it was the first web result for a query on the paper's topic ([GitHub repo](https://github.com/harshithkantamneni/moe-speed-of-light)). At a double-blind venue such as MLSys 2027 (due 30 October), that breaks anonymity unless it is made private or the paper is posted to arXiv first under the venue's preprint rules. And the case-study section must now credit FETCH to FreeToken, HybriMoE and DALI, and PREFETCH to Mixtral-offloading and Speculating Experts, in the same "we use the system only as one system under test" voice it already uses for the cache.

## Conclusion

The recheck moves the paper's centre of gravity. On 27 September the bound was the headline and the system an embarrassment. Today the bound is a defensible synthesis that must cite a 2000 JACM paper and three September preprints, and the most distinctive evidence comes from the newest work: a race refereed by a competitor's own script, and contention numbers that correct a competitor's published model. That work is worth most as a demonstration of the paper's own method, not as a system. The paper predicted its own feature's gain before measuring (+17/+17/+14% against +14/+16/+10%), measured a host lottery that its model should be able to predict, and found that a mechanism flips sign with the PCIe-to-DRAM ratio. Each of these is the paper's thesis applied to itself. Framed that way, FETCH and PREFETCH stop being unoriginal mechanisms and become test cases for "report in seconds against a bound, with the host measured".

The binding risk is no longer being scooped. It is being beaten by one's own denominator and one's own noise. A speed-of-light that a measured system can exceed stops being a speed-of-light, and the A100 anchor already showed a measured-bandwidth floor failing by 16%. A +3% lead inside a ±10% spread cannot be defended. Fixing the denominator (datasheet peaks plus the trace of the evaluated text) and the statistics (launches × prompts with paired CIs) costs hours, not weeks, and it decides whether the revision reads as a rigorous measurement paper or as a fast, careful engineering log.
