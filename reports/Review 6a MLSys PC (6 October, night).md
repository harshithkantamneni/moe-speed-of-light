## 1. Summary

The paper studies batch-1 decode of MoE models whose experts mostly live in host DRAM of a desktop with one consumer GPU (RTX 5090, plus a 4090 and a 3090 in an appendix). The models are gpt-oss-120b (MXFP4) and Qwen3-30B-A3B (BF16), and the workload is 30 AIME-25 problems at 256 greedy tokens. It makes four contributions:

1. **A bound on time per token** (Eq. 1) for any system that executes exact routing with C GPU slots per layer. It combines the host reads of Belady's MIN with bypass on the model's own routing trace, the machine's highest probed host read rate, and a GPU term. The authors' llama.cpp expert cache (a 3,500-line patch) reaches 25-43% of the bound, or 31-56% with the GPU at its measured batch-1 rate. It is faster than FreeToken in 11 of 12 configurations and 2.0-4.0x stock llama.cpp. Scored the same way, 20 published batch-1 measurements reach a median of 13.6% of the bound.
2. **An in-engine oracle factorial.** Oracles that read the recorded future routing run inside the engine on three "full-design" hosts and on a 10-host panel. The key finding is an interaction between two choices:
   - admitting only what MIN admits, and
   - reading each admitted expert from host memory once rather than twice (CPU serves the miss, then a background copy).

   Either choice alone is worth little. Together they give 1.16-1.51x over the deployed cache on fast-link hosts, and 1.25-1.81x when the copies are issued three steps ahead. Whether reading in the step pays depends on the host's link-to-CPU read ratio. A calibrated bandwidth model reproduces each state's time (median error 2.2%) and the sign of the gain on all 43 host-budgets.
3. **A price on foresight.** Degraded windows (exact horizon W; recall r) are measured in the engine and in an event-level trace simulator on nine models. Half of MIN's read saving needs 2-10 exact tokens of foresight. Across models, the half-gain horizon is where the window holds about 0.65C distinct experts per layer. This rule predicts a held-out model's horizon slightly better than a power law in C/k. None of the realisable sources reaches that horizon: online policies, a linear or GRU forecaster, a learned admission order, or speculative batches.
4. **A preregistration discipline.** Every job's predictions are committed before the machine starts. A scorecard grades 565 + 273 clauses, failures included.

## 2. Strengths

- **S1. Measurement rigour well above the norm for this venue.**
  - Predictions are committed before each run. I confirmed the commit order in the GPU branch for jobs 096 and 099.
  - Configuration order is randomised with a cold cache per configuration, paired bootstraps are used, and the panel treats the host as the unit with a two-stage bootstrap.
  - The paper reports that host-to-host variation (SD 0.154 of the speed ratio) dwarfs within-host intervals (median half-width 0.009). This is a methodological point many offloading papers ignore.
  - The full scorecard, including 142 failed clauses, is published and generated by script. I have rarely seen a systems paper report its own prediction failures this candidly.
- **S2. A non-obvious, actionable finding.** The admission-set x read-count interaction (Table 3, Fig. 1) explains why "just add a predictor" or "just prefetch like Belady" designs often disappoint. Belady prefetch reads 1.6-2.3x MIN's bytes. Reading the deployed policy's admissions once gains nothing, because the deployed policy rations admissions. MIN's admissions read twice also gain little, because each extra admission puts a second read on the critical path. This is the kind of mechanism-level result MLSys should publish.
- **S3. The host dependence is shown, not asserted.** Fig. 2 and the calibrated model tie the sign of the in-step single read's gain to the host's link-to-CPU read ratio across 14 hosts, including a host where it loses (0.87x). This turns "it depends on the machine" into a quantitative rule.
- **S4. Restating the horizon in distinct experts** (D(W50) ≈ 0.65C) is a clean, transferable target for router or forecaster designers. It connects sensibly to the paging-with-lookahead literature.
- **S5. Careful negative results.**
  - The online policies tested sit within a few percent of each other and 35-110% above MIN.
  - The linear and GRU forecasters, the learned admission order and speculative batches all fall well short of the needed horizon.
  - The explanation for the forecaster result is plausible: its correct forecasts are mostly experts the cache already holds.
- **S6. An exemplary artifact.** Every number I checked regenerates from committed JSON or raw rows (Section 6). The unit tests verify MIN-with-bypass optimality against brute force. The rental ledger, job scripts and patches are present.

## 3. Weaknesses (ranked, most important first)

**W1. The paper is very hard to read. This is the main obstacle to acceptance (see Section 4).**
- Nearly every sentence carries two to five numeric ranges, often with nested conditions ("on the 10 hosts whose link reads at least half as fast as their CPU... read twice is worth -13 to 26%; read once, 26-57%").
- Core concepts are introduced in passing or only in appendices: "read once in the step", "admission set", "the rest", "host-bound", "full design", "the panel", hosts O1-O6/A/B/S and Pa-Pj, jobs 069c-099.
- The main text leans on 18 pages of appendices for definitions and evidence.
- The abstract alone has about 20 numbers and is close to unparseable on first read. A reader cannot extract the three findings in a few minutes.

*Fix:* rewrite the main text around the three findings, one figure or table each.
- Add a one-paragraph glossary and a two-by-two diagram of the factorial up front.
- Report one or two headline numbers per claim in the text and move ranges to tables.
- Drop host and job identifiers from the main text.
- Move the scorecard narrative (Appendix B prose) to the supplement.

**W2. "Where the seconds go" is only partly answered, and the conclusion overstates what was measured.**
- In the measured accounting (Table 3), "the rest" (what no oracle touches) is 30-62% of the gap on the full-design hosts. On the panel it is 43-45% for fast-link hosts and 73% and 91% for the two slow-link hosts.
- It is then explained only qualitatively or by model: imprecise prefetch, partial overlap, the GPU at about half its datasheet rate, and the bound letting the two read paths share B_host freely.
- Yet the Conclusion states that "the missing time is mostly the price of not knowing the future." On the measured evidence, foresight-attributable shares are roughly 38-70% on fast-link hosts and 9-32% on slow-link hosts. "Mostly" holds only for some cells.

*Fix:* scope the conclusion ("on fast-link hosts, about half of the gap..."). Then decompose "the rest" with measurements already within reach:
- an overlap-off state;
- the GPU-only time at batch 1 (already measured: 3.92 ms vs 2.02 ms datasheet);
- a version of the bound that constrains reads to a feasible CPU/link split, i.e. the two-path bound.

**W3. The bound is loose in a host-dependent way, so "% of bound" is partly a property of the bound.**
- At the host-bound budgets the minimiser sits at c = 0 (an argmin tie). The host term then divides R* bytes by B_host, the highest of any probe line. On host B that is the concurrent CPU + copy-engine sum, 87.5 GB/s.
- The link alone delivers about 53-57 GB/s. Reaching 87.5 GB/s therefore requires running a specific fraction of misses on the CPU, which the bound does not charge for (no CPU compute, no hand-off latency).
- The GPU term at datasheet rate is also about 2x optimistic at batch 1.
- Neither looseness invalidates the bound, and the paper says so in Section 7. But the headline "25-43% of the bound" and the audit's "median 13.6%" compare systems against a ceiling whose slack differs by host. The slow-link hosts' large "rest" is largely this relaxation.

*Fix:* add the two-path-constrained bound alongside Eq. (1). For each c, require c·S/B_c and (R* - c)·S/B_p, plus a joint constraint (R*·S)/B_cp. Report both, and say which one the "% of bound" headline uses.

**W4. Narrow scope and thin coverage where the claims are most interesting.**
- The workload is batch 1, two production models and one dataset (AIME-25, also used during development), at 256 tokens, with teacher-forced replay.
- The full factorial ran on three hosts.
- The panel covers 10 hosts at two gpt-oss budgets with 20 of the 30 problems (panel_099.json: 220 rows per cell file = 11 states x 20 sequences; the paper does not say this). Qwen3 appears only at one budget on three hosts, and only for the recall-0.5 window.
- The slow-link class, which carries the "which path pays" story, is two hosts. The link-ratio correlation of 0.89 (pooled over two budgets, 28 points) falls to 0.59 when those two hosts are excluded.
- The 0.65C horizon rule is established on traces in a simulator, not in the engine. In-engine evidence is gpt-oss only.

*Fix:* see Section 9 (more slow-link hosts, a Qwen3 in-engine W-sweep, one non-AIME workload).

**W5. Practical significance is bounded.**
- The paper's prescriptive output is a target ("about 0.65C distinct experts ahead") that no tested mechanism reaches.
- The deployable system's lead over FreeToken is real but modest and model-dependent:
  - 1.09-1.29x on gpt-oss;
  - 1.03-1.15x on Qwen3, with one loss at 0.974;
  - a tie on FreeToken's own Qwen3.6 model.
- So the paper's value is mainly diagnostic. That is fine for MLSys, but the paper should own it and say what a designer should do differently tomorrow. The single-read admission path on fast-link hosts and the per-host FETCH table appear to be the concrete takeaways.

*Fix:* add a short "implications for designers" paragraph. If possible, show one realisable policy that captures even a fraction of the interaction, for example MIN-like admission driven by the decayed-count signal with single-read fetch.

**W6. The calibrated model is explanatory, not predictive, and the text says "predicts".**
- In scripts/hostdep_model.py, G is fitted per host-cell on the deployed state, and each other state's time is computed from that state's measured engine counters (misses, fetches, admissions).
- The 2.2% median error is therefore conditional on knowing the counters of the run being "predicted".
- States with background copies err by a median 18%, and the shared-traffic variant by about 7%.
- The blind test of Appendix C is a stronger result, and it is about the FETCH table, not the oracle states.

*Fix:*
- Use "reproduces" or "explains, given counters" where appropriate.
- Report the error when counters come from the trace simulator, which the paper says matches engine reads within 2-4%, and with one G per host rather than per cell.

**W7. Novelty per component is incremental. The combination is the contribution (see Section 5).**
- Bandwidth/roofline bounds for MoE offloading (WiSP, Budgeting Bytes, MoE-CAP), the gap between causal policies and MIN with bypass (Zhang [61]), lookahead-oracle hit rate vs C/k across many models (Liang et al. [36]), and an in-engine clairvoyant oracle (SAEM [56]) all precede or parallel parts of this work.
- The distinct-pages view of lookahead is classical (Albers [2]).
- The held-out advantage of D(W) = cC over the power law is small: median error factor 1.16 vs 1.19, 90th percentile 1.42 vs 1.51, 26 points from 9 models. No uncertainty is given on the difference.

*Fix:*
- Sharpen the related-work positioning: state what each closest work measures and what this paper adds (time domain, interaction, host dependence).
- Bootstrap over models for the difference in held-out error.
- Temper "predicts better than a power law" to "matches or slightly improves on".

**W8. The published-systems audit is a sensitive comparison on soft inputs.**
- It scores other papers' measurements against datasheet ceilings, taking the top of the host-DRAM band.
- It sometimes uses sibling-model traces, e.g. an FP8 trace for a Q4 deployment.
- 4 of the 20 in-class rows have imputed per-layer capacity (prereg/audit/audit.json: budget_imputed).
- The paper is appropriately caveated in Appendix J, but the Introduction places "published systems ... a median of 13.6%" next to "ours 18-42%".

*Fix:*
- Report the in-class median excluding imputed rows, with a range under ceiling choices.
- Keep the audit as context rather than a headline.

**W9. Citation accuracy and coverage.**
- The SAEM entry ([56]: "Zhang, Gao, and Mitra. SAEM: Prediction-oracle upper-bound analysis of expert offloading", arXiv:2608.21614) does not match the paper at that arXiv ID. That paper is titled "SAEM: Stage-Aware Expert Management for Memory-Efficient MoE Inference in Chain-of-Thought Reasoning", and the bib entry gives surnames only. The characterisation in Section 6 ("runs a clairvoyant oracle in its engine and finds it worth at most about 14% on an A100") needs checking against the actual paper.
- SpecMD (Apple, ICML 2026; arXiv:2602.03921), a benchmarking study of MoE expert-cache and prefetch policies across hardware, is missing. It is directly relevant to Table 12 and to the claim that online policies are close to one another.
- Many references are 2026 preprints. A pass to verify every title, author list and characterisation is warranted.

**Minor issues**
- Section 2 says "ratios are ratios of those means" (mean time per token). Table 2 actually reports the ratio of mean per-problem tok/s. The difference is at most 0.003, as Appendix A rule 3 discloses, but the two statements should agree.
- Table 2's host S header lists "CPU 56 GB/s, link 53, together 66". The bound for host S uses B_host = 71.3 GB/s, from a copy-engine + CPU concurrent probe line, as Table 7 shows. State which rate the bound uses in Table 2's caption.
- The cost line (66 rentals, $55.9 for jobs 058-098) omits job 099, the panel behind Sections 4-5. That job cost another $9.32 by the ledger.
- "MIN's way beats the best usual way by 1.18-2.03x" refers to the paced prefetch, not to MIN with one read. MIN with one read loses to Belady-prefetched at O4 gpt-oss 25% (1.43 vs 1.53). Name the state.
- The audit quartiles are index-based. The interpolated upper quartile is 20.6%, not 23.1%.
- Figure 1 has 9 rows x 6 panels x 5 marker types plus grey ticks. It is very busy, and the grey panel ticks are hard to read at print size.
- "Shapley value" for a two-factor 2x2 design is just the average of the two orderings. That is fine, but saying so would demystify Table 3.

## 4. Clarity

**Score: 2 / 5.** The science is careful, but the prose is written for someone who already ran the experiments. The three hardest passages follow.

**Passage 1 (Abstract):**
> "Foresight pays when two choices go together: admit only what MIN admits, and read each admitted expert from host memory once, on a path with room. Where the PCIe link reads at least half as fast as the CPU, MIN's admissions read once in the step recover 26–60% of the gap to the bound, while either choice alone recovers at most 26%. Where the link is much slower, the same reads must be copied ahead of use, and a calibrated model of the two read paths predicts which way pays on each machine."

*Problems:* "on a path with room", "read once in the step" and "copied ahead of use" are undefined at this point. "Either choice alone" requires the reader to have already built the 2x2 in their head. "Which way pays" is vague.

*Suggested rewrite:*
> "Knowing future routing helps only when two decisions are made together: admit only the experts Belady's MIN would admit, and copy each admitted expert to the GPU exactly once, instead of first running it on the CPU and then copying it. On hosts whose PCIe link reads at least half as fast as the CPU reads DRAM, doing both closes 26–60% of the gap to the bound; either change alone closes at most 26%. On hosts with a slower link, the copy pays only if issued a few tokens ahead; a bandwidth model of the two read paths tells which case a host is in."

**Passage 2 (Section 4, "An order-free accounting"):**
> "On the 10 hosts whose link reads at least half as fast as their CPU, reading the deployed policy's admissions once is worth −4 to 4% of the gap: the policy rations admissions to a few per token, and their background copies overlap later steps. MIN's admission set read twice is worth −13 to 26%; read once, 26–57%. The difference, the interaction, is 10–58% of the gap: MIN admits 2–11 times as many experts per token as the deployed policy, and with two reads each of those admissions is read again on the critical path. Averaged over the two orders, the Shapley values are 9–35% for the admission set and 1–32% for the single read."

*Problems:* there are four effects, each a range over 10 hosts and 4 budgets, in four sentences. The reader must reconstruct which cell of the 2x2 each number refers to, and "worth" means "share of the gap closed".

*Suggested rewrite:* replace the prose with a 2x2 inset (rows: deployed vs MIN admissions; columns: two reads vs one read). Each cell gives the median share of the gap closed across fast-link hosts, with the range below it. Then write one sentence:
> "Changing either factor alone closes at most a quarter of the gap; changing both closes 26–57%, because MIN admits 2–11x more experts per token and, read twice, every extra admission costs a second host read on the critical path."

**Passage 3 (Section 5, "In the engine"):**
> "Time follows reads, weighted by the path each read takes. At 11% a 4-token window makes 0.52 of MIN's cut in host reads, but still reads 97% of its misses over the link in the step, where MIN reads 50%. Where the link is the slower path, its share of the time gain is therefore smaller than its share of the read gain: by 0.05 on hosts whose link most matches the CPU, and by up to 0.20 on the most link-starved (link/CPU 0.29; correlation of the gap with the log ratio −0.91)."

*Problems:* "makes 0.52 of MIN's cut", "reads 97% of its misses over the link in the step, where MIN reads 50%" and "its share of the time gain is smaller than its share of the read gain" stack three fractions of different denominators. "Where the link is the slower path" is ambiguous: slower than what, and on which hosts?

*Suggested rewrite:*
> "A window's value in time is lower than its value in bytes when it pushes reads onto a slow link. At gpt-oss 11%, a 4-token window removes 52% of the host reads MIN removes, but it fetches 97% of its remaining misses over PCIe (MIN fetches 50%). On hosts whose link is as fast as their CPU, this costs little (time share 0.05 below byte share); on the most link-starved host (link/CPU = 0.29) it costs 0.20."

*General suggestions:*
- Define each term once, in a glossary box.
- Never use a host or job identifier in the main text.
- Keep the abstract to about 8 numbers.
- Give every figure a one-sentence takeaway in its caption's first line.

## 5. Novelty against related work

**Closest prior or concurrent work, and the delta:**
- **Liang et al., ICLR 2026 [36] ("Not All Models Suit Expert Offloading").** Measures, across about 20 MoE models, the hit rate of an oracle cache that sees m tokens ahead, as a function of cache size. This is the closest antecedent to Section 5's horizon study. *New here:* the oracle is run in a real engine in time rather than hit rate, together with the distinct-expert restatement and a held-out-model test.
- **Zhang [61] ("Reproducible Evaluation of MoE Expert Caching").** Reports a 44-46% gap between causal policies and MIN with bypass under a replay contract. *New here:* the paper confirms a similar gap (35-110% excess reads) and then measures what closing it is worth in seconds, on real hosts.
- **SAEM [56].** Cited as an in-engine clairvoyant oracle worth at most about 14% on an A100. The citation needs correcting (W9). If the characterisation holds, the novelty is the factorial decomposition and the host dependence, not the in-engine oracle itself.
- **WiSP [58], Budgeting Bytes [57], MoE-CAP [27], roofline [50].** Bandwidth-over-bytes bounds for MoE decode. *New here:* the per-layer MIN-with-bypass read count as the byte term, two read paths sharing host bandwidth, and application to 52 published rows.
- **FreeToken [54], DALI [65], Fiddler [29], KTransformers [11], HybriMoE [63].** CPU/GPU split systems. The system contribution here (a llama.cpp cache with a per-host FETCH table) is an engineering delta of 1.03-1.29x over FreeToken, not a new architecture.
- **Classical paging and caching:**
  - Albers 1997 [2] and Breslauer [6]: lookahead measured in distinct pages.
  - Jain & Lin 2018 [25]: Belady-style prefetch costs more traffic than MIN.
  - Cao et al. 1995 [8]: integrated prefetching and caching.

  These anticipate the qualitative findings. The paper acknowledges them appropriately.
- **Missing:** SpecMD (ICML 2026, arXiv:2602.03921), a hardware-spanning benchmark of MoE expert-cache and prefetch policies, which proposes an MoE-specific eviction policy. It should be discussed alongside Table 12.

**Assessment:** each component has a close neighbour. What is new is the combination:
1. a controlled in-engine 2x2 factorial showing that admission set and read count interact;
2. a demonstration that which read path pays is a measurable property of the host;
3. a price on foresight in time, by horizon and accuracy, with a distinct-expert rule tested on held-out models;
4. a preregistered measurement discipline.

I rate the novelty moderate (3/5).

## 6. Soundness and the artifact checks

- the conclusion's "mostly" (W2);
- the bound's host-dependent slack (W3);
- "predicts" for a counter-conditioned model (W6);
- conclusions about the slow-link class from two hosts (W4).

Most failures are of magnitude rather than direction, as the paper notes. That supports the qualitative claims more than the quantitative bands.

## 7. Questions for the authors

1. Given Table 3 ("the rest" is 30-62% of the gap on fast-link hosts and up to 99% on slow-link hosts), what supports "the missing time is mostly the price of not knowing the future"? Would you restate it per host class?
2. At the host-bound budgets, Eq. (1)'s host term divides by the concurrent CPU + copy-engine probe (87.5 GB/s on host B), which no single path delivers. What is the bound if host reads must be split feasibly between the CPU (at B_c) and the link (at B_p) under the joint B_cp? How much of "the rest", especially on the two slow-link hosts, does that account for?
3. The host-dependence model fits G per host-cell on the deployed state and uses each state's measured counters. What is its error with simulator-derived counters and one G per host? Can it rank the states before any oracle run?
4. Is the 0.65C rule reproduced in the engine for Qwen3? The panel has only the recall-0.5 W = 8 point at Qwen3 12.5% on three hosts, and no exact W-sweep. Why was Qwen3 25% not run on the panel?
5. What is the bootstrap-over-models confidence interval on the held-out error difference between D(W) = cC and the power law (median 1.16 vs 1.19)?
