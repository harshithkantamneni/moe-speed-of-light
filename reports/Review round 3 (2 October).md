**Where the two agree (in order of cost):**

   the model's foresight state. At the two lowest budgets the "bytes-optimal" oracle reads more than the online policy

   admitted miss is read ONCE — fetched on demand into its slot (reads = R* by construction) or prefetched with enough

## Review 1 — MLSys PC member

(verbatim, as returned)

### 1. SUMMARY

The paper studies batch-1 decode of MoE models whose experts mostly sit in host DRAM behind one consumer GPU (rented RTX 5090/4090/3090 machines with desktop CPUs). It makes seven contributions: a host-memory law T = G + max(Xc/Bc, Xp/Bp, (Xc+Xp)/Bcp) with byte counts from engine counters and rates from an on-machine probe, predicted blind on seven RTX 5090 hosts (29 gpt-oss-120b configurations of the authors' engine, 3.7% median error) and compared leave-one-host-out against additive and per-layer forms; a "speed limit" for exact-routing systems with C slots per layer, built from Belady's MIN with bypass on recorded routing, the highest probed host rate and the GPU datasheet bandwidth, then tightened four ways; a Shapley attribution of the gap between measured speed and the limit over five modelled "fixes", concluding that missing routing foresight dominates where the host binds and serialised host reads where the GPU binds; two in-engine oracles fed a recorded routing trace (a MIN-with-bypass oracle: +13–23% at budgets of 25% and above, at most +6% below; a Belady-prefetch oracle: +25–63% at 25% and above, a loss below); a nine-model trace study that fits W50 ≈ 0.59 (C/k)^1.33 and argues that speculative batching does not supply foresight; an audit scoring 52 published measurements against their own bound; and the authors' llama.cpp expert cache as the "instrument" (3–29% faster than FreeToken on the headline host and −3 to +21% on a second host; 2.0–4.0× llama.cpp; a scorecard of 231 pre-registered clauses).

### 2. SCORES

Novelty 3/5 — the ingredients are published, as the paper's own "What we do not claim" lists; new: a bound in seconds with two read paths, Shapley attribution in this domain, the cross-model W50 fit, in-engine oracles. Technical soundness 3/5, a weak 3 — the measurements are sound and the bound is valid inside its stated class; several central inferences are not (W1, W3–W5). Experimental rigor 4/5. Clarity/writing 2/5. Significance 3/5. Reproducibility 5/5. OVERALL 5/10 — borderline; weak reject in this form.

This is an unusually rigorous measurement paper. Its central causal claim — foresight is the main fixable cost where the host binds — is contradicted by its own engine measurement at exactly those cells. The reason is one the main text does not name (W1). It is also written so densely that most of the PC will never reach its best parts. One experiment (W1) decides whether it becomes an 8–9 or stays a 6.

### 3. STRENGTHS

- S1. Protocol. The predictions really precede the data: job 094's header was committed at 19:10 on 1 Oct and its results at 22:09. Paired-by-problem bootstraps; separate selection and confirmation launches; a bound beside every number; every number regenerated from prereg/*.json. Re-derived and matching: law medians 3.69% per configuration, 3.42% per measurement, 3.30% (p90 7.46%) on desktop hosts; Table 1 fractions and Table 3 limits; Shapley shares (variant exact_max); trace claims (best online policy 1.35–2.10× the optimum, W=4 beats every online policy in all 27 cells, decayed frequency best in 12 cells and S3-FIFO in 10); audit class medians.
- S2. The right question: "how fast could this machine go, and what is the rest made of", in seconds rather than hit rates. Table 3's one-assumption-at-a-time tightening is the honest way to present a bound.
- S3. The nine-model trace study is clean and actionable (online policies cluster 35–110% above the optimum; static placement costs 1.4–13×; the hot set drifts 33–75% per 1,000 tokens; four tokens of lookahead beat every online policy; speculative batching, charged honestly, buys little).
- S4. In-engine oracles are rare in this literature, and the unfavourable results are printed.
- S5. Honesty throughout: failed clauses listed; the confounded i9 run rerun with pinned threads; the stock-clock host where FreeToken wins a cell reported; the ties on FreeToken's own model and Mixtral reported as scope.
- S6. Artifact: scripts, raw rows, traces, job scripts and a $45 cost ledger.

W1 — The central claim is not shown where the paper says it matters, and the main text misattributes why (largest cost; fixable). Host reads per token, misses plus admissions: gpt-oss 11%: R* 38.3, online 63.5, bytes-optimal oracle 66.1 (1.73×), simulated d=1 60.9 (1.59×); gpt-oss 25%: 15.3 / 34.0 / 28.9 (1.88×) / 29.2 (1.90×); Qwen3 12.5%: 96.5 / 160.0 / 168.6 (1.75×) / 157.1 (1.63×); Qwen3 25%: 43.4 / 89.8 / 84.9 (1.96×) / 79.6 (1.84×). The policy study already measures this but Table 4 leaves it out: the policy as shipped (dfa-deployed, reads = misses + copies) reads 1.59 / 1.98 / 2.32× the optimum at E/8, E/4 and 3E/8. The hit-optimal oracle is already at the DRAM roof: host traffic (misses + admissions) × expert size × tok/s is 44.7–47.4 GB/s on the 50.5 GB/s host and 46.8–51.9 on the 50.6 GB/s host; its fraction of the limit is R*/reads × utilisation (gpt-oss 25%: 15.3/31.1 × 44.7/50.5 = 43.6%, measured 43.7%; Qwen3 12.5%: 44.7%, measured 44.8%) — Fig. 2's ~44% plateau and the 093-P4 failures. Overlap is solved; what remains is about 2× excess reads. The experiment that tests the thesis has not been run: prefetch exactly the admissions MIN-with-bypass makes, before their first use; keep bypassed misses on the CPU; each admitted expert is then read once and overlapped. By the law, at the utilisation the hit-optimal oracle reaches, this should land at roughly 60–90% of the host-bound limit, highest at the lowest budgets. Fixable by 30 Oct: the oracle has both modes and lookahead files; the schedule can be computed offline with mosl.cachesim; one to two rentals, $5–10. Until it runs, the abstract's "the limit's own policy given future routing runs 13–23% faster" must say that the engine's implementation reads 1.7–2.0× the bytes of the limit's policy.

## Review 2 — Performance-modeling professor (prospective supervisor)

### 1. Summary

The paper studies batch-1 decode of MoE models whose experts mostly sit in host DRAM behind one consumer GPU. Six parts: the law (Eq. 1), a per-token model with one fitted G per engine and model and probed bandwidths, 3.7% median blind error on the author's cache on gpt-oss-120b across 7 RTX 5090 hosts; the speed limit (Eq. 2), a per-token ceiling from Belady's MIN with bypass per layer on a recorded trace, the GPU datasheet bandwidth and the highest probed host rate, against which llama.cpp, FreeToken, the cache and 52 published measurements are placed; the accounting, a Shapley attribution over five modelled fixes concluding foresight dominates where the host binds and overlap where the GPU binds; the trace study (nine models: online policies read 1.4–2× the optimum, static profiles fail because the hot set drifts, a few tokens of lookahead suffice with W50 ≈ 0.59 (C/k)^1.33, batched speculative verification does not substitute for foresight); the oracles (a hit-optimal prefetcher and MIN with bypass inside the engine on two weak-memory RTX 5090 hosts); the system (2.0–4.0× llama.cpp, −3% to +29% against FreeToken on two hosts, predictions committed before each job, a 243-clause scorecard). The measurements are careful and mostly credible. The two things the title names are not supported as stated: the speed limit is not a bound for two of the three systems the abstract scores against it, and on the headline machine not even for the GPU term; the accounting is a model whose one in-engine test contradicts it at the cells where it is most confident, and the abstract reports that test as confirmation.

### 2. Scores

Novelty 3/5. Technical soundness 2/5 (the measurements alone would earn 4; the inferences drawn from them earn 2). Experimental rigor 3/5 (protocol 4; the design of the decisive experiment 2). Clarity/writing 2/5. Significance 3/5. Reproducibility 4/5. OVERALL 5/10; weak reject / major revision. A faithful rewrite (W1–W4 reframed, Section 7 corrected) would be a 7; adding the factorial in W1 and host-level replication would make it an 8.

### 4. Weaknesses, ranked by cost to the grade

W1. The accounting is a model; the only intervention test contradicts it where it is most confident, and the abstract calls that test its check. Fixable by 30 Oct: yes (an engine change, two rentals, a rewrite). In scripts/shapley_gap.py v(S) for all 32 coalitions is analytic; only v(none) touches a measurement, forced to equal the measured time by a per-cell factor η "calibrated so that the measured time is reproduced with no fix applied". η is not a GPU property: 1.25–1.68 on listing B and 1.005–1.44 on the 7950X host; at gpt-oss 25% it is 1.49 on listing B and 1.005 on the 7950X — same GPU model, same kernels; the faster-memory card comes out as the "less efficient" one; η is tracking host-model error. "Shapley attribution … which no ordering of fixes can bias" is true and beside the point: Shapley removes dependence on the order of fixes, not on the model. The in-engine test (7950X host): foresight alone, model v(none)/v(F): +46, +62, +56, +48, +64, +59%; MIN with bypass measured (W = all): +3.3, +18.1, +23.2, +0.2, +13.1, +18.9%; share realised 10, 40, 52, 0.5, 30, 43%. At Qwen3 12.5% the paper says "Foresight is largest in every one of the 120 orders"; there, perfect future routing bought +0.2% [−0.3, +0.6] with the bytes-optimal policy and −8% / −5% with the hit-optimal one. The Conclusion contains both claims three sentences apart. The test did not implement the counterfactual the model prices: the model's foresight state reads R* experts once each; the engine's "bytes-optimal" oracle serves a miss where it is, then admits it with a background copy, "each read a second time by the copy"; its host reads (misses + admissions) are 1.73, 1.88, 1.93, 1.75, 1.96 and 2.04× the optimum's; at the two lowest budgets it reads more than the online policy (66.1 vs 63.5; 168.6 vs 160.0). The main text compares misses only and omits this third, structural reason; "the limit's own policy" (abstract) is therefore wrong in substance. "Mostly on the bytes it saves": the law on the oracle's own counters attributes 11.7 of 18.1, 20.8 of 23.2, 4.2 of 13.1 and 17.9 of 18.9 points to bytes — at Qwen3 25%, a host-bound cell, a third. Foresight and overlap are complements in the engine but nearly additive in the model (gpt-oss 25% on the 7950X: v(none)−v(O) = 1.88 ms, v(none)−v(F) = 4.86, v(none)−v(O,F) = 6.86; interaction 0.12 ms), while in the engine the within-layer overlap switch moves speed by under 1% everywhere, job 071 found next-layer lookahead cannot buy the overlap, and the hit-optimal oracle's 25–63% gain "arrived only with foresight"; the state "overlap without foresight" enters the Shapley average and is not realisable by an online policy; the two regimes are an artefact of pricing complements as independent players. Pre-registered predictions derived from the model failed (093 P1a/c, P2a/c, P7a/b; 094 P1, P4, P5 6 of 6): a pre-registered refutation of the model's predictions, rewritten in the abstract as findings. What the oracles establish: at budgets of 25% and above on weak-memory hosts, knowing future routing is worth +13–23% when used only for admit/bypass decisions and +25–63% when used to prefetch; within-layer overlap is worth essentially nothing; the engine's W50 at gpt-oss 25% (9.2) is roughly consistent with the trace's 10.5; in this engine the copy path (~3 steps of latency, a 64-copy cap) and the serve-then-admit double read are what bind. What they refute: the accounting's regime claim at 11% and 12.5%; its magnitudes; the separability of the foresight and overlap players; the law's universality (24–53% over-prediction for overlapped execution). What neither establishes: the size of the foresight term in the limit's own sense, because neither oracle reads R*. Fix: (1) implement the counterfactual priced — MIN with bypass in which an admitted miss is fetched on demand into its slot (one read) and a bypassed miss runs on the CPU; reads then equal R* by construction and no lookahead copy is needed, so copy latency and the queue cap drop out; (2) a 2×2 factorial at all six cells on listing B and the stock-clock host, factors {online, MIN-with-bypass with admit-by-fetch} × {no cross-step overlap, lookahead prefetch}, main effects and interaction with intervals beside the model's v(·); (3) present the Shapley decomposition as a prediction and the factorial as the measurement.

W2. The "speed limit" is not a bound for the systems the abstract scores against it, and on the headline machine not even for the GPU term. Fixable: yes, mostly by recomputation. Class: llama.cpp --n-cpu-moe pins whole layers; FreeToken's cache is "one LRU over all layers" (the author's own race_entrants.md: "should be scored against the pooled bound"); against the pooled bound on listing B FreeToken is at 18–39% and llama.cpp at 8–19%. The audit makes the same mistake (the leloch fork row, pooled LRU; Pipelined Sharding scored at per-layer C = 44 and 107 while job 077 saw its planner pin whole layers); speculative decoders (SP-MoE, MoE-SpeQ) are not one-token-per-pass and the batch-K study shows Belady's reads per committed token falling a median 15% at E/8, K = 8, α = 0.9 with free drafts, so the per-token R* is not a bound for them. Ceilings: B_host is a probe sample (a second rental of the same CPU probed 90.3 GB/s against listing B's 87.5); B_gpu is 1792 GB/s but the probe on listing B logged "device read 1 GiB: 1847.1 GB/s" (results 080–082, bw.txt), above the bound, and the card reports 17001 MHz memory (1.21×); with 1847 GB/s the GPU-bound limits rise ~3%, at the clock-scaled rate ~20%. Workload: R* is computed on the trace's greedy text but each system decodes different text (job 073 P4: 0–3% identical outputs); runs start warm, the optimum cold (up to 1.4%); simulated hit rates differ from the engine's counters by up to 5.3 points on Qwen3. At 25% and above the limit is mostly the datasheet GPU term (519 tok/s at gpt-oss 40% against 255 all-in-VRAM); "It can exceed the all-in-VRAM speed, because the CPU's bandwidth adds to the GPU's" misattributes this — with the measured GPU rate the limit is 279 tok/s and the CPU adds about 9%. Fix: state the class (exact routing, unmodified expert bytes, whole-expert residency, C slots per layer, one token per forward pass); score llama.cpp and pooled systems against the pooled bound; B_gpu = max(datasheet × memory-clock ratio, probed device read); call B_host a probe ceiling or use DIMM-rated bandwidth; compute R* on each system's own decoded text with the same warm-up; lead with the measured-GPU ("all") limit and call Eq. (2) "a ceiling under stated assumptions".

W3. The law is validated far more narrowly than it is stated, and the paper's own data refute its general sentences. Fixable: yes. All 29 blind configurations are the cache on gpt-oss-120b on RTX 5090s from jobs 069c–077; 19 copy nothing; the max-vs-additive contest rests on three fast-link FETCH rows; the leave-one-host-out medians (3.1, 3.2, 4.5%) come with no intervals. "On every later machine the job wrote the law's prediction from its own probe before any model ran" is false for the stock-clock host, the 4090 and 3090, the half-link hosts and both oracle hosts (law_prediction.json exists only for 069c–077). G is not a GPU term (5.17 ms refit exceeds the 3.92 ms all-in-VRAM token; a constant absorbing a byte-proportional shortfall leaves the slope wrong by up to 31%, consistent with Eq.
